## 1. Bibliographic facts

- **English title:** *Modern Villainess: It's Not Easy Building a Corporate Empire Before the Crash* — Seven Seas Entertainment (Vol. 1 English edition 2022).
- **Japanese title:** 現代社会で乙女ゲームの悪役令嬢をするのはちょっと大変 (*Gendai Shakai de Otome Game no Akuyaku Reijō wo Suru no wa Chotto Taihen*) — Overlap Novels (オーバーラップノベルス). Tagline: 現代悪役令嬢日本再生譚 ("a modern villainess's tale of Japan's revival").
- **Author:** Tofuro Futsukaichi (二日市とふろう). Self-describes as "an author from Narou."
- **Illustrators:** KEI (景) for Vols. 1–4; **Jaian** from Vol. 5 (per the author's own Vol. 5 afterword). Japanese storefront listings still show 景 in their credit lines — discrepancy noted, afterword treated as authoritative.
- **Origin:** web novel on Shōsetsuka ni Narō (「小説家になろう」発); also serialized on Kakuyomu, where it is still ongoing (≈720 episodes by mid-2026).
- **Recognition (per the web listing's own blurb):** *Kono Light Novel ga Sugoi!* 2022, tankōbon/novels category, 8th; Next Light Novel Award 2021, overall 7th.
- **Japanese edition:** at least 8 volumes; the Japanese Vol. 8 blurb places the story in 2004.
- **Protagonist's name in Japanese:** 桂華院瑠奈 (Keikain Runa). Note 桂 = *katsura* / *kei*: Vol. 5 states that families attached to the Keikain took the derivative surname "Katsura" or put "Kei" in their names — hence Katsura Naomi/Naoyuki and the "Keika" (桂華) group name.
- **Vols. 6–7 (English):** Seven Seas; illustrator Jaian. Vol. 7: translated by Emma Schumacker, adapted by Jack Hamm and Étienne McKian; first printing September 2026 (Japanese edition OVERLAP, 2025).
- **Image-only pages.** The EPUBs of Vols. 6–7 contain pages that exist only as images and so are missing from any extracted text: three colour plates (some with dialogue), the illustrated cast list, a **Keikain family tree**, a **Keika Group chart** and a two-page **"Next Time" preview** of the next volume. They carry real information (Aki's parentage; the group's structure; the Vol. 8 preview) and must be read as images. The image pages of Vols. 1–5 were not checked in the earlier reads.

## 3. Premise and frame

- A modern Japanese office worker dies an unremarkable death after their employer collapses and is reborn as **Keikain Runa**, the villainess of the otome game *Love Where the Cherry Blossom Falls*, set in modern Japan. She keeps full memory of the game and of real-world history.
- In Vol. 1 Ch. 2 she tests and rules out being literally *inside* the game (no UI, real weather, unscripted dialogue). The world closely resembles the game's setting but has its own alternate history.
- **Frame:** Vol. 1 Ch. 1 is a flash-forward to **15 September 2008** — the "Lehthan Sisters" (Lehman Brothers) bankruptcy — at a Teia Tower party in Daiba, where Runa (18) is confronted by fiancé Teia Eiichi, heroine Takanashi Mizuho, Gotou Mitsuya and Izumikawa Yuujirou, and ends the engagement herself. Everything else rewinds and moves forward toward that night. "Before the Crash" in the title is a literal countdown. *(Reread: the narrator of that scene is this world's Runa — her maid Tachibana Yuka, who does not exist in the game, waits outside — so the frame shows where this story ends, not how the game did.)*
- **The game's real plot** (assembled across Vols. 2–5): a scholarship-student movement to reform the academy's privileges, which Runa opposes and loses by one vote in 2008; behind it, a declining ducal house that spends its daughter on a marriage; behind that, a scheme to make Karafuto a tax-and-law haven under noble immunity, with Runa as its face. See §9 item 11.

## 4. The world: alternate history

**Textual facts:**
- **WWII:** the Allies lost the Normandy landings; Germany fought on. Japan's surrender was **negotiated, not unconditional** (glossary: "In the real world, Japan's surrender was unconditional"). Vol. 3 adds that Japan **surrendered to the Allies and then declared war on the Axis**, preserving many national interests. *(Reconciling these is my reading: Normandy's failure prolonged the European war; Japan switched sides into it.)*
- **No demonstration — necessary context.** **No nuclear weapon has ever been used on a city in this world.** There was no Hiroshima or Nagasaki: Runa calls 1945's nuclear "example" "the history that never happened in this world" (Vol. 4 Ch. 11). The Manchurian War's nuclear standoff rested on a Soviet test (Vol. 3 Ch. 3). Okazaki's claim that the Soviets "nuked both friend and foe" there (Vol. 5 Ch. 5) conflicts with that, and even taken literally it was never a city-scale example. What this world lacks is not nuclear use but a demonstration. Without one there is no shared image of the bomb and no "nuclear taboo." That is why planners speak of nuclear strikes "to set an example" and an "exchange rate" (Vol. 4 Ch. 11), why Afghanistan's chemical and biological annihilation becomes "a tool of fear" (Vol. 5 Ch. 5), why use is debated openly in Washington (Vol. 3 Ch. 9; Vol. 5 Ch. 6), and why this world's 9/11 is a nuclear plot (Vol. 3 Ch. 6). *(Added 9 October 2026 from the reader's observation; analysed in the Vol. 5 map, Part II §3a.)*
- **Consequences:** the **peerage** survives (plus "lifelong peerages," compared to UK life peers); the **zaibatsu were never dissolved**; the **Privy Council** survives (its power suspended, never repealed); nobles hold **"impunity to arrest"** in criminal matters and a form of **diplomatic immunity** created when the House of Peers became the House of Councilors — the glossary credits **Runa's grandfather** with inventing it. Nobles keep family laws and can obstruct police through proxies.
- **Japan's wars:** a "Manchurian War" (the narration's Korean War analogue), Vietnam, the Gulf War; Japan has both the SDF and offensive forces.
- **The Karafuto Crisis (Vol. 3 Ch. 3 — the definitive account):** Karafuto (Sakhalin) was split after the Russo-Japanese War. When Japan entered the Manchurian War, the USSR invaded Japanese southern Karafuto and set up the **People's Democratic Republic of Northern Japan** (Soviet-aligned, with a Stasi/KGB-style Ministry of National Security). The West won the Manchurian War; the US defended Manchuria but acknowledged Northern Japan, to avoid nuclear war and to penalize Japan for its WWII surrender. **The partition was of Karafuto, not the home islands.**
- **Reunification:** Northern Japan collapsed after the Berlin Wall fell, via the **"Winter Festival Revolution"** (a secret-police coup during Maslenitsa, endorsed by the army; abroad called the "Maslenitsa Revolution"), letting Japan occupy Karafuto. Hokkaido is ~80% Japanese / 10% Russian / 10% Chinese; Karafuto's later census (Vol. 2) shows ~50% Japanese / 30% Slavic / 20% Chinese, a population of perhaps 20–25 million, and former Northern citizens treated as second-class.
- **Manchuria (Vol. 1 Ch. 7):** Kuomintang forces, backed by the US and Japan, hold Manchuria; the Communists are driven out — the reverse of the real 1948 Liaoshen Campaign. The **Daqing oilfield** analogue is found about a decade early by an American-backed Japanese speculator.
- **The second February 26 Incident (~1967; Vols. 2–3):** literary figures called for a "Shōwa Restoration" and the overthrow of nobility and zaibatsu; a battalion occupied central Tokyo and killed members of those classes; the Imperial Police were disbanded afterward. The nobility retains a siege mentality, now lobbying for arms and hiring PMCs.
- **Later details:** the second February 26 Incident was a coup by the imperial police and parts of the Self-Defense Forces, aimed in part at the Keikains; Hikomaro survived it (Vol. 5 Ch. 8), and its veterans resurface in the Shinjuku plot (Vol. 5 Ch. 6). How the Manchurian War ended is told two ways: a Soviet nuclear test (Vol. 3 Ch. 3) or, by Okazaki's account, Soviet battlefield use against "friend and foe" (Vol. 5 Ch. 5). Japan's population is about 140 million, some 20–25 million of them in Karafuto; this Japan is "more of a 'normal country'," with forces that operate abroad (Vol. 2 Ch. 8). Manchuria survives as a state, with its first change of government in 2000 (Vol. 3).
- **The game's history** ran the same way at the top: Koizumi broke the nobility there too, and the zaibatsu fell in the 2008 crisis (Vol. 4 Ch. 9).
- **Otherwise:** Japan's bubble, its collapse and the Lost Decade follow the real schedule and mechanisms — except where Runa's rescues change them (a Nikkei near 20,000 in 1999–2000; smaller bad loans; healthier banks).

**Added from Vols. 6–7 (textual facts):**
- **Japan sent troops to the Vietnam War** (Vol. 7 Ch. 1).
- **In-world chronology** (Kanbe's board, Vol. 7 Ch. 7): 1993 an opposition coalition government; **1994 the fall of Northern Japan and unification**; 1995 the Hanshin earthquake and a new-religion terror attack; 1996 a Fellowship prime minister again. Unification was paid for by a "national welfare tax" — a Karafuto restoration tax — that brought down a noble prime minister (Hosokawa's analogue; Vol. 6 Ch. 2).
- **Japan was defeated by the United States in the Pacific War but never occupied:** "the GHQ helped us finish things up after the Pacific War, but they'd never ruled over us in this world" (Vol. 7 Ch. 7). There was no direct GHQ order against State Shinto, though a church was built on a State Shinto shrine's site (Vol. 7 Ch. 6). The war's end came after a threatened assassination of the prime minister brought a change of government; the Guards stayed loyal (Vol. 7 Ch. 2).
- **Northern Karafuto** was Soviet territory managed by Northern Japan, taken at reunification, and is now claimed by both Japan and Russia and governed by neither — a crossing for mafias and terrorists (Vol. 7 Ch. 3).
- **Former Northern officials received peerages at reunification** (Vol. 7 Ch. 1). Karafuto counts: "twenty million residents of Karafuto descent in Karafuto, and a few million more" on the mainland (Vol. 7 Ch. 1); "twenty-four million citizens of the former Northern Japanese regime" (Vol. 7 Ch. 6).
- **The 2003 general election** was held in late July and gave the ruling coalition a supermajority (Vol. 6 Ch. 8; Vol. 7 Ch. 1).
- **Iraq after the war** (Vol. 7 Chs. 7, 12): Turkey occupies the Kurdish north, Iran the Shia south; a Sunni insurgency in the centre; the JSDF and British forces fight Iran's army; Saddam is still at large; partition is debated; Washington "seriously considered the use of nuclear weapons in Iraq." During the war a choking incident briefly handed nuclear authority to a hard-line vice president (Vol. 6 Ch. 5).
- **Private violence is legal and growing:** a Detective Industry Act, an amended Security Industry Act and a bounty system for criminal investigations (Vol. 7 Ch. 7).
- **The supernatural is real in-world.** Hotaru was made a would-be guardian god by a ritual "performed with" Hikomaro (Vol. 7 Ch. 1); the academy was built as a feng-shui machine that turned dead children into *zashiki warashi* for the nation's prosperity (Vol. 7 Ch. 6); a god (Dakiniten) intervenes in person (Vol. 7 Ch. 10). The author says Hotaru was invented to match "Prime Minister Koizumi and his incredible luck" (Vol. 7 afterword) — so the occult plot is an allegory of the era's luck.

## 5. Cast

### The Keikain family
- **Keikain Runa** — protagonist (born ~1990). Three-quarters Russian (Romanov via her mother); blonde; kept out of sight as a child, ostensibly over her hair. Real owner of the **Moonlight Fund**. Nicknamed **"Little Queen"** (from a Teisei ad campaign; politicians use it, and Izumikawa calls her "Your Little Majesty" and "small kingmaker"). Gifted singer (Micaëla's aria, the Queen of the Night aria, Beethoven's Ninth). Quietly holds bookkeeping, secretarial and English qualifications and aims for early college graduation — preparing to survive the Keika Group's collapse. Called "the Goddess of Victory" (her own joke, Vol. 2), "a modern-day Sarah Bernhardt" (Vol. 2), "Boss" (her military company), "the Moon Mistress" (Washington, Vol. 4) and "Cassandra" (two heads of government, Vol. 4). Film star and Academy Award winner (Vol. 5). Posts on railway forums as **lunakeikain**. Prefers grape juice.
- **Keikain Hikomaro** (grandfather, dead) — illegitimate by birth, adopted into a dormant ducal line; rose through the Home Ministry to **Special Higher Police** superintendent; failed to solve a prime minister's assassination, resigned, and was then gifted the dukedom (the narration flags this as suspicious). A **fixer** with blackmail files, influence over the police and shady companies; fathered children across about seven branch families; invented noble diplomatic immunity.
- **Keikain Otsumaro** (father, dead) — Hikomaro's illegitimate son by a Russian noblewoman granted asylum; founded the **Far Eastern Group** with Russian émigré backing; the group was a front for Eastern technology theft; caught in a **CoCom** violation (machine tools to Northern Japan during the Sakata combine construction); died by apparent suicide. **Vol. 2 Ch. 7: Tachibana pushed him to suicide** (the English sentence is ambiguous — see §14). Vol. 5 Ch. 9: Runa concludes he was an instrument of the North's informant network.
- **Natasha Alexandrovna Romanova** (mother, dead) — great-granddaughter of Tsar Alexander III; deployed as a honeytrap by Northern Japan's Ministry of National Security (partly to strike at Hikomaro, partly to keep the Romanov line as leverage against the USSR); died shortly after Runa's birth. Graves in Sakata, Yamagata.
- **Keikain Kiyomaro** (spelled "Kyomaro" on the Vol. 1 character page) — current Duke; Runa's uncle; head of the Keika Group. Announced as her guardian (Vol. 2 Ch. 2); formally adopts her in June 2000 (Vol. 2 Ch. 5) — the legal lever used in Vol. 3 Ch. 12, which he declines to pull. Invokes noble immunity to save Tachibana (Vol. 4 Ch. 9). Revealed in Vol. 3 Ch. 12 as sincere: "I don't want to be the kind of terrible father who steals his child's belongings." Sold the original Keika companies to the Iwazaki zaibatsu because he judged the house lacked the managerial depth to run them.
- **Keikain Nakamaro** — Kiyomaro's son and heir. Promised Runa's dying mother to protect her. Testifies to the House of Councilors as an **unsworn witness** in Runa's place (Vol. 2). Booked to be in New York on 11 September 2001 (Vol. 2 cliffhanger) — **survives** (Vol. 3). Engaged to **Asagiri Sakurako**, granddaughter of Iwazaki Yashirou (she is pregnant in Vol. 5 — a child who did not exist in the game, where Nakamaro died). Becomes a Keika Holdings director. Delivers the first rebuke — "you have used too much influence across two prime ministers… reign it in" — and "You are my precious sister" (Vol. 2 Ch. 5); joins the staged defeat (Vol. 4 Ch. 2); "we'll end up clashing someday" (Vol. 5 Ch. 4).
- **Branch families:** the **Kitayama clan** (same original dukedom as Hikomaro; tried to strip Runa's rights in Vol. 5 Ch. 1); the **Katsura** line (Naomi, Naoyuki).

### Runa's inner circle
- **Tachibana Ryuuji** — butler and guardian-of-record (Vol. 1). Born in Karafuto during the Manchurian War; ex-underworld; Hikomaro's bodyguard and informant, then butler. Detected Runa's precocity when she was two or three because the books she read were always reshelved correctly. **Drove her father to suicide** and never fulfilled Hikomaro's dying request to raise Runa as his own. Builds a private intelligence/security apparatus with Toudou (the "Toyohara girls" — orphans trained as spies by Northern Japan — plus ex-Northern soldiers), at first hidden from Runa and Ichijou; it becomes the attendants of Vol. 2 Ch. 6 and the orphan "associates" of Vol. 5 Ch. 7. Managing director, then outside director, of **Keika Railway**; succeeded by the railwayman Mikihara Kazuaki (Vol. 5 Ch. 1). Hires Angela Sullivan away from the CIA. One of the four who stage her Furukawa defeat (Vol. 4 Ch. 2); target of the dawn raid (Vol. 4 Ch. 9); hides the nationalists' approach from her "so that her choices stay her own" (Vol. 5 Ch. 1). Defects on Iraq (Vol. 4: "please withdraw from Iraq"). Stepping down before noble immunity is abolished. Known in the Hong Kong underworld as the "**Hungry Wolf from the Far East**": during the Vietnam War he managed and restricted the drug trade around the US military, then handed the network to the Hong Kong underworld (Vol. 3 Ch. 5).
- **Tachibana Yuka** — his granddaughter; the maid in the 2008 flash-forward; trained as Runa's protector.
- **Ichijou Susumu** — Far Eastern Bank's Tokyo branch manager whom Runa blackmails and recruits at age ~5; co-runs the Moonlight Fund; youngest megabank board member; CEO of **Keika Holdings**; deliberately kept "pure" by the other adults; put on Koizumi's **Council on Economic and Fiscal Policy** (Vol. 2 Ch. 8); plans to step back to a non-representative chairmanship and builds a loyalty faction with Okazaki (Vol. 4). "I'm the second guy she scouted." Still cleans the executive bathroom himself (Vol. 2 Ch. 2; cited by Runa in Vol. 5 Ch. 7); walks out on the orphan plan as "human trafficking" (Vol. 2 Ch. 3); teaches the boys a loan review (Vol. 2 Ch. 6); produces the oil chart that explains Koizumi's hostility (Vol. 5 Ch. 4). Daughter **Ichijou Erika** becomes Runa's secretary-maid (Vol. 3; "Erika's Garden").
- **Toudou Nagayoshi** — resources advisor; Manchuria backstory (Vol. 1 Ch. 7: the unnamed speculator, the Lucky Strikes box, marriage to the speculator's daughter); managing director of **Akamatsu Corporation**; co-designer of the Toyohara plan; proposed the Gulf oil field and negotiated the Saudi railway (Vol. 2 Ch. 5); proposed buying the Sapporo military company (Vol. 2 Ch. 6).
- **Okazaki Yuuichi** — Akamatsu resources man and self-described lover of risk; won Runa's recruitment test by buying information; found the Russian coup tip in a Macau casino (staged Russian gambling "losses" revealed capital flight) and traced the put options behind an assassination attempt on Runa (Vol. 3 Ch. 5); deduces Runa's foreknowledge (Vol. 3 Ch. 6) and asks only for "a front row seat to see how you change history"; dares her into the WorldCom operation and runs it, and the Gulf/Iraq logistics (Vol. 4); diagnoses her breakdown (Vol. 5 Ch. 5); turns an American honeytrap against him into leverage (Vol. 5 Ch. 7).
- **Katsura Naoyuki** — Naomi's son; worked in Hokkaido Kaitaku Bank's integrated-development department; begs Runa to save it; moves to Keika Bank private banking; runs the small-business rescue; holds Runa's secret **Keika Trust Bank** account in Sapporo.
- **Maids:** **Saitou Keiko** (head maid; ex-Ginza hostess; Hikomaro's mistress and Aki's mother — confirmed by the family tree in Vols. 6–7), **Tokitou Aki** (Hikomaro's "Secret Illegitimate Child"; to found a Tokitou branch family through an Iwazaki-linked marriage, Vol. 7 Ch. 5), **Katsura Naomi** (Hikomaro's younger brother's illegitimate daughter), **Kitagumo Ryouko** (Northern-Japanese kendo; ex-Eastern intelligence), ~70 maids by Vol. 3, many armed via **Kitakaba Security** (PMC).
- **Angela Sullivan** — CIA analyst (Vol. 1 Ch. 9; author of the Vol. 2 "Sullivan Report"); bought by Tachibana as Runa's secretary (Vol. 2 Ch. 7); frontrunner to lead the Keika Group, director at Keika Securities, CEO candidate for Keika Holdings (Vols. 4–5); wants Okazaki sidelined. Resigned from the CIA on paper in 2001 (Vol. 2 Ch. 8) but still works for it and reports to its leadership-analysis desk (Vol. 5 Chs. 3–4). Joined the CIA to find a husband who vanished in Japan around 1989 (Vol. 3 Ch. 4). "You could become a monster" (Vol. 3); "Eva would kill you before you even crossed the international date line" (Vol. 4); proposes the subprime play and expects Runa to short it (Vol. 5). Loyalty real; ethics not Runa's.
- **Other intelligence staff:** **Eva Charon** (CIA), **Anisha Egorova** (ex-Eastern intelligence "observer"), **Nakajima Atsushi** (security chief; ex-Northern Japan captain), **Yulia Molotova**, **Irina Berosova**, **Glasya Marsheva** (Vol. 5 "temporary workers… with strings attached by the superpowers").

## 8. Volume summaries

*All seven volumes have now been read end to end (Vols. 1 and 2 twice). Chapter-level detail, quotations and confidence labels are in the event maps (§16).*

### Volume 1 — infancy to autumn 1998 (9 chapters)
**Chapter titles:** 1 September 15th, 2008 · 2 The Lady's Flight · 3 A Lady's Everyday Life · 4 Why My Hair Is Blonde · 5 The Atonement of Keikain Nakamaro · 6 Why My Hair Is Blonde: A Number of Newspaper Extracts · 7 Toudou Nagayoshi's Lucky Strikes · 8 Little Queen · 9 Vultures and the 1998 House of Councilors Election.

- **Ch. 1 (the frame):** 15 September 2008. The narrator is **this world's** Runa, not the game's — her maid Tachibana Yuka, who does not exist in the game, waits outside. The frame is a promise about where the series ends, not a replay of the game.
- **Ch. 2 (the financial core):** finds the household quietly shrinking (¥3m/month out against falling income; Aki's scholarship is the tell). At ~5, Runa demands Far Eastern Bank's real books "and the ones for your secret accounts too," mortgages the estate for ¥500m (against a ~¥1bn appraisal), and through the offshore Moonlight Fund turns it into ~¥50bn: a US browser IPO (~$100m), an IT basket (~$100m), and FX bought near ¥80/$ and unwound toward ¥100/$, leveraged against Far Eastern Life Insurance's assets at a "Japan premium." She closes positions when the yen passes ¥120. She restructures the Far Eastern Group (Hotels carved out for ¥30bn; Developments shrunk to ¥15bn and put into rehabilitation; a 33% stake for ¥20bn), then climbs a ladder of rescues under the **Keika Rules** — Sankai (¥80bn) → Ichiyama merged into Sankai (reverse merger; ¥260bn *tobashi*) → a takeover bid for Hokkaido Kaitaku (¥2.3tn bad debt; waits for ¥59, bids ¥74) through a "California-based" fund front. Then LPCB and Nihon Credit, ~¥8tn in BOJ special loans (Vol. 4 Ch. 1: a confidence device, barely drawn), the name **Keika Bank**, and a crucial **memorandum**: she declines management rights in exchange for the right to bid when the bank is privatized. Ichijou's pudding-and-banknote lesson teaches book versus market value.
- **Ch. 3:** kindergarten, Asuka and Hotaru, Mio; her debut at the group's fiftieth-anniversary party (she names her father's scandal to Teia Shuuichi, glancing at the branch families); meets Eiichi, Yuujirou (at an adult qualification exam), Mitsuya; the "Gakushuukan Quartet"; Katou's emotion; a **video message to her future self** (played back in Vol. 4 Ch. 11).
- **Ch. 4:** Toudou; Katou's complicity in her father's cover-up; the Matsuno Trading bailout; the **kidnapping** at the Keika Holdings launch party — by Runa's deduction it began as a *fake* staged by the Public Safety Bureau with her family's coerced consent, and turned real; foiled by the boys and Maefuji ("You can leave the rest up to the adults"). The Moonlight Fund is a Swiss account in her name behind same-named shells. The MOF corruption scandal and Izumikawa's resignation; Maefuji revealed as the game's arresting officer; Nakamaro's staged blow; the closing address to "Keikain Runa."
- **Ch. 5:** Nakamaro and Kiyomaro's POV; the promise to Natasha; Kiyomaro's late wife Ruriko (née Iwazaki); the engagement calculated.
- **Ch. 6:** in-world press extracts, 1994–98 (Karafuto's troubles, Super-J, the combine, Hong Kong refugees).
- **Ch. 7:** Toudou's Manchuria flashback (the Daqing analogue; the Lucky Strikes box).
- **Ch. 8:** Teisei (DK Bank paralyzed); "Little Queen"; literature (Kajii, Dazai, Akutagawa, Niimi); the **concert** (*Carmen* and *L'Arlésienne* suites; Runa sings Micaëla's aria to Eiichi); Izumikawa's decline ("You only know how much somebody is truly worth once they start their decline"); the dollhouse; Hotaru's sacrifice backstory; Lydia; the library investigation, which does **not** resolve mundanely.
- **Ch. 9:** Akamatsu forms; AIRHO; oil-stockpiling arbitrage; **Taichirou's rescue** (two-seat district → PR list, ~¥10bn); Angela's CIA visit (*bubuzuke*); the LDP defeat; Fuchigami becomes PM; Izumikawa made VP through Runa's scribbled note; Koizumi loses and turns against her; the Russian oligarch behind the kidnapping; Runa foresees the Russian crisis, **warns no one**, and wins the **Keika Bank auction for ¥800bn as sole bidder (20 Sept 1998)**; the Moscow Declaration loan. The glossary ties LTCM to Lehman.
- **Afterword:** the web serial trended on the real Hokkaido Takushoku connection.

### §10a. Volume 4, Chapter 9 — four passages explained *(reader's questions, added 12 October 2026)*

*Based on a reread of Vol. 4 Chs. 8–9 and 12. Real-world parallels marked "general knowledge" were not re-checked.*

**1. "Neoliberalism. This country has an image of personal responsibility, but its true essence is in the way it gives handicaps… 'I'll give you a chance, but it comes with a handicap.'"** (Kanbe's seminar)
- **Set-up:** happiness defined as the TV-anime family's house with a garden ("a distant dream in my past existence"); not everyone can have it. The cities are musical chairs: those already seated win by not moving. "Handicap" is the **sports sense** — a starting disadvantage assigned by position, not a disability.
- **The two halves:** the *public face* is personal responsibility (you had a chance, so failure is yours); the *mechanism* is unequal starting points left uncorrected. Socialism promised **equality of results** ("the Eastern Bloc countries failed the pilot study"); neoliberalism promises only **equality of opportunity**.
- **Economist's gloss:** formal equality of opportunity (nobody barred) versus substantive equality of opportunity (compensating for circumstances not chosen — family, region, birth year). Kanbe says Japan's neoliberalism delivers the first and calls the gap "personal responsibility." In golf the handicap helps the weaker player; here the weaker player carries it.
- **Why it wounds Runa:** the musical chairs is her first life's labour market — firms protected incumbents and cut graduate hiring, so the "employment ice age" cohort (c. 1993–2005, general knowledge) had a chance but started with the handicap of their birth year.
- **The turn:** "But isn't that unfair?" — "Sure. It's based on the premise that people aren't equal." We protect the weak "because we want to believe we're special"; "privileged class"; *noblesse oblige*; "You only have two hands." Her rescues are selective and partly self-regard.
- **Echo:** Vol. 7 Ch. 7's "We'll offer opportunities but never lower the bar" — "the concept that had cut down everyone who was weak, including myself."

**2. "Taking out the minister will cause a backlash that reaches the prime minister too. I'll hand over the achievements and step back myself… The prime minister is a wounded beast right now."**
- **Situation (June 2002):** Minister Takenaga (Takenaka) unveils a seven-point financial programme — the real October 2002 Takenaka plan moved earlier (general knowledge) — and names Keika Holdings on TV: list it "as a symbol of zaibatsu dissolution." Anti-Koizumi factions want Keika's help; Izumikawa offers the weapon: cut the Diet's extension so the bills die. (The English "you'll just get sabotaged" is muddled; the next sentence shows the sabotage is the tactic on offer.)
- **Unpacked:** "taking out the minister" = killing his bills and bringing him down; the "backlash" = Takenaga is Koizumi's face of reform, so beating him means open war with the most popular politician in the country, on the side of zaibatsu and bad banks, on the eve of the Iraq war she is preparing; "hand over the achievements" = let the government claim Keika's finished clean-up and listing as its trophies; "step back myself" = the exit she had already planned (Ch. 6), done so Koizumi wins.
- **Izumikawa:** "you always achieve more than you give away." Her maxim — "if you wanted to gain something, you had to be the one to grant something first" — is *my identification* as Laozi, *Tao Te Ching* 36 ("What you would take, you must first give"); the author quotes Laozi elsewhere in Vol. 4.
- **"Wounded beast":** Koizumi's approval has fallen (the Foreign Ministry scandal; the fight with the secretary-general) and he has purged "both enemies and allies," including his friend Katou over tax evasion. Real: ratings fell sharply after he sacked Tanaka Makiko in January 2002 (general knowledge); Katō Kōichi resigned his seat in April 2002 over his secretary's tax evasion. A wounded animal has nothing to lose — hence "Friend or foe, he's not going to go easy on anyone who stands against him," her reason not to stand against him.
- **Irony:** the next scene shows the concession was not enough — "the wounded beast kept his eyes on me." Eiichi later turns the phrase on her: "you'd be a wounded beast at that point, right, Runa? There's no way your risk management would be any good."

**3. The Bahrain project and the Cabinet Intelligence and Research Office** (Okazaki: "It's about the work in Bahrain. The Cabinet Information Research Office has been sniffing around. I believe they're onto you." — the office is *Naichō*)
- **What Bahrain is:** $10bn of WCI profits in a logistics complex next to a US base (storage, runways, housing, power, desalination, PMC guards, supporting "tens of thousands"), plus Akamatsu's Dammam–Hafar al-Batin mining railway with a branch to King Khalid Military City near the Iraqi border — the supply chain for an invasion, built before the war was declared. She also told the president when and how to invade and pledged $12.5bn for his midterms (Ch. 5); Washington knew (Ch. 3).
- **Why the office matters:** it is the prime minister's own intelligence service. What had been an American matter is now in a Japanese government file: a private citizen — a child — running war logistics abroad.
- **Why she drops her head:** she can call the rest unfair (the tax case is "a stray bullet"; the immunity was Kiyomaro's call), but not this — "It would be no good to have an elementary schooler running that sort of thing instead of the government." Three reasons: **accountability** (war belongs to cabinet, Diet and voters — Koizumi: "You simply aren't qualified… You leave everything to proxies"); **dual power** (Takenaga: "Having the two of them at the very top during a war would be a total nightmare" — the Japanese "dual power structure," the shadow-shogun problem, general knowledge); **killing** ("A child is trying to help kill people"). Her excuse — the Foreign Ministry's paralysis — she keeps to herself.
- **Motive and method:** Bahrain is why Koizumi attacks; the tax case is how. Tachibana: "Make the girl behave… please withdraw from Iraq." Angela and Eva's silence means Washington agrees. Ch. 12: the Saudi railway and the Bahrain base pass to American hands. Vol. 5 Ch. 5 shows the plan reached mass-casualty deterrence (a nuclear strike on Fallujah).

**4. Noble privilege and the "tax evasion" case**
- **Correction: Runa is never accused of tax evasion.** The evader is Katou's office; Keika is the counterparty; Tachibana is the formal target; Runa the real one.
- **How the privilege works** (Chs. 8–9): translated "diplomatic immunity," it is noble immunity — the head of a house decides what happens to its members; crimes are judged under house rules and reported to the Privy Council, which since the war has never punished anyone itself. Membership quotas rise with rank (a duke's are large); houses sell places to outsiders — "criminals such as tax evaders in exchange for a kickback." Media pressure now brings more Privy Council reviews; once the government forced the Council to overturn a house verdict and the tax evader was expelled and arrested. *Real anchor (general knowledge):* Japan abolished peers and the Privy Council in 1947; the nearest real equivalent is Diet members' freedom from arrest during sessions (Constitution Art. 50) — the novel extends a parliamentary privilege to a hereditary class.
- **Exposure before the raid** (Ch. 8's talk show): Runa shown as the Moonlight Fund's owner behind a ~¥20 trillion group and "the source of funding for the Izumikawa faction"; under immunity only her father could punish her ("How can we believe that punishment will be fair and just?!"); noble diplomats escaping arrest in the Foreign Ministry secret-funds scandal; the Privy Council (chaired by Izumikawa, with Fuchigami) called the stronghold of Koizumi's opponents.
- **The chain:** (1) Far Eastern Developments' bubble-era Sakata complex and insider land deals; (2) Katou's office buys Sakata land at the bubble top; (3) Keika Development — the same company after rehabilitation, inside Keika Railway — buys it back at the bubble price, "an under-the-table payout" to Katou (given Katou's role in covering up her father's scandal, Vol. 1 Ch. 4, *plausibly* the price of that silence — my reading); (4) the profit goes undeclared — tax evasion — and Katou resigns; (5) the special investigations department orders a search of Keika Railway and Tachibana; (6) Kiyomaro invokes immunity because the prosecutors are really "digging up the old issue of Far Eastern Development's collusion with the East"; Runa has no say; (7) invoking it looks like guilt — "It's like they're saying there's a reason to suspect us!" — and press and opposition attack nobles and zaibatsu "above the law"; (8) Koizumi promises a bill "to outlaw the recent use of nobles' special privileges in criminal cases."
- **Why the shield became the weapon:** invoking a privilege *signals* that you need it; its value depended on not being seen, and using it on live TV — after the Foreign Ministry scandal had used up public tolerance — turned a legal question into a political one; and it let Koizumi choose targets (Anisha: Tachibana and Ichijou hit, Kiyomaro's pharma and Toudou spared — "cut off your own functioning limbs").
- **Long tail:** Tachibana to step down from Keika Railway "before the Diet removes diplomatic immunity," Ichijou to step back at the listing (Vol. 4 Ch. 12); the press reports that Tachibana "was forced to give up his right of representation when he fell under suspicion of tax evasion in his Sakata land dealings" — the label stuck to him (Vol. 7 Ch. 10); the prosecutors, "having lost him once before," return over Otsumaro's death (Vol. 7 Ch. 9); the bill as Koizumi's "showpiece," blocked by the Privy Council (Vol. 7 Chs. 3, 6); the Keikains' own uses limited to whistleblowers in their bank rescues and saving Tachibana (Vol. 7 Ch. 3). In the game, Koizumi broke the nobility and the downfall depended on her immunity as heir (Vol. 5 Ch. 9) — Vol. 4's raid is the first move of that process.

## 11. The author's method and the series' themes

- **Glossaries name real referents** — treat them as textual evidence. The Vol. 3 afterword admits out-of-order writing and post-publication mistakes, so apparent inconsistencies may be errors rather than design.

- **Cultural references as method:** familiar works whose plots mirror the book's and whose history opens onto its themes, then misread by Runa as characterization ("It's even more interesting when you know the historical context").
- **Themes:** the era itself, loved and loathed (see §11a); bright surface, irredeemable content; economics taught through objects (coin and pudding; the five-yen Valentine's puzzle); institutional inversion (dead things survive, permanent things collapse); marriage as corporate instrument; found family built with foreknowledge of its rupture; the moral cost of foresight; accountability versus proxy power; administrative guidance as a transnational habit (MOF and the Fed).
- **Image pages carry content** (Vols. 6–7): the family tree, the group chart and the "Next Time" previews are images only; check them in every EPUB.
- **The occult is allegory with stated referents** (Vol. 7): the author ties Hotaru to Koizumi's luck and the supernatural arc to the dispatch amendment; the academy's machine models the hidden costs of catch-up growth. Read the Seven Wonders as economics and history in folk dress.

## 11a. Theme: the era — nostalgia and trauma *(added 9 October 2026)*

*The reader's thesis, developed in discussion: the author both loves and loathes the era he lived through. That double feeling is why the series is so densely tied to real events, people, art and media, and why Runa fights the era itself while treasuring every moment of respite. The evidence below supports it.*

**The core reading (strongly supported).** The series' subject is less finance or politics than how it felt to live through Japan from 1997 to 2008. The economics and politics are the vehicle. The author writes as someone who grieves the period and misses it at the same time.

**Trauma — what the era did.**
- **The first life is a case history of the "employment ice age" generation:** a mortgage taken at bubble prices, student debt, an exploitative employer, isolation, and an early death after the company collapsed (Vol. 4 Ch. 11; the premise in Vol. 1). *Plausible:* the author writes as one of that generation.
- **The whole alternate history grows from one wish:** that the collapses of November 1997 had been stopped. The Vol. 1 afterword notes that the web serial took off because of its Hokkaido Takushoku connection — readers responded to that "if only."
- **The scar shows in small habits.** She orders diner food at her villa because "my soul had been shaped by the financial struggles of my previous life" (Vol. 2 Ch. 6). She values the coins in her wallet over the billions in her account, and won't let Mitsuya pay for her coffee (Vol. 2 Chs. 3, 6).
- **Her father is the era's earlier victim:** "He got caught up in the times. That was what made it so tragic when the times abandoned him" (Tachibana, Vol. 1).

**Nostalgia — what the era gave.**
- **It lives in the culture, not the system:** PHS phones, MiniDisc Walkmans ("In this era, we still used minidiscs"), photo booths (glossary: "exploded in popularity between 1999 and 2002"), *Serial Experiments Lain*, *Cowboy Bebop*, *Boogiepop*, *Maria-sama ga Miteru*, *Final Fantasy VII*, *Suiyō Dō Deshō*, *Iron Chef*, Bungei Shunjū's gossip column, 2channel threads, Porno Graffitti on the radio.
- **The glossaries work as an annotated scrapbook** — memory turned into footnotes.
- **The author says so.** Vol. 2 afterword: "part of me can't believe how much time has passed, while another part of me feels nostalgic… a tumultuous time full of changes in the world, which I experienced for myself. Hopefully I properly conveyed my feelings from that time."

**The split runs through the politics too.**
- **The author on Koizumi:** "a real beacon of hope" when he first appeared (Vol. 2 afterword), and "as someone who lived through that era, I couldn't write about the theater politics negatively in the end" (Vol. 4 afterword).
- **Yet Koizumi dismantles everything Runa protects:** the zaibatsu, patient capital, public works, regional railways.
- **Runa's side is the old order** — the same system that failed her first self. She saves the system that crushed her in order to save the people inside it. That is love and loathing in one plot.
- **The author's stated aim is understanding, not verdict:** "what I want to emphasize is how the people of that time chose those things" (Vol. 4 afterword); "Why did the people of that time period choose Koizumi Junichiro over Ishihara Shintaro?" (Vol. 5 afterword).

**Runa against the era.**
- **The fight:** "Hoping is futile. The economy isn't going to recover by itself. We need to fix it" (Vol. 2 Ch. 2). The Japanese tagline calls the series "a modern villainess's tale of Japan's revival." In Vol. 4 Ch. 2 she says her goal was "the revival of Japan's economy," and "I've deserted such good people… this country… this era."
- **The era takes the ground back.** Foreknowledge only redirects disasters. Koizumi wins; 9/11 happens; subprime arrives. "There was no struggling against the powerful currents of the times we lived in" (Vol. 2 Ch. 8). The times "wouldn't allow" her to walk away (Tachibana, Vol. 2 Ch. 7).
- **The villainess is the era's casualty:** "a sacrifice, taken down by Japanese culture and its systems" (Vol. 4 Ch. 5). In the game, the era used her up (Vol. 5 Ch. 9).
- **The series' mature answer:** you cannot beat an era. You can soften it and choose what to keep — her own phrase is a twenty-year "war of retreat" (Vol. 2 Ch. 5).

**The respites — the counter-argument.**
- **The scenes:**
  - the Avanti table booked for every 22 December;
  - Tanabata — "That the four of us can keep having fun," "a wish I knew would not come true" (Vol. 2 Ch. 4);
  - the fireworks — "Yes, let's," words "so heart-wrenchingly wistful, it was as if somebody else entirely had spoken them" (Vol. 2 Ch. 6);
  - "Dear God, even if I fall to my utter ruin, please don't let me forget the time I spent with them";
  - the busker; *Little Women*; the cake she bakes badly; the tape — "Yes, I'm happy" — and the chapter titled "Time to Eat" (Vol. 4 Ch. 11).
- **She knows they are temporary as she lives them.**
- **What they are made of:** pocket money, grape juice, songs, meals. *Strongly supported:* the era prices everything as "bad debt" with an exchange rate — banks, companies, and finally twenty-three million Iraqis (Vol. 4 Ch. 11). The respites are the only things in the book nobody puts a price on. They are not an escape from the theme; they are its answer.

**Nuances and limits.**
- *Plausible:* **the nostalgia remembers the era from comfortable rooms.** Elite school, maids, peerage. Those the era crushed — Northern migrants, Iraqis, purchased orphans — are mostly seen from above. Nostalgia misses the parts of the past that were kind to you.

## 15. Glossary of key terms

- **Convoy system (*gosō sendan hōshiki*):** the Finance Ministry's postwar regime ensuring no bank failed — rate caps, segmentation, branch licensing, administrative guidance, arranged rescue mergers. Effectively ended Nov 1997–1998.
- **Jūsen:** "housing loan specialist companies," founded in the 1970s, drifted into commercial property lending; the 1996 settlement used ¥685bn of public money.
- **Sōkaiya ("corporate bouncers"):** racketeers who extorted companies with the threat of disruption at shareholder meetings — and were paid by management to silence other shareholders.
- **Tobashi:** hiding losses by shuttling them between accounts or entities across reporting dates.
- **MOF-tan / amakudari:** a bank's ministry liaison / retired bureaucrats placed in firms they once supervised.
- **BOJ special loan:** unsecured emergency central-bank lending at the government's request; needs no Diet vote.
- **RCC:** Resolution and Collection Corporation (1999; from the Jusen collection body and the Resolution and Collection Bank).
- **Keika Rules:** dismiss executives; punish malpractice; send all bad loans to the RCC; capital reduction; third-party allotment; BOJ special loan.
- **Capital reduction (100%):** cancelling worthless shares so owners absorb losses first, paired with a new share issue.
- **Third-party allotment / reverse merger:** new shares issued to a chosen investor / a smaller entity absorbing a larger one to reset carrying values and keep its licences and loans.
- **Call market / Japan premium:** the interbank overnight market whose closure kills a firm / the surcharge foreign lenders charged Japanese banks.
- **Mark-to-market (current-value accounting) / cross-shareholding:** carrying assets at current prices / mutual group shareholdings that act as a takeover shield.
- **BSPC:** Banks' Shareholdings Purchase Corporation (2002), buyer of banks' unwound shares.
- **Financial Big Bang (1996–2001):** Hashimoto's "Free, Fair, Global" deregulation; the holding-company ban lifted (1997); the deposit guarantee capped at ¥10m plus interest (2002/2005).
- **FILP:** Fiscal Investment and Loan Program, funded by postal savings and insurance; financed public works.
- **Closed / open list:** 1998 upper-house PR (party sets the rank) / since 2001 (candidates' personal votes set the rank).
- ***Kōnin* / *tsuika kōnin*:** party endorsement / post-election endorsement.
- **Shōnin kanmon / sankōnin:** sworn Diet witness (perjury liable) / unsworn reference witness.
- **Jiban, kanban, kaban:** support base, name, money — the "three ban" of Japanese elections.
- **Mikoshi:** a figurehead carried by others. **Misogi:** an election as ritual purification. **Kuromaku / fixer:** the power behind the curtain.
- **Gang of Five (Gonin Gumi):** the April 2000 closed-door selection of Mori.
- **Privy Council:** surviving noble institution; power suspended, not repealed.
- **Noble immunity ("diplomatic immunity" in the translation):** the head of a house judges its members under house rules and reports to the Privy Council; membership quotas by rank; places sold to outsiders; subject to Privy Council review under media and government pressure (Vol. 4 Chs. 8–9; §10a).
- **Cabinet Intelligence and Research Office (*Naichō*):** the prime minister's intelligence office; it uncovers the Bahrain project in Vol. 4 Ch. 9.
- **Dual power structure:** a formal leader alongside an unaccountable shadow one — Takenaga's "two of them at the very top during a war."
- **Formal vs substantive equality of opportunity:** no one barred / circumstances compensated — the gap Kanbe calls neoliberalism's "handicap" (Vol. 4 Ch. 9).
- **CoCom:** Cold War export-control regime.
- **Vulture fund / *hagetaka*:** distressed-debt buyer; in Japan conflated with turnaround and buyout funds. **Defect warranty:** the LTCB sale clause letting the buyer return loans that lost 20%+ of value.
- **CDS / subprime:** credit default swap (AIG's undoing) / lending to weak borrowers.
- **Zashiki warashi:** a household spirit in Japanese folklore; in Vol. 7, the spirits of dead children harvested by the academy's feng-shui machine for national luck.
- **Choufuu Council:** the Keika presidents' council (from *kachōfūgetsu*).
- **Avanti:** the café for the Quartet's 22 December ritual.
- **Consolidated / market-value accounting:** Japan's 1999–2003 "accounting big bang," which made informal, off-book structures (like the Moonlight Fund) untenable (Vol. 6).
- **Committed credit line:** a guaranteed lending facility, legalised for Japanese banks in 1999 (Vol. 6 Ch. 2).
- **Pooling equilibrium:** when taking help is a signal of weakness, the strong take it too and the signal disappears (Vol. 6, public funds).
- **Reflexivity:** Soros's term for market beliefs changing the fundamentals they price (Vol. 6, the yen trade).
- **Placement / layering / integration:** the three stages of money laundering (Vol. 6, the Anastasia account).
- **Anastasia account:** a Karafuto Bank laundering account in Switzerland labelled "the Romanov family treasure" (Vol. 6 Ch. 6).
- **Keika Resource Development:** the Moonlight Fund's resource core with Gulf Oil Development and Nichikaba; 49.9% Keika Corp / 50.1% Runa's Swiss account (Vols. 6–7).
- **Worker Dispatch Act (*haken*):** temporary-agency work law; the 2003 amendment (effective March 2004) admitted manufacturing; *haken-giri* — the mass cancellation of dispatch contracts in 2008–09; the **Toshikoshi Haken Mura**, a tent village for laid-off dispatch workers in Hibiya Park over New Year 2008–09.
- **Special Zones for Structural Reform (*kōzō kaikaku tokku*):** Koizumi's regional exceptions to national regulation (December 2002) — the model for the Karafuto Special Zone Act (Vol. 7).
- **Scavengers:** Okazaki's word for funds that feed on the first vultures' leftovers, preaching shareholder value (Vol. 7 Ch. 3).
- **Free cash flow (Jensen):** cash beyond profitable investment, over which managers and shareholders fight.
- **Control standard:** in Japanese practice, 40–50% of votes plus control (e.g., a board majority) makes a parent company.
- ***Muko yōshi*:** adoption of a son-in-law as heir, a family-firm succession device.
- ***Shinpan / fudai / tozama*:** the shogun's kin / hereditary vassals / outside lords (Kanbe's "outsider theory," Vol. 7 Ch. 7).
- **Well-and-fence (*idobei*) politician:** one who spends his fortune on the nation until only his well and fence remain (Vol. 7 Ch. 7).
- ***Kachigumi / makegumi*:** "winners" and "losers," the 2000s vocabulary of inequality (Vol. 8 preview).
