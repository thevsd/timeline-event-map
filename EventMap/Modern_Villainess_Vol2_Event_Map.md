# Modern Villainess Vol. 2 — Complete Event Map (Second Edition)
### Chapter-by-chapter, with extended economic and political analysis and a running answer to the "what is Runa, actually?" question

**Second-edition additions and corrections.**
1. **New finding — the "locked room" is the real Gang of Five.** The officials waiting at Fuchigami's hospital bed in Ch. 5 match, role for role, the five LDP leaders who chose Mori in April 2000, and the names appear to be deliberate inversions: **Hayashi = Mori** (森 forest → 林 woods), **Akagi = Aoki** (blue tree → red tree), **Murashita = Murakami** (village-above → village-below), **Tsurui = Kamei** (turtle → crane); **Nonaka** keeps his real name. Hayashi's later cabinet is Mori's.
2. **New identifications.** Takenaga Nobutame = **Takenaka Heizō**; "Ayukawa Motors" = **Nissan** (named for founder Ayukawa Yoshisuke; taken over by Renault in 1999); the treacherous coalition partner in Ch. 5 = **Ozawa Ichirō**.
3. **New Part II sections (§6–§7):** the full mechanism linking market-value accounting, cross-shareholdings and zaibatsu dissolution, with verified real-world data; and the economics of postal privatization as it bears on Runa.
4. **New Part III — the politics of Volume 2:** Ichijou's hiring veto, Nakamaro's unsworn testimony and the guardianship, the "knife" line, Katou as the man who took Runa for "a mere prop," Fuchigami's hospital confession, the caretaker strategy, the 2001 leadership-election trap, and the "political pandemonium" of the Aso endorsement. The old synthesis is now Part IV; "What Is Runa, Actually?" is Part V.
5. **Verified 2001 leadership figures** added (Koizumi 123 of 141 prefectural votes; final 298–155–31).
6. **Labeling.** The novel never spells out how postal privatization hurts Runa; that analysis is marked as inference throughout.

**Scope:** All 8 chapters, all glossaries, afterword, plus synthesis and continuity with Vol. 1. Built from a full read of the EPUB (Tofuro Futsukaichi, illus. KEI; Seven Seas English ed.; Japanese original 2021).

**Coverage:** Fall 1998 → spring 2001, confirmed by the author's afterword. Vol. 1 ended autumn 1998; this volume picks up immediately and runs to April 2001, closing on a date the reader will recognize instantly.

**Method note, unchanged from Vol. 1:** each chapter carries an author's glossary that frequently names its own real-world referents. Where a glossary states a source, that is **textual evidence**, marked *confirmed by the text*. Parallels I identify myself carry separate confidence labels.

**Structural note:** Volume 2 is markedly more documentary and more multi-POV than Volume 1. Chapter 1 is a CIA dossier with no narration at all; Chapter 3 and Chapter 7 are entirely outside Runa's POV; Chapter 8 ends with a block of in-world newspaper extracts. Roughly a third of the volume is told by people other than the protagonist — a significant escalation of a technique Vol. 1 used sparingly.

# Part I — Chapter-by-Chapter Map

## Chapter 1 — The Sullivan Report

**Narrative position:** Opens the volume as a pure document — a CIA intelligence assessment written by Angela Sullivan, analyst at the US Embassy in Japan. No narration, no scene. Functions as both a recap of Vol. 1's outcome and a corporate-structure reference sheet.

**What happens (contents of the report)**
- Establishes Runa as "the real owner of the foreign-run Moonlight Fund," monitored outright since the Asian and Russian crises, on account of her parents' Eastern connection and her Romanov blood.
- Maps the post-Vol. 1 corporate structure: Keika Corp merged into **Akamatsu Corporation**; Keika Maritime Insurance and Far Eastern Life Insurance merged with Teisei's insurance arms under Keika Bank; **Keika Pharmaceuticals remains the group's central company**, and — pointedly — the Keika Group's main bank is the **Imperial Iwazaki Bank, not Keika Bank**.
- Notes Runa personally holds few group shares; her control runs through Keika Bank (which inherited the old Far Eastern Bank holdings) and through the Moonlight Fund, which owns Keika Hotels, Keika Bank, Akamatsu, AIRHO, and more.
- Records that she has made **no move to take over the Keika Group out of respect for her uncle, Keikain Kiyomaro**.
- Keika Bank: bought at auction for ¥800bn in fall 1998; market capitalization now said to exceed **¥3 trillion**; management has promised a stock listing within five years to answer fears it has become an "institutional bank."
- Teisei Department Stores: bad debts "in excess of 1.57 trillion yen," restructuring underway, financial arms to Keika Bank, hotels to Keika Hotels, retail to Akamatsu. Its fresh-food-direct-to-Kanto model, built on the old Hokkaido Kaitaku Bank's primary-industry relationships, is working.
- Notes the Keika Group's internal "tug-of-war" between old-guard companies wanting a presidents' council and newer ones wanting Runa's independent leadership.
- Flags her political reach: Izumikawa is now party vice president; **Yanagitani**, of Izumikawa's faction, chairs the Financial Reconstruction Commission.
- Records that Izumikawa has unofficially complained to the US Embassy about the surveillance of Runa.
- One section is marked **[OMITTED]**.
- Concludes: she must not be allowed to side with America's enemies; recommendation is continued monitoring **and finding a way to procure her as a US asset**.

**Revelations**
- The CIA knows everything material about her. Vol. 1's secrecy is over.
- The Iwazaki Bank — not her own bank — remains the Keika Group's main bank, which is the structural crack the whole volume exploits.
- The recommendation to "procure her as an asset" sets up Ch. 7's payoff.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** "institutional bank" as a pre-war marker of zaibatsu status; and bank holding companies as "a centerpiece of Japan's financial big bang," correctly described, including the advantage of "using a smaller subsidiary bank to swallow up a larger one" — the exact reverse-merger trick from Vol. 1's Ch. 2.
- **Textual inconsistency worth noting:** Teisei's bad debts are given as ¥1.57 trillion here and ¥1.75 trillion in Vol. 1 Ch. 8. Possibly post-restructuring revision; possibly a transposition error.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* Opening with a hostile external assessment inverts Vol. 1's structure. Vol. 1 opened inside Runa's head at her moment of defeat; Vol. 2 opens outside it, in the file of a foreign intelligence service. The reader is repositioned from confidant to observer.
- *Plausible:* The **[OMITTED]** marker is a deliberate reminder that even this document is partial — nobody in this book has complete information, including the narrator.

**Connections:** Recaps Vol. 1 Chs. 2 and 9. The Iwazaki relationship drives Chs. 2 and 8. Angela's recommendation is answered, with irony, in Ch. 7.

## Chapter 2 — The Grade-Schooler and Her Place: Fuchigami's Regime

**Narrative position:** Fall 1998 → spring 1999. The volume's first major arc: a political scandal aimed squarely at Runa, resolved by winning the Tokyo governorship.

**What happens**
- At the Moonlight Fund's Silicon Valley building, Runa hosts a technicians' party, bringing Japanese anime on VHS as gifts. Angela Sullivan attends, having flown economy to tail her.
- They fence amicably. Angela is exhausted from "cleaning up after that collapsed Wall Street fund"; Runa is moving into resources, "buying low" amid the Russian-crisis wreckage via Akamatsu.
- A technician begs her to fund building something exceeding the limits of the anime. Her internal reply: it'll take about ten years.
- **Prime Minister Fuchigami telephones her** to ask whether Ayukawa Motors — Japan's second automaker, ~¥2 trillion in interest-bearing debt — can be saved. She says no: no one has a rehabilitation plan, and "there's a difference between a product a company thinks is good and a product consumers think is good." She recommends a merger with Tazmi Motor Corporation over Teia, on antitrust and scale grounds.
- She identifies his real question: he's worried about the *banks* absorbing ¥2 trillion, not the carmaker.
- Fuchigami asks Keika Bank to handle small and medium enterprises under the **Keika Rules** as banks turn hostile. Runa agrees and asks for public funds to accelerate SME bad-debt resolution. Ayukawa later becomes a European carmaker's subsidiary.
- Ichijou presents his SME rescue toolkit (see the economics section — this is the volume's best sustained piece of financial writing).
- **The accounting reform fight:** the Imperial Business Federation freezes the "accounting big bang" — a switch from book-value to **current/market-value accounting**. Both ruling and opposition parties see the switch as economic stimulus bundled with **dissolution of the zaibatsu**, threatening cross-shareholdings.
- Arriving at a Federation party as Kiyomaro's "accessory," Runa is mobbed by **entertainment** reporters — and notices the telling absence of *financial* reporters. Nakamaro orders her withdrawn before she can enter.
- The scandal breaks: a "dark link" between Vice President Izumikawa and the Keika Group, alleging the Keika Bank auction was rigged in exchange for the group's support of Councilor Izumikawa Taichirou. The opposition considers subpoenaing Izumikawa.
- Runa concludes a Keika Bank executive leaked that the Moonlight Fund is in her name, aiming to dethrone Tachibana and Ichijou. Ichijou vetoes planting a Far Eastern loyalist, recommending instead hiring cut-off ex-MOF officials.
- **Nakamaro visits.** He states the family's reasonable move would be to take the Moonlight Fund — and assumes she has already moved the assets (she has: ¥300bn hidden in Akamatsu shell companies; the Fund itself now over ¥1 trillion, most of it earmarked to repay Keika Bank's BOJ special loan). He reveals **Iwazaki Pharma has proposed a merger with Keika Pharmaceuticals** — i.e. a takeover of the Keika Group. He declines to take the Fund, and volunteers to **testify to the House of Councilors as an unsworn witness in her place**, knowingly accepting public suspicion. Kiyomaro is publicly declared her guardian.
- At school, three girls offer superficial support (Katsuki Shiori, Kurimori Shizuka, Takahashi Akiko — Runa notes these are probably her in-game followers, none of whom stayed). Asuka and Hotaru are genuine. Eiichi, Yuujirou, and Mitsuya treat her exactly as before; Eiichi deduces she controls the Fund by elimination.
- Paparazzi breach the school. Eiichi's coded pager warns them; the four execute a practiced evasion.
- **Runa counterattacks.** Visiting the PM's residence as Hokkaido's Goodwill Ambassador of Agriculture — a legally careful PR event with produce samples and a postcard competition — she uses fifteen private minutes to tell Fuchigami: "It doesn't take a knife to silence a politician. All it takes is an electoral victory."
- She engineers the **Tokyo gubernatorial election**: have Izumikawa persuade the ruling party's candidate to withdraw, promise post-election endorsement, and then back a candidate nobody expects.
- She visits retired literary master and former Transport Minister **Iwasawa Makoto**. He dismisses her until she offers him not logic or profit but a *narrative*: "We're taking back the capital." He accepts on one condition — that he be allowed to write her into a book.
- Iwasawa wins in a landslide; the party endorses him after the fact. The scandal collapses; Keika Holdings proceeds. His campaign novel, *The Little Queen Takes Back the Capital*, becomes a bestseller with a film adaptation; Runa declines the lead role. His brother's action-production company warns the media off her.

**Revelations**
- The Iwazaki Group is moving to absorb the Keika Group — the volume's central threat.
- Nakamaro and Kiyomaro have "seen through" Runa entirely.
- Nakamaro's sacrifice is voluntary and reputationally costly.
- Runa's assets are far larger than Vol. 1 implied: over ¥1 trillion, plus ¥300bn hidden.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the anime is **Serial Experiments Lain**; the OS is **Windows 98**; the thing exceeding anime's limits is **the iPhone** (announced January 2007); the bounty-hunter anime is **Cowboy Bebop**; **PHS** took off in 1999, ending the pager era.
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** "Big Bang Accounting Reforms: In real life, it was the collapse of the Long-Term Credit Bank of Japan, which until then looked perfectly financially healthy, that triggered these reforms. Even this wasn't enough warning to prevent the collapses of Enron or Lehman Brothers Holdings Inc." — the book again linking its own 1990s material forward to 2001 and 2008.
- **Iwasawa Makoto = Shintarō Ishihara. Very high confidence, verified.** The real Ishihara: Akutagawa Prize-winning novelist; **Minister of Transport 1987–88**; quit national politics in **1995**, handing his base to his son; his younger brother Yūjirō was a legendary action star whose company (Ishihara Promotion) managed action talent; announced late and **won the 11 April 1999 Tokyo gubernatorial election** with 1,664,558 votes. Every one of those specifics appears in the novel.
- **The surrounding field is also real.** The incumbent "former celebrity with all the independents behind him" is **Yukio Aoshima**, a television personality who won in 1995 as an independent. "One of the opposition members from the Lower House" who resigned to run is **Kunio Hatoyama**. "The Tokyo chapter… moving to back a political scientist" is **Yōichi Masuzoe**. The LDP's official "bureaucratic candidate" was **Yasushi Akashi**, who finished fourth — the party's worst-ever showing, which is exactly why Runa says "we won't win this race by choosing an ex-bureaucrat."
- **One clean alteration:** in reality Aoshima announced he would *not* seek re-election. The novel has the ruling party "take the city back from the current governor," implying an incumbent contest.
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** "Acknowledging a Candidate Post-Election… often used by a certain ruling party in real life, even now" — the real LDP practice of *tsuika kōnin*, post-election endorsement, which is the exact mechanism Runa proposes.
- **Real, used accurately:** censure motions in a hung House of Councilors; unsworn-witness Diet testimony as political theatre; paparazzi privileges; the Political Funds Control Act.
- **The hung upper house was real.** After the 1998 election the LDP lacked an upper-house majority until coalition deals with the Liberal Party (January 1999) and Komeito (October 1999) — the "alliance negotiations" Runa mentions. During that period an upper-house censure motion did force a cabinet minister to resign.
- **Sworn versus unsworn testimony.** A sworn Diet summons (*shōnin kanmon*) runs under the 1947 testimony law — oath, perjury punishable by three months to ten years, a public ordeal. An unsworn "reference witness" (*sankōnin*) appears voluntarily with no oath. Offering the unsworn version is the standard ruling-side compromise — the "deal with the House of Councilors" Nakamaro proposes. *See Part III §2.*
- **"Ayukawa Motors" = Nissan. High confidence.** The name is Nissan's founder, Ayukawa Yoshisuke; Japan's second automaker with roughly ¥2 trillion in debt matches Nissan in 1999; and "becomes a European carmaker's subsidiary" is Renault's 1999 takeover.
- **The "knife" line** carries real weight in Japanese political memory — Prime Minister Hara Takashi was stabbed to death at Tokyo Station in 1921, Socialist leader Asanuma Inejirō on stage in 1960. *[The echo is my reading; the text names neither.]* *See Part III §3.*

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* The chapter's thesis is that **a scandal is a market in attention**, and the way to end one is not rebuttal but a bigger story. Runa doesn't disprove the allegation; she buries it under an election. The absence of financial reporters at the party is the tell she reads correctly.
- *Strongly supported:* Iwasawa is persuaded by narrative, not argument — and then turns Runa herself into narrative. The book is quietly noting that its protagonist has become a fictional character *inside her own world*.
- *Plausible:* Runa declining the film role while accepting the fictionalization marks the limit she does police: she will be a story, but not a performer of one.
- *Strongly supported:* **The accounting reform is a threat to the zaibatsu's skeleton.** Cross-shareholdings only work if nobody sells; market-value accounting gives everyone a reason to sell. Tachibana: it "could either make or break the zaibatsu, and it risks our cross holdings." *See Part II §6.*
- *Plausible:* **Ichijou's hiring veto is institution-building.** Planting a Far Eastern man would confirm the merged bank's other tribes' fear of colonization; neutral ex-ministry officials belong to no tribe. The hunt for the traitor goes to Tachibana — Ichijou stays clean. *See Part III §1.*
- *Strongly supported:* **Nakamaro's testimony and the guardianship are a two-part exit**: unsworn testimony buys time, the Tokyo election ends the scandal — exactly as Nakamaro predicts ("It will last no longer than the Tokyo gubernatorial election"). The guardianship announcement also plants the seed of Vol. 3's control crisis. *See Part III §2.*
- *Strongly supported:* **The "knife" line rejects her grandfather's world while reproducing his method** — Runa wins by kingmaking from behind the curtain, which is precisely what Fuchigami warns her about in Ch. 5. *See Part III §3.*

**Connections:** The Iwazaki merger threat runs to Ch. 8. Iwasawa reappears in Ch. 3. The accounting-reform fight is the thread Koizumi picks up in Ch. 8. The entertainment-industry leverage is developed in Ch. 4. The guardianship becomes the formal adoption in Vol. 3 that the Iwazaki Corporation tries to exploit.

## Chapter 3 — The Party at Meguro's Wise Dragon King's Palace

**Narrative position:** A **fully external POV chapter** — two parallel secret meetings at a high-class restaurant, neither attended by Runa. Structurally the most important chapter in the volume for what the reader learns and she doesn't.

**What happens — upstairs (the politicians)**
- Fuchigami, Izumikawa, and Governor Iwasawa meet secretly, arriving separately and dining apart.
- They toast "to our Little Queen." Iwasawa asks if she works for one of them. Answer: "Of course not. We're the ones being used."
- They compare her to **her grandfather Hikomaro**, described as a wartime fixer with influence over the police and dubious companies — and the comparison frightens them.
- Izumikawa confirms **the ¥800bn for Keika Bank was sourced by Runa herself**, and that he "decided to stop thinking too hard about it."
- Katou's failure in the gubernatorial election has cost him; he had been planning to seize Izumikawa's faction via "generational change." Izumikawa notes Runa has *not* turned on him — she still funds the Yamagata Shinkansen, in Katou's own region. "His failure was in thinking she was a mere prop. I might have too, if I hadn't met her in person." Katou treated her the way the public did — as a figurehead for the Keika Group's adults, the political *mikoshi* carried by others — and never imagined she would act independently through Izumikawa. *See Part III §4.*
- **¥60 trillion of public funds** has been set aside for bad debts; Keika Bank is to be the model encouraging megabank mergers.
- The real purpose: Izumikawa asks Iwasawa to have the Tokyo Metropolitan Police **protect Runa**, citing the kidnapping (hushed up as a family matter) and CIA contact. Fuchigami will add CIRO and the Public Safety Bureau.
- Iwasawa proposes a specific man as lieutenant governor and crisis manager: head of the Cabinet Security Affairs Office, riot-police commander, veteran of confrontations with far-left extremists.
- Closing line: "The little queen would never know that she was being protected."

**What happens — downstairs (Runa's men)**
- Tachibana, Ichijou, and Toudou meet separately on Tachibana's summons, to discuss Runa's cash flow — known only to the three of them.
- The problem: her fortune is in US dollars; Russian bonds are being taken in exchange for oil, sold in Japan for yen — but **there is no domestic account to hold the yen**. The Keikain family account is explicitly "not secure," because the kidnapping was arranged by someone connected to the family.
- The solution: a **trust bank**. Three trust banks (Ichiyama, Long-Period Credit, Nihon Credit) will merge into **Keika Trust Bank**, and Runa's account will sit in its Sapporo private-banking division under **Katsura Naoyuki**, chosen because the post is too junior for executives to fight over — buying time before the main family notices. ~¥50bn is expected in the first year.
- Tachibana notes he already has Runa's approval: he told her he wanted an account for her profits and she agreed instantly, with no interest in trust mechanics.
- **The chapter's key aside:** "These three men had no way of knowing that Her Ladyship valued the few thousand yen in her wallet much more than the billion yen in her bankbook — to the extent that she found herself desperately making excuses to her maid… who scolded her for spending too much on manga, novels, candy, and games."
- **Toudou proposes a private intelligence and security apparatus**: recruiting from "the power elite of Northern Japan… the brainwashing factory that trained orphans to be spies" — **the Toyohara girls** — plus retired Northern Japanese soldiers.
- Ichijou objects that this "sounds very much like human trafficking, no matter how you spin it," and walks out saying he will "pretend you didn't say a word."
- Toudou, alone with Tachibana: "If you, Her Ladyship's treasurer, is not kept pure, you are sure to corrupt her. Leave all the dirty work to Tachibana-san and me." Tachibana authorizes the plan in secret.
- Izumikawa then joins Tachibana privately. They reminisce: Izumikawa was once the young MOF bureaucrat summoned by Hikomaro, with Tachibana as the messenger. Tachibana nominates **Ichijou for the top position at Keika Holdings**; Izumikawa agrees.
- Tachibana returns home. Runa greets him in pajamas and tells him not to overwork. He says nothing about the account. The chapter's last line: she remained unaware, "even after Keika Holdings and the account at Keika Trust Bank were established, and the account filled with tens of billions of yen."

**Revelations**
- Runa is being protected by a covert apparatus — police, CIRO, Public Safety — without her knowledge.
- Her own men are building a private intelligence service from trafficked former child spies, deliberately concealed from both her and Ichijou.
- Ichijou is being kept deliberately "pure" as a moral firewall — an arrangement he has half-consented to by walking out rather than objecting further.
- She is financially dissociated: indifferent to billions, anxious about pocket money.
- Her father's generation and Izumikawa's are linked through Tachibana as Hikomaro's messenger.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the restaurant is based on the real **Meguro Gajoen**, "which gave inspiration for the bathhouse in the animated movie Spirited Away"; and its bubble-era scandal earned it the nickname "the Demon of Gyoninzaka."
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the security chief is "a descendant of a Sengoku military commander, he led the police force during the **Yasuda Auditorium** and **Asama-Sansou** incidents" — both real (1969 and 1972). This is unmistakably **Sassa Atsuyuki**, real first head of the Cabinet Security Affairs Office and a descendant of Sassa Narimasa. *Uncertain whether his novel-appointment as Tokyo lieutenant governor mirrors real events; not verified this pass.*
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** "The Former Prime Minister: The **Keiseikai** Faction's founder and first leader… before he overthrew his seniors and formed the faction, everyone called his boss 'Father.'" Keiseikai is the real Takeshita faction; **Noboru Takeshita** really did break from **Tanaka Kakuei** ("Oyaji"), and Obuchi really did inherit the faction — consistent with Fuchigami = Obuchi.
- **Real, accurate:** trust banks and the theoretical bankruptcy-remoteness of trust assets (the glossary is dryly skeptical: "in practice this apparently doesn't work too well"); **C. Wright Mills'** concept of the power elite.
- **Real, ~¥60 trillion in public funds** matches the scale of Japan's real late-1990s financial-stabilization framework.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* This chapter formalizes the volume's central irony. Runa believes she is the manipulator; two rooms of adults are simultaneously managing her, one to protect her and one to arm her, and neither tells her.
- *Strongly supported:* Toudou's "keep the treasurer pure" doctrine is a deliberate moral architecture — the book is constructing a protagonist who is *insulated* from her own operation's worst parts rather than innocent of them.
- *Plausible:* The Toyohara girls are set up as a future moral reckoning. Ichijou's walkout is not a refusal; it's plausible deniability, and the text knows it.

**Connections:** The trust-bank account and Naoyuki's posting pay off across the volume. The Toyohara plan is unresolved. Tachibana's protective arrangement is deepened in Ch. 7.

## Chapter 4 — A Lady's Everyday Life: Fall 1998–Summer 1999

**Narrative position:** The volume's long slice-of-life chapter, interleaving school life with continuous business developments. Mirrors Vol. 1's Ch. 8.

**What happens**
- **Field day.** Runa wins the sprint; the Quartet sweeps its events but the class loses ground because rival classes concentrate talent where the Quartet isn't running, and their own classmates coast.
- A pushy sports photographer gate-crashes to shoot her in new sneakers. She refuses, is ignored, and relents. The resulting shoes sell out as a Teisei exclusive.
- She and Eiichi are entered in the **three-legged race** and fall flat immediately — Mitsuya's verdict: "a prime example of what happens when two partners are both in it for themselves."
- In the final relay, cheered on by all three boys, she invokes the frame — "I was born a villainess. I would lose to the protagonist — but nobody else" — and takes her team from last to first.
- **Culture festival.** The class stages an opera; Runa sings the **Queen of the Night aria** from *The Magic Flute*. Tachibana, Ichijou, and Nakamaro appear unannounced. She receives genuine applause, and cries: "I'd long since forgotten what praise was like in my previous life." The conductor: "This applause is the praise you should have received at the previous concert."
- Walking with Yuujirou beforehand, she admits feeling "guilty, like I'm cheating somehow." Narration: "That was because I was."
- **The KDK Imperial Opera Company**, a subsidiary of Kansai Dentetsu Railway slated for disbandment, asks for patronage. Tachibana pushes it; pressed, he admits the real reason — "it would be good to have connections within the performing arts for the sake of your protection." Runa notes the deep links between performing arts and the underworld. It becomes the **Keika Opera Company**, touring Teisei venues.
- Runa articulates the media strategy: many TV networks began as newspapers; control the networks and you restrain the papers. She sponsors actors for Iwasawa Productions' detective drama.
- **The megabank merger wave begins** (see economics section): DK Bank + Fuyou Bank + Commercial Bank of Japan; then Futaki Bank + Yodoyabashi Bank; then Owari Nagoya + Yamanaka; then Asahi + Osaka Nomura.
- Ichijou and Runa assess **Sougou Department Stores** as the most likely large failure. Keika Bank has already sold its dangerous loans cheaply to the RCC and built reserves. Discussion of buying **Sachii** is dropped since it has fixed its cash flow.
- Runa confirms **Ichijou will head Keika Holdings** for at least five years and instructs him to build a team and choose a successor.
- **The Sougou–Teisei bailout merger** is announced, spearheaded by the RCC, with ¥630bn of debt written off and Sougou's managing director resigning. CBJ had already announced the three-way merger; Sougou was its largest bad-debt source.
- **December 22, Avanti café.** The four say their pre-holiday goodbyes. Eiichi books the table for the same date every following year. Runa: "Dear God, even if I fall to my utter ruin, please don't let me forget the time I spent with them."
- New Year PHS calls connect all four; she has an antenna station built near the Vice President's house.
- A school snowball fight (she loses to a coordinated Eiichi–Yuujirou pincer), snowman building, and a ski trip to Karuizawa.

**Revelations**
- Runa's musical talent is real and matters to her more than money.
- Her patronage decisions are partly disguised security decisions, chosen by Tachibana.
- She experiences her own advantages as cheating.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** *Catan*; the Indian film **Muthu**; Mitsuya's book **Boogiepop and Others** (Kōhei Kadono, KADOKAWA); Runa's book **Maria-sama ga Miteru** (Oyuki Konno, Shueisha); Beethoven's Ninth as a Japanese year-end fixture; NHK's *Old Year, New Year*; **RayStorm**; **Street Fighter**; **Super Real Mahjong PV**; and a frank definition of pork-barrel politicians.
- **Real, very high confidence — the megabank wave.** All four announced mergers map onto the real 1999–2000 consolidation: **DK Bank + Fuyou + Commercial Bank of Japan = Dai-Ichi Kangyo + Fuji + Industrial Bank of Japan → Mizuho** (announced August 1999); **Futaki + Yodoyabashi = Sakura (Mitsui) + Sumitomo → Sumitomo Mitsui**; **Owari Nagoya + Yamanaka = Tokai + Sanwa → UFJ**; **Asahi + Osaka Nomura = Asahi + Daiwa → Resona**. Note the precision: "Fuyou" is the real name of Fuji Bank's keiretsu group, and **Osaka Nomura Bank is Daiwa Bank's real original name**.
- **Sougou Department Stores = Sogo. High confidence.** The real Sogo over-expanded in the bubble; its **Kobe flagship was damaged in the Great Hanshin earthquake**; its main banks were the **Industrial Bank of Japan** and **LTCB**; and it sought debt forgiveness of roughly ¥639bn — against the novel's ¥630bn. **Divergence:** in reality the waiver plan collapsed under public outcry and Sogo went bankrupt in July 2000; here it is absorbed by Teisei.
- **Real:** Karuizawa's improved Shinkansen station; the real 1999 PHS boom.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* The opera scene is the volume's emotional centre and the clearest window into her motive. What breaks her is not wealth but **being praised**, which her previous life never gave her. This substantially strengthens the "deprivation and anger" reading over the "it's all a game" reading.
- *Strongly supported:* The three-legged race is a deliberate joke on the whole series — two brilliant, self-directed people, physically tied together, fall over instantly. It prefigures the engagement.
- *Plausible:* The Avanti standing reservation is the volume's most poignant object: a ritual deliberately built to outlast the ruin she knows is coming.

**Connections:** The Sougou merger completes in Ch. 5. The entertainment holdings recur in Ch. 8. The Avanti table recurs in Ch. 6.

## Chapter 5 — Heisei's Queen of the Railway: Izumikawa's Regime

**Narrative position:** Late 1999 → 2000. The volume's largest single alteration of real history, and the start of Runa's infrastructure phase.

**What happens**
- Runa sings Beethoven's Ninth at Fuchigami's request, in thanks for bailing out **Hizen Co. Ltd.**, a hypermarket with ~¥400bn in bad debts that DK Bank intended to sacrifice before its three-way merger. She took it because Hokkaido's financial circle asked and because it had stores there; it goes under Teisei.
- **During the standing ovation, Fuchigami stumbles.** Runa recognizes it immediately: "It reminded me of the future." She has Tachibana phone Izumikawa at once — "Unless we acted, Prime Minister Fuchigami's life was in danger."
- January 2, after the Y2K non-event: Izumikawa reports that Fuchigami went to hospital on her urgent request and has been ordered to retire. **He has suffered a stroke** — a transient ischemic attack at the concert, compounded by chronic heart disease. He survives.
- At the hospital, seven senior figures wait (Secretary-General Hayashi, Policy Research Chairman Tsurui, House of Councilors Committee Chair Murashita, Acting Secretary-General Nonaka, Chief Cabinet Secretary Akagi, Izumikawa, Tachibana).
- Fuchigami tells her plainly: her father and his own mentor "both knew what they were getting into was dirty, and yet they couldn't pass up the money," and that Otsumaro believed he was making citizens happy. He admits: "I'll fully admit I used you. But I didn't want you to be subpoenaed." He warns her against following her grandfather's path.
- **Izumikawa asks her directly whether he should become prime minister** — "This is why I summoned you, small kingmaker… Of the people in said locked room, it is those with the money who hold the most sway."
- Her counsel: accept, but declare a **caretaker government**, block the leadership election until a **July general election**, because the main factions will split and the coalition partner — "a man who once held the same values as him but later went on to utterly betray him" — will exploit any opening. Izumikawa accepts a premiership he knows will last six months.
- She explains why she never backed Katou: no contact was made in either direction, probably his residual resentment of the Keikain house over her father.
- She notes a crucial mechanism: because stock prices in this timeline are **higher than they "should" have been**, unrealized gains could be used to absorb hundreds of billions in bad debts — making the cleanup far easier than in the history she remembers.
- **The railway phase.** Moonlight buys the **KYOSHO Rapid Railway** (Tokyo–Chiba, connecting to the subway) for ¥100bn, paying off its bubble-era high-interest debt at a stroke, lowering fares, and rebranding under a new **Keika Railway** company — with **Tachibana as managing director**, explicitly to build a visible, respectable business core independent of the "suspicious" Moonlight Fund before Keika Pharmaceuticals and Keika Chemicals are absorbed by Iwazaki.
- She plans to fund the **Shin-Tokiwa Railway's** unfinished Akihabara→Tokyo extension (~¥100bn, or ~¥300bn including subway linkage).
- **The Kagawa Railroad problem:** Shikoku's Kagawa Railroad depends on the Sougou store at its terminal and **guarantees that store's loans**, so pressing Sougou's creditors risks collapsing the railway. Both must be rescued together, and Keika Railway — not the Fund — must be the buyer.
- She proposes a **Shikoku Shinkansen**, Okayama–Takamatsu, ~¥400bn, exploiting the Great Seto Bridge's Shinkansen-capable design; plus a Hokkaido Shinkansen (Asahikawa–Sapporo–Hakodate) at over ¥2 trillion, which she declines to fund alone. She intends to buy a general contractor in rehabilitation so construction money returns to her own companies.
- She buys **Dog Express** for ¥140bn to strengthen logistics, handing it to Toudou and Akamatsu; next targets are the Sougou merger, Sachii post-rehabilitation, and eventually **Taiei** (~¥3 trillion in debt).
- Ichijou and Tachibana walk her through why revitalizing shopping districts is close to impossible: motorization, suburban superstores, the Large-Scale Retail Store Law revision, and — the detail that defeats her — shopkeepers *live above their shops*, so closure doesn't free the premises. Her answer is a jointly-owned convenience store and parking lot at each district's entrance, an apartment complex to consolidate residents, and freight partnerships with the Imperial Freight Railway Company.

**Revelations**
- Runa has now altered a death she remembered — the volume's clearest demonstration that her foreknowledge is actionable on individual fates, not just markets.
- Her interventions have inflated asset prices relative to her remembered history, which is *why* the bad-debt cleanup is going better. Her foreknowledge has become self-undermining: the timeline is diverging from the one she knows.
- She is deliberately constructing a "clean," visible business core against the coming Iwazaki absorption.

**Historical references**
- **The Fuchigami stroke = Obuchi's. Major alteration, high confidence.** The real Keizō Obuchi suffered a stroke on 1–2 April 2000, fell into a coma, and died on 14 May 2000. Here, warned by Runa, he is hospitalized in time, survives, and retires. Arguably the volume's single largest deliberate divergence.
- **The 1999 leadership field is real.** Fuchigami defeated "former General-Secretary **Katou Kazuhiro** and **Yamaguchi Takumi**, former chair of the Policy Research Council" — the real 1999 LDP contest, in which Obuchi beat **Kōichi Katō** and **Taku Yamasaki**. With Koizumi, this completes the mapping of the real **"YKK" trio** (Yamasaki, Katō, Koizumi → Yamaguchi, Katou, Koizumi).
- **The hospital "locked room" = the real Gang of Five. Very high confidence — new finding.** After Obuchi's stroke in April 2000, five LDP leaders — Aoki, Mori, Murakami, Nonaka and Kamei — met behind closed doors at the Akasaka Prince Hotel and chose Mori without consulting the party; he became prime minister on 5 April. The novel's waiting room matches them role for role, with the names apparently inverted (my reading of the romanizations): **Secretary-General Hayashi = Mori** (森 forest → 林 woods; Mori really was secretary-general, July 1998–April 2000); **Chief Cabinet Secretary Akagi = Aoki** (青木 blue tree → 赤木 red tree); **upper-house chief Murashita = Murakami** (村上 → 村下, the same up/down trick as Vol. 4's Murakami Fund); **Policy Research Chairman Tsurui = Kamei** (亀 turtle → 鶴 crane, Japan's classic longevity pair); **Acting Secretary-General Nonaka** keeps his real name, in his real April 2000 post. Izumikawa's line that succession is settled "in a locked room" echoes the "secret room" criticism the real meeting drew.
- **The coalition partner who "utterly betrayed" the prime minister = Ozawa Ichirō. High confidence.** Ozawa and Obuchi came up in the same faction; Ozawa's 1993 defection, voting with the opposition on a no-confidence motion, cost the LDP power; his Liberal Party joined the LDP coalition in January 1999 and pulled out in April 2000, splitting his own party — and Obuchi's stroke came the night their talks collapsed.
- **Runa's no-confidence warning is foreknowledge.** In November 2000, Katō and Yamasaki backed a failed no-confidence motion against Mori — the "Katō Rebellion." Her counsel to delay the leadership contest is designed to deny the fringe factions exactly that opening. *See Part III §6.*
- **Timing shift.** The novel gives Izumikawa a ~six-month caretaker premiership ending in a July election; the real Mori called his general election for June 2000, about three months after taking office.
- **"The prime minister who taught me"** is the Keiseikai founder (real Takeshita Noboru, whose government fell in the 1989 Recruit share scandal; his own mentor Tanaka Kakuei fell to Lockheed) — the money politics Fuchigami says her father and his mentor "couldn't pass up." *See Part III §5.*
- **KYOSHO Rapid Railway ≈ Hokusō Railway. Moderate-high confidence.** Real: Chiba, through-service to Tokyo's subway, and notorious for very high fares driven by construction debt.
- **Shin-Tokiwa Railway ≈ Tsukuba Express. Moderate-high.** Real: terminates at Akihabara with a long-discussed Tokyo Station extension; "Tokiwa" is the real JR Jōban Line express name serving the same corridor.
- **Kagawa Railroad ≈ Kotoden (Takamatsu-Kotohira Electric Railroad). High confidence.** Real: went bankrupt in 2001, entangled with the Sogo store at its Takamatsu terminal.
- **Real:** the long-proposed, never-built **Shikoku Shinkansen**; the Great Seto Bridge's genuine Shinkansen-capable design; the real Hokkaido Shinkansen program; the real revision of the Large-Scale Retail Store Law and the real hollowing-out of regional shopping districts (*shōtengai*).
- **Hizen Co. Ltd. ≈ Nagasakiya. Moderate confidence.** Real: a hypermarket chain that failed in February 2000 with debts around ¥380bn; "Hizen" is the old province name covering the Nagasaki area.
- **Taiei ≈ Daiei. High confidence** (nationwide supermarket, debt of that order, later dissolution).
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the real **2000 US presidential election** and its 327-vote Florida margin; the **desalination** debate in Kagawa; the LDP's real **Forty-Day Conflict**; *Country Roads*; and a note that Runa's "About [blank]" threads parody 2channel's military board.
- **Y2K:** real non-event, used accurately.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* Saving Fuchigami is the first time Runa spends foreknowledge on a *person* rather than a position. The book marks it as ambiguous — she cannot tell whether to be pleased she saved him or upset at how his power ended.
- *Strongly supported:* Her own observation that inflated stock prices are making the cleanup easier is the book acknowledging that **her advantage is decaying**. Every intervention makes her remembered future less predictive.
- *Plausible:* The shopping-district sequence is the volume's most honest failure. She has unlimited money and still cannot fix it, because the obstacle is people's homes. It is the clearest counterweight to the wish-fulfilment mode.
- *Strongly supported:* **"I'll fully admit I used you" is a gift of victim status.** Runa recognizes it: "he openly said he had used me. As such, I was allowed to be the victim." By then agreeing to back Izumikawa as kingmaker, she gives that protection up — and knows the subpoena risk is "back on the table."
- *Strongly supported across volumes:* Fuchigami's warning against "the same path as your grandfather" — the fixer's path — is the same charge Koizumi makes in Vol. 4 ("even the fixers of long ago came to face the public"). Two prime ministers, one warning.
- *Strongly supported:* Runa's narration offers a compact theory of the old LDP money politics — it worked while growth and a communist enemy kept everyone satisfied, and failed once the bubble burst — and names it as "the reason for Prime Minister Fuchigami and his faction's future decline."

**Connections:** Izumikawa's caretaker premiership runs into Ch. 6. Keika Railway becomes central in Ch. 8. Kagawa/Sougou completes the Vol. 1 Teisei arc. Hayashi — Mori's counterpart — becomes prime minister after Izumikawa, and his cabinet's fall opens Ch. 8.

## Chapter 6 — A Lady's Everyday Life: The Year 2000

**Narrative position:** 2000, under the Izumikawa caretaker government and after. Second slice-of-life chapter, with the volume's most direct character interrogation.

**What happens**
- Runa's female friend group has grown: Asuka, Hotaru, Mio, plus Takahashi Akiko (kendo, family dojo), Kurimori Shizuka (closest to an ordinary person), and Katsuki Shiori. She notes the split between those who call her "-san" and those who say "-sama," and that the latter are "probably my followers during the game. Not that any of them stayed after my downfall."
- Asked at lunch which of the three boys she likes, she deflects and tells them they're free to confess — because "with my family's status, they're the ones who will decide my love life." She notes **Takanashi Mizuho will join on a scholarship at high-school level** and denounce their way of living as "weird."
- A love letter falls from her shoe cupboard; she pockets it unopened, registering the three boys' tension.
- Yuujirou, now a former prime minister's son, is already in marriage negotiations; retired PMs are made counts.
- **At the Teia household**, Shuuichi discusses **hybrid automobiles** and opposes buying "Shiyo" outright. At breakfast, **Eiichi proposes marriage** — purely strategically: "Making an enemy of you wouldn't be smart… This'll be the best way to bring your answer in line with the Teia Group's interests." Runa sprays her grape juice and calls him an idiot; the older Teia men roar with laughter. Nothing is decided.
- **At Avanti, Mitsuya confronts her**: "Why are you trying to cram so much into your life, Keikain? You go full speed in everything… that's not what kids do. That's how adults live." Then: "Do you know what the three of us like least about you? … you're growing up far faster than us."
- She deflects with a line about girls maturing faster, and refuses to let him buy her coffee: "I have to be able to cover costs like this for myself."
- She lays out the **Shikoku Shinkansen** plan to him — a ¥400bn project discussed by two grade-schoolers in a café — admitting the line itself will barely break even, that the money is in extra Shin-Osaka platforms run as a **category 3 railway business**, and that the real objective is political: "The upcoming party leader election — I'm after the Shikoku vote."
- Festival scenes; a made-to-order yukata based on the Keikain crest (moon-and-flower), with an emerald hairpin.

**Revelations**
- Mizuho's entry point is fixed: a high-school scholarship student.
- Eiichi's proposal is strategic, not romantic — and Runa's reaction reveals she wanted it to be otherwise.
- The boys have collectively noticed she is not a child, and have decided it's the thing they most dislike.
- The Shinkansen is explicitly a vote-buying instrument.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** Mitsuya's book **Neko no Chikyūgi** (Mizuhito Akiyama, KADOKAWA); Runa's **Rakuen no Majo-tachi** (Satomi Kikawa, Shueisha); **category 3 railway business** correctly defined via the real Kobe Rapid Transit Railway; **Music Hour** by Porno Graffitti; the police "Director" rank made famous by Muroi in **Bayside Shakedown**; the **Cochabamba Water War** (with a genuinely even-handed note that "evil corporations" have support in the third world because "everyone is equal in the eyes of money"); **PMCs**; the 1999–2002 photo-booth boom; **Yubari**, noted as going bankrupt in 2007; Cyndi Lauper's *The Goonies 'r' Good Enough*; and — a nice callback to Vol. 1's Art Nouveau thread — Runa's outfit "based on the poster for **Gismonda** by Alphonse Mucha."
- **Real:** Toyota's hybrid development (the real Prius launched 1997) maps onto Teia's hybrid work.
- **Real:** the Shin-Osaka platform-capacity problem and the Sanyō Shinkansen are genuine.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* Mitsuya's confrontation is the book turning its own premise into a character flaw observed from outside. The three boys have correctly diagnosed her without knowing why.
- *Strongly supported:* Refusing to let Mitsuya pay for her coffee, when she owns a trillion yen, is the sharpest single illustration of her financial dissociation — small money is real to her; large money is not.
- *Plausible:* Eiichi's strategic proposal is the book stating its thesis about her world: even affection arrives as a merger term. Her irritation is the gap between what she is offered and what she wants.

**Connections:** Mizuho's arrival is set. The Shinkansen's political purpose pays off in Ch. 8. Eiichi's proposal recontextualizes Vol. 1 Ch. 1's engagement.

## Chapter 7 — Tachibana-san's Home

**Narrative position:** A short **POV-shift chapter** to Tachibana's apartment, structurally parallel to Vol. 1's Ch. 5 and Ch. 7. Contains the volume's darkest revelation.

**What happens**
- Tachibana, a widower, lives modestly near the Keikain residence, having quit smoking and drinking to serve Runa as his final act. She is "like a granddaughter to him."
- **Tachibana Yuka** — his granddaughter, and the maid from Vol. 1's 2008 flash-forward — asks when he decided Runa was worth serving.
- **His answer:** the study. Runa went in and out constantly, but there was never anything to tidy. A two- or three-year-old looking at books is unremarkable; one who *reshelves them correctly* is not. He checked what she read — history books and encyclopedias — and concluded she could read and "was hurrying to understand her situation." She has been caught since infancy.
- His background: born to a poor farming family in **Karafuto during the Manchurian War**; worked in the underworld; recruited by Keika Pharmaceuticals as **Hikomaro's bodyguard and informant**; became his butler when such work grew unnecessary. Kiyomaro introduced him to his wife, from a fallen samurai family; Yuka resembles her.
- He warns Yuka that Runa sometimes considers walking away and starting fresh, and that she must not be allowed to — too many are waiting to pounce. He has quietly thwarted plots beyond the kidnapping.
- He acknowledges the limits of his knowledge: he does not know that Japan, America, Russia and others are coordinating around her to the point that leaving would be suicidal.
- He assesses the structural problem plainly: he, Ichijou, and Toudou are her only real pawns; she is buying too many companies too fast and **they are losing control**. Runa "never cared much for her companies beyond whether they could make a profit," and so has little say over anything else. Hence recruiting Nakamaro as an outside director.
- Yuka asks who will protect Runa further afield. Tachibana smiles: "She will be here soon."
- **Angela Sullivan arrives.** She has an offer from a Wall Street investment bank as a fund manager; Tachibana has outbid it to make her Runa's secretary.
- **The final paragraph:** Tachibana's peaceful old age rests on his having *never fulfilled Keikain Hikomaro's final request*. "It was Tachibana who had pushed him to suicide over the scandal with the east and resulting cover-up, and it was only because he was working as Runa's butler, instead of taking her in as his own, that he was enjoying such a peaceful life now."

**Revelations**
- **Tachibana drove Runa's father to suicide.** The man raising her, whom she trusts most, killed her father in all but name. She does not know.
- Hikomaro's dying request was that Tachibana take Runa in as his own child — unfulfilled.
- Yuka is being trained as her bodyguard, which retroactively reframes her presence in Vol. 1's opening scene.
- Angela has been bought, answering her own Ch. 1 recommendation in reverse: the asset acquired the handler.
- Runa's empire is structurally out of control, and her own men know it.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** "Investment Bank Fund Manager: In other words, she would be in charge of vulture investing" — the job Angela turned down.
- Otherwise minimal; this is a character chapter.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* This is the volume's structural counterpart to Vol. 1's Ch. 5. Both take the warmest relationship in the book and reveal the guilt underneath. Nakamaro's warmth was a promise to a dying woman; Tachibana's devotion is **atonement for a killing**.
- *Strongly supported:* Tachibana's detection method — noticing that nothing needed tidying — is the best-constructed deduction in the series. The tell isn't precocity; it's *the absence of mess*.
- *Plausible:* The chapter quietly argues Runa has never been the hidden player she believes herself to be. She was identified at age two or three and has been managed ever since.
- *Speculative but well-supported:* Tachibana's guilt gives him a permanent motive to keep her from learning her father's history — which makes him a structurally unreliable source for everything the reader has been told about Otsumaro, including in Vol. 1 Ch. 2, where **Tachibana is the narrator of that entire backstory**.

**Connections:** Recontextualizes Vol. 1 Ch. 2 wholesale. Yuka's role explains Vol. 1 Ch. 1. Angela's recruitment answers Ch. 1 of this volume.

## Chapter 8 — Cassandra's Struggle

**Narrative position:** Late 2000 → April 2001 and beyond. The finale, closing on a date the reader recognizes before Runa finishes hearing it.

**What happens**
- **The Choufuu Council.** The Keika Group's managing-director committee convenes as the Iwazaki Group moves to absorb the original Keika companies. New members: Tachibana (Keika Railway), Echigo Engineering/Keika Parts, and Shiyo Electric as observer.
- **Iwazaki Yashirou**, president of the Imperial Iwazaki Bank and one of the group's three Dons, attends "as an observer" and asks Runa directly: bring all your companies and join us. She refuses, sweating, taking a long moment to find the courage: "There is still much I have left to do."
- The proposed new hierarchy: Keika-Iwazaki Pharma, then Keika Holdings, then Akamatsu. Nakamaro is offered outside directorships across her companies — deliberately, to show the main family they are not being cut out.
- Asked afterward what she has left to do, she cites her promise to Izumikawa and Fuchigami to finish the bad debts. Asked "Do you not trust us adults?", she suppresses an immediate no.
- **The US presidential election** resolves; Florida turns red. She sighs with relief in front of Nakamaro, "essentially admitting my involvement in the election."
- **The Hayashi cabinet collapses** across a sequence of real-modeled disasters: a Chief Cabinet Secretary's mistress-and-far-right-dinners scandal; the **Foundation for the Development of Small- to Medium-Sized Companies** scandal, which takes down Vice President Murashita and others; crushing gubernatorial defeats in Chiba (March) and Akita (April); the **submarine–training-ship collision**, handled single-handedly by Izumikawa as Minister of Disaster Management, exposing Hayashi's paralysis; and finally Secretary-General Nonaka withdrawing support.
- **The Fair Trade Commission blocks her.** Her bids for a bankrupt Sachii and for Taiei's convenience-store division both fail. The Antimonopoly Act — an Allied postwar imposition that in this timeline never got to finish its job, since the zaibatsu survived — is described as the zaibatsu's oldest enemy, and heavily influenced by foreign firms favoring deregulation.
- **The Afghanistan report.** Relief supplies routed Japan → Pakistan → India are being diverted to the Afghan government, which is secretly requesting eastern-bloc arms, ammunition, tanks, and combat helicopters from Akamatsu. The capital isn't all Pakistani. Runa has Angela pass it to Washington — "Make sure it's clear what it means that this information ended up with me too." The US is astounded; an ambassadorial apology over the submarine collision follows.
- **The leadership election.** Hayashi leaves two traps: prefectural member votes worth 147 of a total close to a third of Diet votes, and **prefectural and Diet votes counted on different days**, spread across a week — which allows momentum shifts and mid-race withdrawals. Runa alone recognizes the trap: a candidate relying on Diet votes can be eliminated before they are cast — because prefectural landslides arrive first, and Diet members facing a July upper-house election desert the candidate the rank and file rejected. *See Part III §7.*
- Koizumi Souichirou runs on **postal privatization, structural reform, and dissolution of the zaibatsu**, with a slogan of destroying his own party; his speech attacks "the triangle prison of business, politics, and bureaucracy." Runa cannot back him and knows he will win. Deputy PM Izumikawa warns a "conspicuous, newer zaibatsu" like hers makes an easy scapegoat. *See Part II §7 and Part III §8.*
- She backs **Minister Aso** instead — publicly on the grounds that he is a dietman from a noble family and the **Privy Council** (suspended, never repealed) is the only mechanism that could check Koizumi as prime minister. The text flags this as a cover story: it was "too much of a pain… to explain why I was making such a risky bet, so I came up with an alternative reason." Aso is Izumikawa's estranged former faction-mate and a possible successor to his faction. The Izumikawa Faction's announcement causes "political pandemonium" — a public break with the mainstream Hashizume faction, for a candidate Runa admits cannot win. *See Part III §9.*
- **The result.** Koizumi's speeches sweep the prefectural votes: he wins Karafuto and Chishima on day one, takes Aso's home prefecture of Fukuoka, and by day three the early favorite Hashizume is reported to be considering withdrawal. Koizumi clinches on 23 April. Runa loses Hokkaido and Tokushima, "prefectures I previously considered my power base" — Tokushima being in Shikoku, where she built a Shinkansen to win the Shikoku vote.
- Koizumi's cabinet breaks precedent (ministers chosen without faction lists, several women, outsiders) and keeps Izumikawa as Deputy PM, reassigned to crisis management and the Northern Karafuto Crisis. Runa talks Izumikawa out of resigning: she will need "a direct line to the Minister for Crisis Management come fall." Izumikawa warns her that this man "will be unlike any other leader the party has seen."
- At Aso's headquarters in the Shinjuku Keika Hotel, she works the phones herself and arranges voting partitions at the Tokyo convention — "an attempt at meddling with the voting system… If we still won a race like this, I'd know justice was on our side."
- The chilling detail: **the Koizumi campaign hasn't bought a member register or printed pamphlets.** He doesn't need the machinery. Runa feels "a murky dread."
- **Minister Takenaga's televised case** for market-value accounting and the unwinding of cross-shareholdings, naming Teia Motor Co. and Keika Holdings directly, and questioning Shinkansen spending. Runa identifies it as "a request from Wall Street, a plan by a flock of vulture funds."
- **Koizumi's counterstrike:** he offers Ichijou a private-member seat on the **Council on Economic and Fiscal Policy** — and has already secured Teia Shuuichi. Runa recognizes it instantly as a move to take her only capable pawn. She accepts anyway, to exert influence from inside, and asks Nakamaro to become a full Keika Holdings board member.
- **The final beat.** Nakamaro mentions he'll have to cancel a New York trip — a pharmaceutical trade fair. She asks the date. "I believe it was **September 11**. Yes, it's a real shame…" Her closing thought: "Dear God, is it really the fate of every villainess to be beset by this much despair and misfortune? And if I tried to save my cousin, meaning I couldn't be a villainess, then… what was I?"
- **Closing newspaper extracts:** Koizumi's cabinet formed with Yamaguchi as vice president and a Katou-faction minister; the Hashizume Faction reduced to minor status; Izumikawa demoted to Minister for Crisis Management. Karafuto census chaos (population possibly 20–25 million; ethnic composition ~50% Japanese, 30% Slavic, 20% Chinese; former Northern citizens treated as second-class). Karafuto Heavy Industries profiting by selling and maintaining eastern weapons to the third world. A **gated-community/mega-float "special ward"** proposal for Karafuto migrant workers, criticized as a new Dejima — raised by Takenaga, developed by Ichijou. A memorial for the **second February 26 Incident** thirty-four years earlier, in which literary figures called for a Shōwa Restoration and the overthrow of nobility and zaibatsu, and a battalion occupied central Tokyo and murdered people linked to those classes. The noble and zaibatsu classes now lobby for the right to bear arms, with PMCs contracted for a walled island's security. Takenaga and Ichijou clash over public-works cuts; Ichijou offers to fund the Shinkansen from Keika Railway's own money; Koizumi mediates.

**Revelations**
- Runa has intervened in a US presidential election.
- Koizumi can beat her without buying a single voter register — a form of power her money cannot address.
- The Privy Council is her only structural check on him, which is why a noble-family minister matters.
- Her arms-trading exposure is real, via Akamatsu, and she converts it into diplomatic leverage rather than refusing it.
- Nakamaro is scheduled to be in New York on 11 September 2001.
- A **second February 26 Incident** occurred ~1967 in this timeline — an anti-nobility, anti-zaibatsu military coup that killed members of both classes. The class Runa belongs to has a living memory of being hunted.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the **KSD scandal** (real, and it really did bring down senior figures); the **Ehime Maru Incident** — the real February 2001 collision between the USS Greeneville and a Japanese training ship — described as "the final nail in the contemporary administration's coffin"; the Gulf War commander and Chairman of the Joint Chiefs (Schwarzkopf and Powell, the latter then the new Secretary of State); the homeless-veteran problem via *Rambo*; Bungei Shunjū's real "Nagatachō and Kasumigaseki Confidential" column; special deregulation zones; and the **Council on Economic and Fiscal Policy**, correctly described as chaired by the prime minister.
- **Real, very high confidence: the 2001 LDP leadership election.** Koizumi Jun'ichirō really did win in April 2001 on the strength of an unprecedented prefectural-vote surge that overwhelmed the Diet-vote machine, really did run on postal privatization and structural reform, really did campaign against his own party, and was really nicknamed the "Lion King" for his mane of hair. **Minister Aso** is left with a real surname — Tarō Asō, Yoshida Shigeru's grandson, really did run in 2001, as Minister of State for Economic and Fiscal Policy, exactly the post the novel gives him. The mapping of the whole race is unusually direct.
- **Verified 2001 figures.** Prefectural votes were expanded from one to three per prefecture for that race and awarded winner-take-all (the novel's 147 = 49 prefectures × 3, counting Karafuto and Chishima; the real total was 141). Hashimoto, the old guard's candidate, was expected to win until just before the primary. Koizumi took 123 of 141 prefectural votes (Hashimoto 15, Kamei 3, Asō 0); Kamei withdrew after a policy deal Koizumi later ignored; the Diet vote went 175–140–31; final tally **298–155–31**. Fear of losing the July upper-house election drove the members' revolt.
- **Divergence:** Yuujirou projects the Izumikawa-backed Aso at up to 15 regional and 70–80 Diet votes — roughly triple the real Asō's 31. The novel doesn't report the final count.
- **Takenaga Nobutame = Takenaka Heizō. Very high confidence.** Koizumi's real Minister of State for Economic and Fiscal Policy and the architect of his financial reforms; Takenaka → Takenaga.
- **Hayashi = Mori.** Confirmed by the Ch. 5 name inversion and by the plot: the Ehime Maru mishandling, the KSD scandal, Nonaka withdrawing support, and Koizumi being "like brothers" with him (the real Koizumi and Mori shared a faction).
- **Real:** the **Buddhas of Bamiyan** destruction (March 2001); the **1993 World Trade Center bombing**; the January 2001 **Gujarat earthquake** in western India; Pakistan's real humanitarian offer to India; the 2000 US election's Florida contest; Y2K.
- **Real, used accurately:** the **Antimonopoly Act** as an Allied postwar imposition and the **Fair Trade Commission**'s role; market-value accounting and cross-shareholding unwinding as genuine late-1990s Japanese reform debates with genuine foreign-investor backing.
- **Fictional:** the "second February 26 Incident" (the real one was the 1936 coup attempt, with no sequel); the Karafuto mega-float ward; Karafuto Heavy Industries.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* The title is the thesis. **Cassandra** was cursed to see the future truly and be believed by no one. Runa's problem in this volume is no longer knowledge — it's that knowledge has stopped being sufficient. She knows Koizumi will win and backs his opponent anyway; she knows what September 11 means and cannot say it.
- *Strongly supported:* The volume's final question — "if I tried to save my cousin, meaning I couldn't be a villainess, then… what was I?" — is the book making your interpretive question explicit and refusing to answer it. The villainess frame is now load-bearing identity, and saving people threatens it.
- *Plausible:* Koizumi is constructed as her true structural opposite. She is a zaibatsu heiress whose power runs through cross-shareholdings, opacity, and patient capital; his platform is dissolving zaibatsu, market-value accounting, and quarterly returns. The conflict is ideological, not personal.
- *Strongly supported:* **Koizumi's "triangle prison of business, politics, and bureaucracy" is where Runa lives** — zaibatsu heiress (business), kingmaker (politics), bank rescuer living on central-bank loans and ministry guidance (bureaucracy). His three promises are one attack on all three corners.
- *Strongly supported:* The Koizumi campaign not buying a member register is the volume's sharpest image of the new politics: his power comes from outside the locked room where Runa's money buys influence.
- *Speculative:* The real Asō became prime minister on 24 September 2008 — nine days after Lehman collapsed, the date Vol. 1 opens on. Backing him in 2001 may be an early investment in the man who leads Japan through the crash. *(The dates are fact; the motive is a guess.)*
- *Plausible:* The second February 26 Incident is a significant late-volume worldbuilding move. It retroactively explains the nobility's siege mentality and the "impunity to arrest" privilege from Vol. 1 Ch. 4, and makes the PMC/gated-city thread a logical extension rather than dystopian flourish.

# Part II — Extended Economic Analysis

Volume 2's financial material is different in kind from Volume 1's. Volume 1 was about **resolution** — how you clean up an insolvent bank. Volume 2 is about the three problems that come *after* resolution: how you keep a rescued system lending, how you consolidate an over-banked industry, and what accounting rules you measure everything by. It is a less dramatic set of questions and a more advanced one.

## §1. The SME credit crunch, and why the whiteboard matters

The best sequence in the volume is Ichijou's toolkit for rescuing small and medium enterprises, and it is worth taking seriously because it is a genuinely accurate account of a phenomenon economists call a **credit crunch**.

The setup: with ¥60 trillion of public funds committed and banks racing to clean balance sheets before merging, banks become reluctant to lend and aggressive about calling in loans. Runa's metaphor is exact: "If money was blood, large companies would be the major organs and small-to-medium firms would be the capillaries." Banks starve the capillaries to keep the organs alive. This is real — during Japan's crunch, credit contraction fell disproportionately on smaller borrowers with no access to bond markets.

Ichijou's diagnosis is the part worth dwelling on: "what made many of those companies past saving was the fact that their managing directors didn't understand accounting." Not insolvency — **illiquidity plus incompetence**. And since Keika Bank is overstaffed from its mergers with people who *do* understand accounting, the two problems solve each other.

His requested budget items are, in order:

| Item | Problem it solves |
|---|---|
| **Two-month wall calendar** | Firms planning one month ahead discover a payment due at month-start and rush to the bank too late. A two-month board eliminates the blind spot structurally. |
| **Cleaning tools** | Ichijou cleaned the branch bathroom himself; Naoyuki copied him. Signals the relationship is service, not supervision. |
| **Snacks and tea sets** (~¥1,000/week) | In many small firms the managing director's wife or girlfriend controls the books. Getting accurate figures requires getting her cooperation. |
| **50cc scooters and helmets** (~¥100,000 each) | Commercial districts lack parking and have roads too narrow for cars; directors defer payment chores because they're a hassle. Bank staff absorb the errand. |
| **Cheap PCs** (<¥100,000, spreadsheet-capable) | Restructuring headquarters in several towns, to merge or combine firms and strengthen their negotiating and cash-flow positions. |

Three observations a lecturer would make.

**First, the scale inversion is the joke and the point.** Thousands of ¥100,000 mopeds "tally up to hundreds of millions," and the PCs push the bill into the hundreds of billions. Runa's reaction: "the bad debts we were dealing with were in the trillions. Compared to that, hundreds of billions strangely felt like nothing at all." She then notes that "Ichijou's excellent presentation skills might have influenced my feelings" — the book flagging **anchoring bias** in its own protagonist, accurately.

**Second, this is relationship banking done properly.** Japan's *main bank* system is a real and much-studied institution: a lead bank holds equity, seconds staff, monitors closely, and intervenes in distress. Ichijou is describing the micro-mechanics of exactly that, at a level of granularity most fiction about finance never attempts. The whiteboard is not whimsy; it is a real observation that small-firm failure is often a **working-capital timing problem**, not a profitability problem.

**Third, note who supplies the insight.** Ichijou is a regional banker. Runa's foreknowledge is useless here — she cannot tell you which specific machine shop in Hakodate will miss a payment. This is the volume quietly demonstrating that her edge does not scale downward.

## §2. Mark-to-market: the volume's real economic conflict

The **accounting big bang** is the spine of the volume, and the book explains it correctly.

Under book-value accounting, an asset sits on the balance sheet at purchase price. Unrealized gains and losses are invisible until realized. Under **market-value (fair-value) accounting**, assets are marked to current price each period.

The novel states the asymmetry plainly: "That was fine if the value was on the upswing, but if it was trending down, you would constantly need to cover your losses — and that was why Japan's bad debts seemed never-ending after the change."

This is genuinely the crux of Japan's lost decade, and the book takes a position on it. Japanese banks held enormous **cross-shareholdings** in their borrowers, purchased decades earlier at low prices. Under book value, the embedded unrealized gains functioned as a hidden capital buffer — which is exactly what Runa exploits in Ch. 5 when she notes that "stock prices were higher than they 'should' have been — thus enabling the use of unrealized gains to deal with bad debts worth several hundred billion, or even trillions."

Mark-to-market destroys that mechanism twice over. It converts the hidden buffer into reported volatility, and it forces the unwinding of cross-shareholdings — which means mass share sales, which depresses prices, which erodes bank capital further. The feedback loop is real and the book describes it precisely: "its own shares fell in value as a result, leaving the bank needing to recalculate its losses. If that bank's credit then grew riskier, it became more difficult for it to borrow, which in turn hindered its companies' economic activities and lowered their stock value, devolving into a spiral of loss." That is a textbook account of **procyclicality** — the same mechanism blamed for amplifying the 2008 crisis, which is presumably why the Ch. 2 glossary links the reform directly to Enron and Lehman.

Takenaga's televised case for reform is given fairly, and is the case real reformers made: cross-shareholdings force Teia to buy expensive captive parts when cheaper imports exist; untangle it and Teia could become the world's largest carmaker. Runa cannot rebut it. She reframes it instead — "a request from Wall Street, a plan by a flock of vulture funds to release Japanese shares into the market so they could feast on the carcasses."

**Both are true, and the book knows it.** This is the most intellectually honest thing in either volume: the reform is genuinely efficiency-enhancing *and* genuinely serves foreign buyers, and the protagonist opposes it partly on principle and substantially because she is a zaibatsu.

## §3. Consolidation: why four mergers, and why in that order

The megabank wave is reported almost as real history, with one explanatory passage worth extracting. Ichijou's arithmetic:

> A firm borrowing ¥10bn typically borrows ¥3bn each from city banks A, B, and C, with ¥1bn elsewhere. Merge A, B, and C and the resulting institution holds a single ¥9bn exposure to that borrower.

This is the **loan concentration** problem, and it cuts both ways: "In a healthy economy, the bank would fund them with huge profits, but in our current struggling economy, it would instead bury that institution under a mountain of bad debt in one fell swoop." But — and this is the reason the mergers happened — a merged bank large enough to absorb the concentration can also **restructure the borrower unilaterally**, without coordinating three creditors. Consolidation converts a collective-action problem into a management decision.

The real sequence the book reproduces (Mizuho, Sumitomo Mitsui, UFJ, Resona) was driven by exactly this logic plus the newly legalized **bank holding company**, which the Ch. 1 glossary correctly identifies as enabling "clever tricks such as using a smaller subsidiary bank to swallow up a larger one" — the reverse merger from Vol. 1.

There is also a sharp observation about pre-merger balance-sheet cosmetics: Sougou was CBJ's biggest bad-debt source, and "there is gossip within the financial world that the intent was to smarten itself up before the merge." Hizen is described the same way — "set to be sacrificed to hurry along the settlement of DK Bank's bad debts before the three-way merger." Merger announcements create a deadline that forces loss recognition. That is a real and well-documented dynamic.

## §4. The infrastructure pivot, and Runa's first strategically coherent move

Volume 1's Runa bought distressed financial institutions. Volume 2's Runa buys **railways, logistics, and retail**, and the shift is explained rather than asserted.

The stated reason is defensive. Keika Pharmaceuticals and Keika Chemicals are going to Iwazaki. Keika Holdings will be listed. She therefore needs "a new business core for our companies focused on Keika Hotels and Keika Railway, away from the 'suspicious' Moonlight Fund, before that happened. And there was nothing like a railway to build confidence."

That is a genuine insight about legitimacy as an asset. A fund is suspect; a railway is a civic institution. Buying KYOSHO and **lowering fares** converts a financial identity into a public one.

The economics of the railway purchases are also sound:
- KYOSHO's problem is bubble-era **high-interest debt**, not operations. Paying it off at a stroke converts a loss-making company into a profitable one without touching the business — a straightforward **balance-sheet recapitalization**.
- The Kagawa Railroad case is the most elegant: Kagawa **guarantees Sougou's loans**, so pressing Sougou's creditors would collapse Kagawa. Guarantee chains transmit distress sideways in ways a single-entity analysis misses. Both must be rescued together — the same lesson as Vol. 1's circular Far Eastern Group debt, at regional scale.
- The Shikoku Shinkansen is explicitly *not* justified on returns. It will "only just be in the black"; the money is in Shin-Osaka platforms operated as a **category 3 railway business** (owning infrastructure that another operator's trains run on — a real, accurately described Japanese arrangement). The line's actual purpose is the **Shikoku vote** in a party leadership election.
- Buying a **general contractor in rehabilitation** so that construction spending returns to her own companies is classic **vertical integration** to recapture margin.

## §5. What the volume gets right, and where it cheats

**Right:**
- The credit-crunch mechanics and main-bank micro-practice are unusually well observed.
- The mark-to-market debate is presented with both sides intact, and the procyclicality argument is correct.
- Guarantee chains, loan concentration, and pre-merger loss recognition are all real dynamics handled accurately.
- The **Fair Trade Commission** finally blocking her is an important corrective: the book introduces a constraint money genuinely cannot buy, and she simply loses twice.
- The Antimonopoly Act's presence is well-reasoned alternate history — in reality it accompanied zaibatsu dissolution; here the zaibatsu survived, so the law became a permanent guerrilla war instead.

**Cheats and strains:**
- **Scale drift.** Runa ends Vol. 1 with ¥800bn spent; by Ch. 2 of Vol. 2 the Fund exceeds ¥1 trillion with ¥300bn in shell companies, and she is contemplating a ¥2 trillion Shinkansen. The dot-com run-up explains part of it, but the book increasingly treats her balance sheet as unbounded.
- **The trust-bank arrangement is legally thin.** A private account in a subsidiary's Sapporo branch, staffed by a loyalist specifically to hide it from the controlling family, is not robust against a determined shareholder action. The book's answer — the family lacks the courage — is political, not legal.
- **Political causation is too clean.** One gubernatorial election ends a Diet scandal outright. In reality, scandals with prosecutorial interest survive electoral cycles.
- **The Sougou outcome is optimistic.** The real Sogo's ¥639bn waiver plan collapsed precisely because public opinion rejected using public money to save a retailer. Here the equivalent goes through.
- **Foreknowledge is decaying but the plot doesn't fully price it in.** Runa notes her interventions have raised asset prices above remembered levels — yet she continues to treat her memory as reliable for 2001 political outcomes. That tension is acknowledged in Ch. 5 and then mostly set aside.

## §6. Market-value accounting, cross-shareholdings, and the end of the zaibatsu — the full mechanism

**What cross-shareholding is.** Companies in a group hold blocks of each other's shares, with the group bank at the centre holding shares in its borrowers while they hold shares in it. Nobody sells. The shares work as mutual pledges rather than investments: stable owners who back management, block hostile bids, and keep business inside the group. The practice insulated Japanese managers from pressure to maximize returns, and by late 2001 cross-holdings were estimated at perhaps 40% of all Japanese shareholdings. In real history the Occupation tried to break up the prewar combines, and cross-shareholding is how their successors re-knit themselves. In the novel, where the zaibatsu were never dissolved, it is each group's literal skeleton.

**Why book value made it work.** Under historical cost, shares bought decades ago sit on the books at the price paid. Rising markets piled up vast unrealized gains — invisible but real — that acted as a hidden reserve. Banks could count part of them toward regulatory capital, and bank shareholdings were huge: almost twice core Tier 1 capital in 1999. Runa exploits this reserve in Ch. 5, using "unrealized gains" to absorb bad debts "worth several hundred billion, or even trillions." Book value also let losses hide.

**What market value changes.** Mark-to-market makes listed companies carry securities — including the cross-holdings that bind groups together — at current prices, exposing profits to stock-market swings; under Japan's rules the changes flow into shareholders' equity. For a bank holding more stock than its core capital, every fall in the Nikkei eats capital in real time. The rational response is to sell — so a system that only works if nobody sells now gives everyone a reason to sell.

**The spiral, and the missing buyer.** Takenaga describes it on television: a bank's losses lower its share price, its credit worsens, borrowing tightens, its clients suffer, their shares fall — "a spiral of loss." Runa's reply is the crux: it would help "as long as there were buyers for the mass of released shares." In reality, foreign investors absorbed much of the unwound stock, and when they pulled back, Tokyo prices weakened. In March 2001 major banks still held shares worth 1.5× their Tier 1 capital, implying about ¥10 trillion in sales; a 2001 law capped holdings at Tier 1 capital, and the state became the buyer of last resort — the Banks' Shareholdings Purchase Corporation from early 2002, then the Bank of Japan buying shares directly from banks from autumn 2002. By March 2008 major banks' equity portfolios had fallen from roughly 150% of Tier 1 capital to about 50%.

**From unwinding to dissolution.** The bank stops being the anchor (research finds the 2001 limit weakened main banks' role in keiretsu and pushed non-financial firms to unwind as well). Stable owners become ordinary sellers, so management answers to the market and the group loses its takeover shield — Runa's "flock of vulture funds" can buy what's released. Transparency ends mutual rescue: once each member's losses are visible every quarter, strong members stop quietly carrying weak ones, and the group's internal convoy breaks. A presidents' council of companies that no longer own each other has no teeth.

**Make or break.** For a strong group (Iwazaki), the upheaval is a chance to absorb weaker ones; for a young group (Keika), it is existential — and Vol. 2's Iwazaki merger threat unfolds in exactly this climate. Takenaga's example points to "Ayukawa Motors" — Nissan, whose Renault-era revival plan sold off almost all its cross-holdings. In this timeline, accounting rules finish what the Occupation never did. The novel follows through: Vol. 5's news chapter has Futaki-Yodoyabashi's cross-holdings ruled antitrust violations and Iwazaki dissolving its own parent company.

*Sources:* RIETI discussion papers on the unwinding of cross-shareholding (Miyajima & Kuroki, 05-E-006); Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, "Japan's Cross-Shareholding Legacy" (2009); Financial Services Agency FAQ on the Banks' Shareholdings Purchase Corporation; *GlobalCapital*, "Japanese Accounting Changes" (Nov 2001); Columbia Business School, "The Japanese Economy, Banking Industry and Securities Markets"; *Journal of International Accounting* study of the 2001 bank shareholding limitation act (ScienceDirect).

## §7. Postal privatization — why it threatens Runa *(inference)*

The novel never spells out the postal mechanism; it says only that Runa "could not back him" and that Keika is an easy scapegoat. What follows is inference from her holdings and the real economics.

**The pipe that funded public works.** Postal savings and life-insurance money was historically placed with the Fiscal Investment and Loan Program, which lent to public corporations and local governments for public works; in 2001 that program totaled ¥418 trillion, about 82% of GDP. Many LDP members feared privatization because their machines relied on rural postmasters to get out the vote and on postal assets to fund popular public works. Runa's empire leans on that world — Keika Railway's Shinkansen projects, the general contractor she rescued so construction money would flow back to her companies, her Shikoku and Hokkaido strategies. Vol. 3 confirms the direction: Takenaga puts her Shinkansen on the chopping block.

**A giant competitor.** The postal system was the world's largest financial institution, with savings about three times those of Japan's largest private deposit-holder and roughly a quarter of individuals' total assets — about ¥330 trillion, around 65% of GDP. Privatized, it becomes a commercial giant competing for the same savers and borrowers as Keika Bank and Keika Trust Bank, especially in Hokkaido and rural areas, and its "remobilized" assets flood the very markets Runa believes Wall Street wants to feed on.

**Her political currency loses value.** Runa's power works in the locked room, where "those with the money hold the most sway." The postmasters were among the old factions' most reliable vote machines, and postal privatization was Koizumi's weapon against that room. The Koizumi campaign "hasn't bought a register" — his power comes from outside it.

**What's ahead.** In August 2005 the upper house rejected the privatization bills 125–108; Koizumi dissolved the lower house, won the 11 September 2005 election decisively, and the law passed that October, splitting Japan Post into four companies under a holding company. That fight lies ahead in the series' timeline. *[How the novel uses it: speculative.]*

*Sources:* Porges & Leong, "The Privatization of Japan Post" (2006); Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco Economic Letter, "Postal Savings in Japan and Mortgage Markets in the U.S." (2006); Reason Foundation, *Annual Privatization Report 2005*; *Al Jazeera*, "Koizumi calls for poll after bills defeat" (9 Aug 2005); Wikipedia, "Japan Post Holdings."

# Part III — Extended Analysis: The Politics of Volume 2

## §1. Ichijou's hiring veto (Ch. 2)

After the leak, Runa asks Ichijou to send in one of his own men. He refuses instantly: "Showing favoritism to those formerly of Far Eastern Bank will pique the jealousy of the other staff, and those under Katsura Naoyuki currently have their hands full with the small and medium businesses. It will do more to help our employees settle if we take on former politicians from the Ministry of Finance who have been cut off." ("Politicians" is almost certainly the translation's word for bureaucrats.)

**Factional logic.** Keika Bank is stitched together from Far Eastern Bank, Hokkaido Kaitaku, the LPCB, Nihon Credit and the old securities firms, and the leak is a symptom of those tribes fighting for control. Far Eastern is the smallest component — a regional bank now running a megabank. Planting its man would confirm the others' fear of colonization and breed more leaks. Vol. 4's banker memoir shows the problem never fully disappeared: a Hokkaido faction later pushed former Kaitaku executives against Ichijou.

**Capacity.** Naoyuki's people are carrying the small-business rescue, the volume's most important banking work.

**Neutral outsiders — amakudari turned inside out.** Ex-ministry officials belong to no internal tribe, owe their jobs to Keika's leadership, know regulatory procedure from the inside, and carry natural authority over staff from banks they once supervised. After the 1998 bribery scandal, many officials were punished and their usual landing spots dried up. Normally *amakudari* gives the ministry a channel into the banks; here the ministry has cast these men off, and Keika picks them up — the channel now runs Keika's way. *[Plausible — my reading of a one-line rationale.]*

**Character.** Ichijou refuses to build a personal faction here, imports outside talent and stages a succession race in Vol. 4, and is deliberately kept "pure" by the other adults in Ch. 3. The hunt for the traitor goes to Tachibana: Ichijou builds institutions, Tachibana does the dirty work.

## §2. Nakamaro's unsworn testimony and the guardianship (Ch. 2)

**The pressure.** The "dark link" allegation targets Izumikawa's ally Yanagitani, and the opposition is considering summoning Izumikawa himself. Its weapon is the upper house, where the ruling party has no majority — "A censure in the House could be used to fell any councilor."

**The bargain.** A sworn summons is a public ordeal under oath with perjury liability; an unsworn reference witness takes no oath. Offering the unsworn version is the standard compromise — Nakamaro's "deal with the House of Councilors."

**Why Nakamaro.** Runa is a minor; Kiyomaro is too senior to put in the dock; Tachibana and Ichijou know the operation too well. Nakamaro is senior enough to be credible and knows relatively little, so he can testify truthfully without revealing much. *[The last point is inference.]*

**The cost.** Runa: "But then the public will see you as a criminal when you haven't done anything wrong!" Nakamaro: "Nevertheless, somebody must step up to the role, and you, Runa, are still a child. Allow me to do what a good brother should." In Ch. 5 Runa notes the testimony "exposed him to the media." It is the promise he made her dying mother in Vol. 1, paid in reputation.

**The guardianship.** Announcing Kiyomaro as guardian of "Keikain Runa, owner of the Moonlight Fund" answers the media's question with a duke's face, gives the Diet an accountable adult, and positions the family as protector rather than predator. It also plants the seed of Vol. 3, where formal adoption gives Kiyomaro legal authority over the Fund — the lever the Iwazaki Corporation tries to pull.

## §3. "It doesn't take a knife to silence a politician. All it takes is an electoral victory." (Ch. 2)

**The targets** are the scandal's drivers: the opposition and the ruling party's "fringe groups," quietly backed by a rival zaibatsu.

**Why "a knife."** Japan's political memory includes Prime Minister Hara Takashi stabbed at Tokyo Station (1921) and Socialist leader Asanuma Inejirō stabbed on a televised stage (1960); in the novel's world, Runa's grandfather investigated an unsolved prime minister's assassination, and the second February 26 Incident killed nobles. Runa explicitly chooses the ballot over the blade. *[The echo is my reading.]*

**Why an election silences.** Scandals feed on attention and claims about "the will of the people"; resignations and defeats only invite more digging. An electoral victory supplies a counter-verdict — Japanese politics even calls an election a *misogi*, a ritual purification. Tokyo is the strongest verdict available: ten million residents and every major media headquarters. A win would "quiet the opposition and give us the advantage in those alliance negotiations" — the coalition-building that ended the real LDP's upper-house minority in 1999. And winners owe you: Iwasawa later arranges police protection, and his brother's production company warns the media off her.

**The paradox.** Fuchigami calls it "the most difficult method you could choose." And *how* Runa wins — persuade the official candidate to withdraw on a promise of post-election endorsement, then back a surprise candidate — is pure kingmaking from behind the curtain. The line rejects her grandfather's violence while reproducing his fixer's method, which is exactly what Fuchigami warns her about in Ch. 5. *[Strongly supported.]*

## §4. "His failure was in thinking she was a mere prop." (Ch. 3)

Katou, Izumikawa's own lieutenant, had been angling to seize the faction in the name of "generational change." In the Tokyo race he negotiated behind the scenes for a replacement candidate and lost face, while Izumikawa, backing Iwasawa, came out on top. Katou treated Runa as the public did — "a tragic little girl whose name had been stolen for the 'scheme'" — a *mikoshi*, Japanese politics' word for the portable shrine of a figurehead carried by others (a famous quip attributed to Ozawa Ichirō holds that the best mikoshi is light and empty-headed). Izumikawa's admission — "I might have too, if I hadn't met her in person" — is the volume's recurring point: the only defense against underestimating Runa is meeting her. In Ch. 5 Runa guesses Katou never contacted her as quiet retaliation against the Keikain house over her father; he dealt with the *family*, never with *her*. *[Plausible.]* The faction dynamics echo the real Kōchikai, where Katō Kōichi pushed his seniors aside in the name of generational change — though Izumikawa himself is a composite. *[Plausible echo.]*

## §5. "I'll fully admit I used you. But I didn't want you to be subpoenaed." (Ch. 5)

**How he used her:** Keika Bank as the model to coax the megabanks into merging; the rescues he requested, including the Hizen hypermarket and the small-business program; the Tokyo election win.

**How he protected her:** Nakamaro's unsworn appearance was the price of keeping Runa or her guardian from a sworn summons. With Fuchigami retiring, Runa sees at once that the subpoena possibility "was back on the table now."

**Why the admission is a gift:** "he openly said he had used me. As such, I was allowed to be the victim." If anything comes out, the story is a prime minister's use of a child. By then agreeing to back Izumikawa as kingmaker, Runa gives that protection up — "I let my previous life — and my past in this current life — make the decision for me."

**The money politics:** "Your father and the prime minister who taught me both knew what they were getting into was dirty, and yet they couldn't pass up the money." The mentor is the Keiseikai founder — real Takeshita Noboru, felled by the 1989 Recruit scandal, whose own mentor Tanaka Kakuei fell to Lockheed. Runa's narration explains why the system worked (growth plus a communist enemy) and why it failed (the bubble burst) — "the reason for Prime Minister Fuchigami and his faction's future decline."

**The grandfather's path:** Hikomaro was a fixer. Fuchigami warns that kingmakers end up in the witness chair; Vol. 4's Koizumi says the same in harsher words. "I am passing on, as a politician" is poignant: the real Obuchi died in May 2000; the novel lets Fuchigami live but retire.

## §6. The caretaker strategy (Ch. 5)

**The locked room** is the real Gang of Five, mapped role for role (see the Ch. 5 entry).

**The betrayer** is the Ozawa figure: a former faction-mate who once brought the party down through a no-confidence vote, joined the coalition in 1999, and left it in April 2000.

**Runa's logic.** Izumikawa can't win a contested leadership race, and a leadership fight would expose the party's splits: "Should the opposition put out a motion of no confidence, you run the risk of fringe factions siding with them." If the cabinet then had to resign, the Diet would choose a new prime minister, and the opposition could combine with a ruling-party splinter to pick one — exactly what happened in 1993. Runa also knows that in November 2000, Katō and Yamasaki backed a failed no-confidence motion against Mori. So: a caretaker label, which lowers the stakes (no one needs to topple a government that has announced its own end), and a leadership contest held off until just before the general election, so the decisive fight is against the opposition, not inside the party. In practice the in-world press reports a late-June leadership vote ahead of a late-July general election — the same principle, slightly adjusted.

**Why Izumikawa accepts six months:** a former prime minister's rank (retired PMs become counts in this world), continuity on bad-debt policy, and patronage — "I'll give a ministerial seat to whoever is looking to boost their prestige." He then appoints Koizumi secretary-general as an olive branch to the factions Fuchigami froze out, and resigns his faction leadership, handing it to Katou — "generational change" granted at last.

## §7. The leadership election trap (Ch. 8)

The outgoing prime minister's two traps — winner-take-all prefectural votes and prefectural and Diet votes counted on different days — mean the prefectural results arrive first, over several days. A landslide makes Diet members, who face a July upper-house election, desert the candidate the rank and file rejected before they vote. Losers can drop out mid-race. Runa also notes that even if Hashizume won through Diet votes, Koizumi's camp wouldn't secede but would "fight tooth and nail" in the July election, which the party would then lose. The real 2001 race followed exactly this arc (figures in the Ch. 8 entry), and the novel tracks it: Hashizume is the early favorite and is reported considering withdrawal by the third day. Runa sees the trap because she remembers it.

## §8. Koizumi's platform (Ch. 8)

"Destroy the Fellowship of Constitutional Government," with three promises: postal privatization, structural reform, zaibatsu dissolution — the real Koizumi's "I will destroy the LDP" and reform without sacred cows, with zaibatsu dissolution swapped in because this world still has zaibatsu. His target, "the triangle prison of business, politics, and bureaucracy," is where Runa lives. The postal mechanism is analyzed in Part II §7; the accounting-and-cross-shareholding mechanism behind zaibatsu dissolution in Part II §6.

## §9. "Political pandemonium" (Ch. 8)

The headline — "Political PANDEMONIUM! Izumikawa Faction Announces Support for Minister Aso!" — probably renders the stock Japanese tabloid phrase for a political earthquake. It's a shock for three reasons: a public break with the mainstream Hashizume faction, which had enjoyed Izumikawa's favor; backing a candidate Runa admits "I failed to come up with a scenario where he can win," which the press assumes means "some sort of deal"; and splitting the old guard's vote just when Hashizume needs it. Runa's stated reason (the Privy Council) is explicitly a cover story; her positional reasons are that *how* they lose shapes Aso's and Izumikawa's futures, and that Aso could succeed to Izumikawa's faction — "killing two birds with one stone." *(For the 2008 speculation, see the Ch. 8 entry.)*

# Part V — On Your Question: What Is Runa, Actually?

You proposed that Runa's risk appetite might come from treating the world as half-fictional, or from anger at an era that discarded her, and that this is unresolved and deepening as she attaches to the world. Volume 2 does not settle it — but it supplies substantially more evidence than Volume 1, and it shifts the weight.

**Evidence for the "half-fiction" reading:**
- The financial dissociation is now explicit and authorially underlined: "Her Ladyship valued the few thousand yen in her wallet much more than the billion yen in her bankbook," anxious about manga and candy spending while a trillion sits in the Fund. In Ch. 6 she refuses to let Mitsuya buy her a coffee — "I have to be able to cover costs like this for myself." Small money is real; large money is abstract. That is precisely the psychology that permits ¥400bn Shinkansen bets discussed over cake.
- She delegates without curiosity: Tachibana notes "anything that would make money was given the go-ahead, and she had shown no interest in learning about the inner workings of trust operations." Ch. 7: "Runa never cared much for her companies beyond whether they could make a profit."
- She keeps invoking the frame at decision points — "I was a villainess; my job was to dole out punishment as my wicked heart pleased" before counterattacking the press; "I was born a villainess. I would lose to the protagonist — but nobody else" before the relay.

**Evidence for the "anger and deprivation" reading — and this is where Volume 2 adds most:**
- The opera scene is the strongest single data point in either volume. She weeps not at wealth but at applause: "I'd long since forgotten what praise was like in my previous life." Later, singing with a street violinist: "Mine was the high-spec body of a villainess: it allowed me to easily reach the peaks I was never able to climb before… This experience was blocked off to me in my previous life."
- Her guilt is real and stated: "I just feel guilty, like I'm cheating somehow." Narration: "That was because I was."
- The decisive line in Ch. 5: facing the choice to back Izumikawa, "I let my previous life — and my past in this current life — make the decision for me."
- Mitsuya's diagnosis from outside: "Why are you trying to cram so much into your life?" She answers about illness, and the narration adds only: "It was sweet. My previous life was not."

**What the volume changes:** the two readings stop being alternatives. The "villainess" frame is shown to be a **motivational device she consciously invokes**, not a belief that the world is unreal — she reaches for it at precisely the moments she needs permission to be ruthless or to refuse defeat. Meanwhile the attachment is unmistakable and accelerating: the Avanti standing reservation booked for every future December 22; the prayer, "even if I fall to my utter ruin, please don't let me forget the time I spent with them."

And then the volume closes by making your question the protagonist's own, in as many words. Told Nakamaro will be in New York on September 11, she thinks: *"if I tried to save my cousin, meaning I couldn't be a villainess, then… what was I?"*

That is the book stating the thesis outright. The villainess identity has become the scaffolding holding her together — a role that explains her ruthlessness, licenses her risk-taking, and promises that her eventual ruin is *meaningful* rather than merely sad. Saving people she loves threatens to dissolve it. She saved Fuchigami already; the frame survived because he was an instrument. Nakamaro is not.

**My assessment, labeled:** *Strongly supported* — the frame is a coping and licensing structure, not a metaphysical belief, and the attachment is real and growing. *Plausible* — the financial dissociation is a distinct mechanism from the frame, closer to the numbness of someone for whom money was once a source of pain, and it is what actually enables the "insane" bets. *Speculative* — the eventual downfall may be triggered by precisely the choice Ch. 8 poses: that she stops being a villainess, and the machinery around her, which needs her to be one, breaks.
