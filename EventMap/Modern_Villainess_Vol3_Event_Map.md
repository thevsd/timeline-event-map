# Modern Villainess Vol. 3 — Complete Event Map
### Chapter-by-chapter, with economic analysis and the 9/11 arc

**Scope:** Prologue + all 13 chapters, all glossaries, afterword, plus synthesis. Built from a full read of the EPUB (Tofuro Futsukaichi, illus. KEI; Seven Seas English ed.; Japanese original 2021).

**Coverage:** Spring to winter 2001, confirmed by the afterword.

**Structural note — a major format change.** Volume 3 abandons the single-protagonist structure almost entirely. The volume opens with a **2channel-style message board thread**, and then rotates through the POVs of a new maid/secretary, a police director, a trading-company employee, and a beat inspector, alongside Runa's own chapters. Two chapters are pure documents (recollection transcripts; an opinion column). The chapter count jumps from 8 to 13 while the word count stays similar — this is a mosaic volume.

**On the author's own admission:** the afterword states outright that he writes "the parts they want to first and works out the pacing of the overarching story later," doesn't write developments in order, and that his editor "buried their head in their hands once we reviewed my mistakes after publication." He explicitly invites readers who "sensed that something was off" to attribute it to this. That is worth carrying into any continuity reading of this volume.

# Part I — Chapter-by-Chapter Map

## Prologue — TV Live Watch pt. 2001

**Narrative position:** An anonymous message-board thread reacting live to a late-night TV drama, *Imperial Guard*, dramatizing a real incident from Runa's life. No narration.

**What happens**
- Posters note the drama's budget exceeds a prime-time production because "you've got the entire Keika Group backing you," and that Keika approached production directly, cutting out ad agencies.
- They recognize Runa playing herself, from the Teisei Department Stores posters.
- A recurring poster, **ID: lunakeikain** — evidently Runa herself, or someone very close — supplies insider commentary throughout.
- The "**Akihabara Daimyo's Maid Procession Incident**" is recounted: a noble girl tried to sneak into a maid café; her ex-CIA secretary responded with motorcycle outriders, sunglassed maids, and an attempt to rent an entire building rather than a shop, producing a street spectacle and a public argument with her secretary.
- The drama depicts a **gunfight and an inside betrayal** — bribed with stacks of ¥10 million in cash, laid down one at a time. The criminal organization turns out to be **Russian intelligence**.
- Posters explain the three-way jurisdictional deadlock: personal security handles kidnapping, the Public Safety Bureau claims it because the organization is Russian, and the **maid corps** blocks both by invoking noble privilege.
- The legal architecture is laid out: nobles retain **family laws** (trying their own members internally), **diplomatic immunity** inherited from the House of Peers, and the **Privy Council**. A maid acting as proxy for a duke's daughter can obstruct police interference. "It's like taking on a daimyo family, just without the land."
- The **second February 26 Incident** is recalled: triggered when an author called for "the fulfillment of the Showa Restoration," with the imperial police implicated in Anpo protest suppression after the Special Higher Police were broken up.
- The Keika–Iwazaki merger is reported as proceeding: Keika Pharmaceuticals → Keika-Iwazaki Pharmaceuticals; Keika Chemicals → Keika-Iwazaki Chemicals; Keika Corp and Keika Storage absorbed by Iwazaki Yusen and Iwazaki Logistics. What Iwazaki actually wants is **Keika Holdings, then Akamatsu Corporation** — the latter because it's a major transporter of Russian crude and Iwazaki is deep into Karafuto.

**Revelations**
- Runa now commands an armed private force and a maid corps with quasi-legal immunity.
- The nobility's legal privileges are far more extensive than previously shown, and were **designed by her grandfather** (see below).
- A Russian intelligence operation against her has already happened and been dramatized.

**Historical / worldbuilding references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** "Diplomatic immunity… When the House of Peers turned into the House of Councilors, diplomatic immunity was enacted to avoid chaos in the National Diet, as nobles wanted a way to have special privileges while escaping the responsibility of defeat. **Runa's grandfather is a real demon for coming up with that idea.**" The privilege that protected her father from arrest in Vol. 1 was Hikomaro's own invention.
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** "Privy Council… It may appear to have no real power, but its existence is what allowed the nobles to exist until today. Combined with diplomatic immunity, this allows nobles such as Runa's parents **to continuously commit crimes**."
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the second February 26 Incident is an acknowledged borrowing — "I laughed when I looked up '**Kerberos Saga**' and saw that it had its own version of 2/26." (Mamoru Oshii's Kerberos series, which does contain an alternate-history uprising.)
- **Real:** the Anpo protests; the real February 26, 1936 coup attempt as the template.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* Opening in an anonymous forum completes a progression — Vol. 1 opened inside Runa's head, Vol. 2 in a CIA file, Vol. 3 in public gossip. Each volume moves the camera further out.
- *Plausible:* The lunakeikain poster is a small, sharp joke: the most powerful person in the country participates in her own fandom anonymously.

## Chapter 1 — Ichijou Erika, Graduate of Professor Kanbe's Seminar, and Her New Job

**Narrative position:** POV of Ichijou Susumu's daughter. A deliberately ordinary entry point into a now-enormous organization.

**What happens**
- Erika, raised in Sakata, moved to Tokyo only after her father's promotion. She remembers him struggling to pay for their apartment; after Far Eastern Bank was absorbed, the family moved to a house and Susumu became the youngest board member of a Japanese megabank in history.
- He offers to arrange a job. It turns out to be **personal maid and secretary to Keikain Runa**. Erika resolves not to use the connection — then discovers every other employer only wants her because of her father.
- The Keika Group holds **no recruitment events**: it grew by buying distressed firms, so it always has surplus staff to redeploy. The few open positions go to people with connections.
- On a Teisei Department Store rooftop, Erika overhears a girl planning a rooftop koi pond and blurts out that it will get dirty. She explains that koi are omnivores that wreck a pond's ecosystem, and that stagnant water breeds mosquitoes — reducing Runa to horrified shrieking. She proposes a **biotope** instead: a hibachi above-ground basin, aquatic plants, Japanese rice fish.
- Runa, delighted, scouts her on the spot.
- The maid organization is described from inside: roughly **seventy maids** on four shifts, run by hotel maid trainer Nagamori Kaori. Fifty are Runa's personal maids — mostly **armed bodyguards from Kitakaba Security** — with a handful in hotel service and about twenty staffing **Café Vesuna** on the tower's ground floor. Some have Russian features; many have military experience, evident in how they squeak their shoes in unison.
- Saitou Keiko, Tokitou Aki, and Katsura Naomi have been promoted to head and assistant head maids.
- Erika is assigned the rooftop open-air garden of Kudanshita Keika Tower — vermiculite soil (one-tenth the weight of water), shallow-rooted trees, a deliberately shallow pond, all to manage structural load.
- Her actual job, she realizes, is to have tea with Runa, listen, answer back, and laugh. The garden becomes **"Erika's Garden,"** Runa's favorite place, open only to intimates.

**Revelations**
- The organization has become a closed system that cannot recruit normally.
- Runa's companionship is now a staffed function.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** hibachi basins in biotope construction; "Safety first" (*goanzen ni*) attributed to Sumitomo Metal Industries; and a wry note that "dressing in a nice suit and watch is a way of altering reality."

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* Erika is a control sample. She is the only person in the volume who meets Runa without knowing who she is, and she is hired for exactly that — she talked to Runa like a person.
- *Plausible:* The garden is the volume's answer to Vol. 2's Avanti café — a deliberately constructed small, human place inside an inhuman structure.

## Chapter 2 — Keikain Runa's Birthday

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV. Domestic-scale chapter establishing the new household.

**What happens**
- The household has grown from a handful to over ten live-in staff plus the wider corps. New arrivals include **Anisha Egorova**, whom Tachibana describes plainly as "more of an observer than a maid since she worked for an Eastern intelligence agency."
- **Angela Sullivan** and Anisha refuse to look at each other; Runa understands Angela's presence is precisely why Anisha came.

- Birthday material, household reorganization, and the beginnings of Runa's relationships with the new staff.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* Runa's home is now staffed by at least two rival intelligence services simultaneously, openly, with her knowledge, and the negotiated outcome is a seating arrangement. The book is making comedy of something genuinely sinister.

## Chapter 3 — Director Maefuji's Unsolved Case Files

**Narrative position:** POV of Maefuji Shouichi, now a Director — the Public Safety officer established in Vol. 1 as Runa's eventual arresting officer. Contains the volume's most important worldbuilding.

**What happens**
- A Russian intelligence officer negotiates with Maefuji, presenting photographs: a **Chechen sniper** and his target — Runa.
- To explain, he gives the full history of the **Karafuto Crisis**, which finally establishes the alternate timeline's origin in detail:
  - Karafuto was split after the Russo-Japanese War: south to Japan, north to Russia.
  - **Japan surrendered to the Allies in WWII, then declared war on the Axis powers**, preserving many national interests despite its own defeat.
  - The Soviet secretary general resented this. When Japan entered the **Manchurian War**, the USSR invaded Karafuto and forcibly established the **People's Democratic Republic of Northern Japan**.
  - The Manchurian War ended in Western victory, but the Soviets used the opportunity to invade southern (Japanese) Karafuto.
  - Near the war's end the Soviets tested a nuclear weapon. America defended Manchuria but **acknowledged the Northern Japanese government** — both to avoid all-out nuclear war and to penalize Japan for its WWII surrender. This is the Karafuto Crisis.

**Revelations — and an important correction**
- This substantially revises the picture from Vol. 1. Volume 1's narration (Runa reading picture books as a toddler) gave the impression that the Japanese home islands were partitioned. Volume 3 clarifies: **the partition was of Karafuto/Sakhalin**, not Honshu, and the mechanism was a Soviet invasion during the Manchurian War, not a WWII settlement. The "Allies lost at Normandy" element from Vol. 1 still stands as the war's altered course, but the surrender-then-switch-sides detail is new and explains why Japan kept its peerage, zaibatsu, and Privy Council.
- Runa is now a live assassination target for Chechen operators.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** real bad-debt figures are given with a cited source (Tameike Tsuushin, "Kanbei's Irregular Statements," 20 June 2001): sixteen major banks disclosed ¥18.0306 trillion as of March 2001; ~¥150 trillion in financial-institution funds for troubled companies; ¥63.935 trillion in other classified credits; approximately ¥100 trillion in bad debts eventually paid off. The book is citing real data to anchor its fiction.
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the **Kargil War** (real, 1999, with credible reporting that Pakistan prepared nuclear options); the **mujahideen** "who would later form the Taliban and al-Qaeda"; **Nepal**'s 2001 royal massacre as the trigger for the monarchy's fall; a US Deputy Secretary of State who "would later write a report with his name on it" (the Armitage Report).

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* Placing the timeline's founding explanation in Maefuji's chapter is deliberate. The man who will eventually arrest Runa is the one who explains why her world exists.

## Chapter 4 — The Last Days of the Den-en-chofu Manor

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV. The move from her childhood home to Kudanshita Keika Tower.

**What happens**
- Fifth grade brings club activities. Eiichi joins track and field, Yuujirou kendo, Mitsuya the "going-home club." Runa joins **kendo** — partly to avoid the attention track would bring, partly because "I was almost kidnapped once. I feel like I should learn enough to be able to defend myself."
- She is taught by her maid **Kitagumo Ryouko**, and finds **Northern Japanese kendo, with its Russian military influences**, fascinating.
- The farewell to the Den-en-chofu manor — the house from Volume 1, inherited from her father.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the **Chechen War** described as "the nightmare of the Soviet Union and Russia after their Afghanistan intervention," with extremist fighters returning from Afghanistan to Chechnya, "giving glimpses of the terrorist networks that would form in the future"; **British immigration** patterns from Pakistan and the resulting exposure to Islamic extremism; and *Golgo 13* (Saito Takao, Shogakukan).

**Deeper significance**
- *Plausible:* Leaving the manor severs the last physical link to her parents. The volume replaces it with a fortress.

## Chapter 5 — Okazaki Yuuichi's Job

**Narrative position:** Framed partly as a televised interview with Tachibana, partly as Okazaki's introduction.

**What happens**
- A broadcast interview with **Tachibana Ryuuji, managing director of Keika Railway**, covers the railway strategy: KYOSHO Rapid Railway bought "as a way of gaining some know-how," Kagawa Railroad acquired during the Sougou bailout, then the Shikoku Shinkansen, Shin-Osaka platform expansion, and **Shinjuku Shinkansen**. The original target was always Shin-Tokiwa Railway.
- The commentator presses: didn't you use massive political leverage for the Shinkansen? Tachibana's counter: "I don't deny that they say that, but railway work is a huge industry that eats up hundreds of billions of yen at a time. **And despite all that, we lost the presidential election.**" The room falls silent.
- The interviewer's central question: why is the Keika Group — known for the IT bubble, bad debts, and serial acquisitions — spending such incredible amounts on infrastructure?
- **Okazaki Yuuichi** is introduced: late twenties, from Akamatsu's resource management department, recommended by Toudou, and the man who "unified the Moonlight Fund and Akamatsu Corporation." He functions as Runa's messenger and resource buyer.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* Tachibana's "we lost the presidential election" is the book's answer to accusations of omnipotence. Vol. 2's Shinkansen was explicitly built to buy the Shikoku vote, and the vote was lost anyway.

## Chapter 6 — Cassandra's Lament

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV. The volume's centre, and the payoff to Vol. 2's cliffhanger. Spring to 12 September 2001.

**What happens**
- Runa moves into **Kudanshita Keika Tower** and is walked through its defenses: a second basement connected to Kudanshita Subway Station and, through an unmarked iron door, to the **basement of the Kudanshita police station**; duplicate facilities on the roof "in case of a prolonged battle where one end of the building fell into enemy control"; the purchase of the Kyomei Bank building across the street for dormitories, also basement-connected for reinforcements.
- Security chief **Nakajima Atsushi** — former captain and spy-ring commander for Northern Japan, expert in counterterrorism — requests permission to carry weapons. Runa refuses: follow the law. The legal workaround is that weapons are stored under the police station, costing at least five minutes in an emergency.
- Her compromise is a **reinforced armored skeleton**: Northern Japanese army equipment for cold, blast and shrapnel protection and heavy-weapon operation, abandoned after the Imperial Police were disbanded following the second February 26 Incident, and revived internationally for CBRN protection after "the 1995 terrorist attacks by a new religious movement."
- Three escape plans are prepared, including a **Hind helicopter on permanent standby** at Tokyo Heliport, thirty minutes out.
- **Okazaki returns from overseas with a report: a northern alliance general's captive says "They've got a nuke."** Runa immediately orders it distributed through every available channel — CIA (Angela, Eva), former Eastern intelligence (Anisha, Kitagumo Ryouko), and Tachibana. "This was simply too risky to keep to myself."
- A months-long international mobilization follows. **A Russian citizen is arrested in Tokyo** buying precision bomb-making instruments — a refugee from the Soviet collapse who became a follower of Islamic extremists, radicalized and instructed over the internet from foreign addresses. The dirty-bomb plot is prevented, because hardened nuclear-waste security delayed his acquisition of material.
- The intelligence then worsens. There were fewer missing warheads than feared — **two 500-kiloton warheads**, not multi-warhead systems — but they had already been sold to fund an escape to Kazakhstan. The buyers were the **former Romanian secret police** ("the 'children' of the Romanian president at the time," dissolved after the revolution, many absorbed into organized crime). The warhead traveled Romania → Hungary → Czechoslovakia → Germany, moved by an officer disguised as Romani, then vanished on a sea route.
- Worst: the ultimate sponsors are identified as **Libya and Iraq**, who backed Soviet conservatives and the Romanian police to obtain a bomb. The US prepares an IAEA complaint. The Tokyo dirty bomb was a **diversion**; the real targets are the US and Europe.
- Angela's assessment: if a nuclear or dirty bomb detonates, damage is unpredictable, so "we have to think of these diversion attacks as **collateral damage**." Runa knows exactly what that means and says nothing about hijacked planes.
- **11 September 2001.** Extra police and guards puzzle ordinary Japanese, who go about their day. Runa has arranged, through the vice president and deputy prime minister, extensive **disaster preparedness training** under cover of an approaching typhoon.
- A northern alliance general survives a **suicide bombing** because Runa's warning got protection to him in time.
- **Okazaki confronts her privately.** His reasoning: "Your methods are too precise. Also, despite the threat of attacks, you've made no attempt to manage your assets. You're acting to prevent terrorism under the assumption that there will be losses." He notes the blank-sum check she once gave him was a bet on the Russian president that "casts shadows in the opposite direction," and that no elementary schooler could have engineered the House of Councilors election outcome that forced approval of the training.
- Runa offers the Bamiyan Buddhas and the Gujarat earthquake relief as her cover story. Okazaki: "Let's go with that story, then. I don't believe in the supernatural. However, don't forget that the currently flustered United States has agencies that research the paranormal, and not just on TV shows."
- She offers him anything — Akamatsu, billions, "a bouquet of beautiful girls, excluding myself." He refuses all of it: "**Let me have a front row seat to see how you change history. I promise to pay for that ticket by working as your eyes and ears.**"
- Emergency call: a **freight train carrying nuclear waste has been hijacked in Colorado, heading for Denver**. The culprit is shot; the train is derailed. Runa's reaction is dread, not relief — Colorado is the Midwest, which means American attention is now pulled off the East Coast.
- The tower's completion party begins at 9 p.m., deliberately timed so political and business figures can share information if anything happens. The engagement of **Nakamaro and Asagiri Sakurako** is to be announced; Sakurako is the granddaughter of **Iwazaki Yashirou**, president of Iwazaki Bank, and their engagement had been in danger of cancellation over the Keikain succession dispute.
- Runa gives the opening remarks under a spotlight: "I had always longed to stand in this light in my past life, but now it was like the harsh rays of judgment." She darkly jokes to herself that a plane hitting the building now would end the country. *"Please don't let anything happen. I'm the only one who knows what's going to happen. I am, aren't I?"*
- Okazaki opens the door: something has happened in New York. The first tower is smoking. Initial reports say **ten planes hijacked**. The second plane hits. "I heard something snap inside of me." Her legs give out — and it is **Prime Minister Koizumi** who catches her: "Please rest well, and thank you for what you've done. What comes next is a job for adults."
- **12 September.** She wakes in the afternoon. Final count: ten hijackings reported, of which two hit the Twin Towers and two more hit **the Pentagon and the White House**. The president was already airborne on Air Force One and continued giving orders; the other six planes were false reports from the chaos. Tokyo and London markets are in freefall.
- Then the worst news: **ten years earlier an Israeli intelligence agency had raided a ship at sea and stolen one of the warheads**, was obliged to report it to the US, and the reports vanished into classified files across the 1992 and 2000 administration changes. The US is furious.
- She has a nightmare: Nakamaro dead, the Keikain family collapsed, the Keika Group dissolved or absorbed, only the dukedom left, and her dream-self at the funeral vowing to save the family "even if I have to sell my soul to the devil" — while the dreaming Runa screams at her not to take the devil's hand. She wakes mid-scream.
- Tachibana confirms Nakamaro is alive, at Keika Holdings' Kayabacho headquarters confirming the safety of all New York employees. Kiyomaro is at the Privy Council.
- **Tachibana Yuka** brings sandwiches in her grandfather's place; both their stomachs growl and Yuka asks for one too. Their late-night sandwiches become "our little secret and a memory we would never forget."
- Runa takes a week off for counseling, then returns to school, greeting her friends normally: "As the person who'd been unable to stop it, what right did I have to experience such normalcy?"
- Discussion of the coming Afghanistan campaign: the landlocked-country problem, Pakistan's army-intelligence links to Islamic extremism, the northern alliance's defensive war. Japan supports US deployments to Tajikistan and Uzbekistan; Koizumi prepares a JSDF dispatch; Japanese PMCs are held back at US request ("Please let us be the first ones to give those guys a good smack in the face").
- Akamatsu nonetheless supplies weapons: thirty **T-72 tanks** bought from the JSDF, sold on to Tajikistan, delivered across the border by PMCs; plus anti-tank rockets, ammunition, food and medical supplies, flown by chartered **An-124s**.
- The **attack on the Indian parliament** pushes India and Pakistan toward war, which would sideline Afghanistan entirely.
- **Eiichi notices her state.** "Runa, don't push yourself so hard… It's okay to cry." She realizes she forgot to. He closes the door so no one comes in, and she weeps. Afterward: "It's okay. Now I know you're a kid, just like the rest of us."

**Revelations**
- Runa's foreknowledge was insufficient. She mobilized everything she had and the attacks happened anyway.
- Okazaki has deduced the truth and chosen to trade his silence for a front-row seat.
- The nightmare is the clearest picture yet of the "original" timeline she is avoiding: Nakamaro dead, family collapsed, a devil's bargain.
- Her intervention **created new divergences**: the nuclear-terror mobilization, the prevented Tokyo dirty bomb, the surviving northern alliance general, the Colorado hijacking, and two additional aircraft striking the Pentagon and the White House.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the armored skeleton is **Kerberos Saga**; the **northern alliance** and the assassination of one of its leading generals in a coordinated attack (the real Ahmad Shah Massoud, killed 9 September 2001); the real **Thule and Palomares B-52 crashes** involving nuclear weapons; the **RT-23** rail-mobile ICBM; **Atomic Train** and **The Sum of All Fears** as the sources for the Denver and Baltimore scenarios; **Spirited Away** as "the newest movie by the master anime director"; **Tokyo DisneySea**; **The X-Files** as the paranormal research department; the landlocked-country overflight dilemma (in reality Iran and Pakistan "pretended not to notice the US Air Force"); the Pakistani military's dominance over its government and its intelligence bureau's simultaneous support of Islamic fundamentalism and nuclear development.
- **A dated and self-flagged alteration:** the glossary states that the attack on the Indian parliament "**in real life… occurred in December of the same year. It was after Afghanistan had been somewhat cleaned up, so the chronology was different, but in this story, the timing was perfect**." The author is telling you exactly what he moved and why.
- **Real:** the 1995 Aum Shinrikyo attacks; the real 9/11 attacks and the real fourth plane (United 93, which in reality crashed in Pennsylvania rather than hitting the White House — the novel's alteration); the real detail that President Bush was away from Washington and directed events from Air Force One.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* The title completes Vol. 2's. "Cassandra's Struggle" was the attempt; "Cassandra's Lament" is the failure. The curse was never disbelief — she was believed, mobilized nations, and it was still not enough.
- *Strongly supported:* The book chooses a specific, defensible position on its own premise: foreknowledge lets you **redirect** catastrophe, not cancel it. She stopped a nuclear detonation in Tokyo and the cost was two more aircraft hitting Washington.
- *Strongly supported:* Koizumi catching her is the single best-constructed image in the series so far. Her structural antagonist — the man whose platform is dissolving everything she owns — is the one who physically holds her up and tells her the adults will handle it.
- *Plausible:* Eiichi's "now I know you're a kid" completes the arc Mitsuya began in Vol. 2 ("you're growing up far faster than us"). The boys have spent two volumes trying to locate the child in her; this is the first time one of them finds her.

## Chapter 7 — Article Extracts / Terrorism Stories

**Narrative position:** A documentary chapter of first-person recollections, in the mode of Vol. 1's newspaper chapter.

**What happens**
- **A security firm member** (ex-Northern Japanese soldier) recalls being mobilized during a typhoon for ostensible disaster relief, then reassigned to city defense and authorized to arm by a Metropolitan Police inspector whose face had drained of blood. "When I was in the army, the elite officers at the barracks often looked the exact same way."
- **A traveler** at Haneda watches the departures board empty, spends the night watched by JSDF, police and guards, and takes the Shinkansen home.
- **A trader** recalls the fund's trading computer crashing and issuing **buy** orders during a mass sell-off; with the boss unconscious, they sold everything and ate the loss. "The computer was right. The boss laughed off the few hundred billion we lost that day." **General Energy Online collapsed after that.**
- **A travel writer** notes empty planes, packed trains, the acceleration of the Hachinohe Shinkansen, and new night trains — plus that AIRHO's cabin crew "consists entirely of ex-Northern Japanese female soldiers," trained for hijack resistance, likened to American sky marshals.
- **The photographer** from Vol. 2 recalls capturing "the moment she collapsed when she saw the airplane hit one of the Twin Towers, just as the prime minister reached out to catch her. **It's the best photograph I've ever taken in my career.**" He then asks the interviewer for ideas on how to photograph her nude.
- A news item: the **Offshore Town Act**, proposed by committee member Ichijou, accelerated after the attacks. A ten-thousand-person trial city at **Kisarazu**, built from several 300-ton deteriorated tankers with residential upper and infrastructure lower layers, ¥20 billion budget, aimed at the "second-class" Karafuto population priced out of city housing and currently crowding crime-prone boathouses.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* The trader's anecdote is the volume's sharpest financial irony. The machine was right and the humans were wrong, and the only reason they sold was that Runa was unconscious.
- *Strongly supported:* The photographer's entry is deliberately repellent. Placing his career-best photograph — of a child collapsing — beside his interest in photographing her nude is the book refusing to let the reader enjoy the image it just created.
- *Plausible:* The Offshore Town Act shows the pattern the whole volume traces: a security crisis converts a contested proposal into law.

## Chapter 9 — The Moonlight Fund's Intelligence Meeting

**Narrative position:** POV of Okazaki and the intelligence staff, without Runa.

**What happens**
- Fleet strategy: rather than replacing four ~14,000-ton tankers with two larger ones, they adopt the "**Let's use our aging boats carefully and forever**" plan. Angela: "Do it fast, then. Before the euro gains real power."
- The currency analysis: the dollar remains a hard currency because oil is priced in it. **Iraq, Libya and other anti-American states have declared they will deal in euros**, threatening that status. Okazaki jokes about the nuke they found; Angela only smiles. Iraq, seeking nuclear weapons, "would make for an easy enemy in the shadow of the terrorist attacks."
- The US contacts the PLO to prevent Palestine becoming a complication as it did in the Gulf War; Israel is forced into large concessions after the stolen-warhead revelation.
- **Anisha returns from Moscow.** The oligarchs who opposed the Russian president are now cozying up to him; the military is nearly consolidated.
- **The failed Russian coup is explained:** after a nuclear submarine sank in the Arctic, the defense minister was replaced, knew his generals would follow, and attempted a coup. Anti-president oligarchs and "the media mogul known as the puppet master in Russian politics" funded it. They were exposed; the generals were overthrown and the mogul fled. **The centerpiece they had wanted for the new administration was Keikain Runa.**
- The Russian request, relayed through Keika's channels because the Foreign Ministry is dysfunctional: *"Don't make your mistress the ruler of Russia or any neighboring country."* Runa is apparently called **"the final oligarch"** by the Russians.
- Okazaki and Angela agree to call Tachibana and "decide what we should and shouldn't tell the mistress."
- Angela spots the news: a reputable American financial magazine reports suspicion of accounting fraud at **General Energy Online**. Okazaki privately realizes Runa's earlier, unexplained order to cancel a joint California water deal with GEO means she knew. He says only: buy up all the crude you can — if there's war in Iraq, prices can only rise.

**Revelations**
- Runa was nearly installed as a figurehead ruler of Russia by a failed coup.
- Her own staff now filter information before it reaches her — the Vol. 2 pattern, escalated.
- She knew about Enron in advance and acted on it without explanation.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the **Kursk** submarine sinking (real, August 2000), with the author noting he read about it in *Golgo 13*; the Russian media mogul who "just happened to lose his position at this time" (Boris Berezovsky, who really did flee Russia in 2000–01); the **euro** entering circulation 1 January 2002 and the real conspiracy theory that the Iraq War was meant to protect the dollar; **Hokkai Brent** and **WTI** in crude futures; and the pointed observation that working backward from the January 2002 "**axis of evil**" speech, American leadership "must have been full of people who were already targeting Iraq immediately after the terrorist attacks," with the real invasion beginning 20 March 2003.
- **Real:** Iraq's actual 2000 switch to euro-denominated oil sales under the oil-for-food programme.

## Chapter 10 — The Lady's Field Trip: Elementary School Edition

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV. Deliberate tonal relief after the 9/11 arc.

**What happens**
- The Kyoto field trip was nearly cancelled on safety grounds after the attacks. Runa funds the entire thing personally to revive it — explicitly because "I was the main reason for the safety risk in the first place."
- They charter an entire eight-car **Shikoku Shinkansen** set for the Tokaido run. The party: 100 students, 10 teachers, **30 bodyguards, 10 maids, and 3 police** — 153 people total.
- They deliberately take reserved seating rather than first class, so everyone travels together.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* The ratio is the joke and the point — 110 children and teachers to 43 armed escorts. Runa buys back one ordinary childhood experience and can only do so by militarizing it.

## Chapter 11 — When the Lady Is Away...

He was helped by Director Maefuji, a drinking buddy.

**Deeper significance**

## Chapter 12 — The Rise and Fall of the Electricity Disruptor

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV. The volume's second major real-world arc, braided with a corporate-control crisis.

**What happens**
- **Big-boned policies.** The Council on Economic and Fiscal Policy reverses the traditional order: policy is decided first, budget built around it, stripping the Ministry of Finance of its leverage. Runa's Shinkansen lands on the chopping block and becomes a political controversy for Minister Takenaga.
- **The Iwazaki Motors recall.** A headline reports a secret recall. Runa lets slip a memory — a truck's tire flying off — and Eiichi corners her against a wall until she tells him everything. The real scandal involves doors and brakes; the truck-wheel failure is still in the future. Runa explains the mechanism: "they've been doubling their books and adjusting the data… since all the departments were most likely doing the same thing, the higher-ups don't understand that the data is inflated."
- The Koizumi administration intends to break Iwazaki Motors out of the Iwazaki zaibatsu and merge it with **Teia Motor Co.** as a showcase zaibatsu dismantling — without wrecking automotive employment.
- The financing problem is laid out with real texture: Teia's main bank is **Futaki Yodoyabashi**, but the former Yodoyabashi earned the nickname "**Cowardly Yodoyabashi**" for cutting off Teia's funds during a past crisis and chasing repayment; Teia has never forgotten. The next option, Gowa Osan Bank, is mid-purge. Teia can self-fund — it has saved so much it's nicknamed "**Teia Bank**" — but doing so would invite questions about whether it needs the Futaki zaibatsu at all, which Takenaga would treat as proof of his thesis.
- A summit at the **Wise Dragon King's Palace** in Meguro: Keika (Runa, Ichijou, Tachibana, Nakamaro), Teia (Eiichi and Shuuichi), Asagiri Kaoru, **President Iwazaki Yashirou**, Yuujirou and Mitsuya as outsiders, and Deputy Prime Minister Izumikawa as a third party. Izumikawa confirms with the MLIT that the data redundancies are real and no Iwazaki Motors data can be trusted.
- **Enron.** Okazaki and Toudou explain the oil trade's timing risk: about a month from Middle East loading to Japanese conversion, so a price dip below the buying price means loss — hence the practice of funding Hong Kong or Tokyo purchases with European oil-market proceeds. They identify **round-trip transactions**: "Multiple companies collude to repeat orders from each other and create fake sales," and suspect their purchased natural gas is being used in them.
- **General Energy Online** is the culprit: suspected accounting fraud, debt from failed foreign ventures shifted into **specified purpose companies (SPEs)**, sales inflated by round-tripping, a possible **tobashi scheme**, and a plunging stock price. Its Japanese vanguard is **Blackship Energy**, "legally a Japanese company." GEO built itself by repeatedly buying and selling gas from the marketplace and applying the same system to electricity, and is lobbying the US government to pressure the deregulation-committed Koizumi administration into opening Japan's energy market. Runa's read: they want "to become the dealer in Japan's energy market casino, and the dealer always has an edge."
- **The control crisis.** Tachibana, Ichijou and Naoyuki arrive together: Runa has been **officially adopted into the main Keikain family**, making Kiyomaro her legal guardian, and moves are underway to remove the Moonlight Fund from her control. Until now, Tachibana, Ichijou and Toudou acted "of their own judgment" on her instructions precisely because her orders had no legal force — which also meant liability stopped with them. Now Kiyomaro can direct the fund, and would carry the consequences.
- The instigator is not the Keikain family but **the Iwazaki zaibatsu's three pillars and the Iwazaki Corporation**.
- **Asagiri Kaoru** explains the Iwazaki structure: Iwazaki Honsha at the top as a non-operating holding company, with Iwazaki Heavy Industries, Iwazaki Corporation and Imperial Iwazaki Bank as the three pillars governing collegially — "the organized Iwazaki" — which produces factions. Heavy Industries owes Runa for the Iwazaki Motors bailout; the Bank owes her via Kaoru's grandfather. So Iwazaki Corporation could not act alone: "The Iwazaki Corporation is indirectly telling you, Runa-san, that they're discussing this now." Which means a **third party** is whispering to them.
- Toudou's read: Iwazaki wants **Akamatsu** for its Russian crude, given Iwazaki's Karafuto pipeline project. The Moonlight Fund holds Akamatsu's majority, and the Fund is Runa's — so the only route is through Kiyomaro's new guardianship. Asked whose orders he'd follow: "Legally, Kiyomaro-sama's. Emotionally, yours, my lady. But I have faith that you're not that foolish."
- **The Shirokane dinner.** Kiyomaro and Nakamaro invite her to move in. Runa tests them and finds, disarmingly, that they are sincere. Kiyomaro: "Seeing you collapse made me realize something, Runa. You're a child standing up against the world at such a young age." Asked why they didn't simply take everything: "**I don't want to be the kind of terrible father who steals his child's belongings.**" Nakamaro adds that he's seen friends whose relatives took their New Year's money and never returned it.
- **Why Kiyomaro sold the Keika Group to Iwazaki** — and it is not what Runa assumed. Not to dispose of bad debts (she did that herself). "The main reason was that it was better for us to leave the work up to other people… The bigger a business gets, and the more companies you buy up, the more people you need. And you need people you can trust to manage it all." Iwazaki has been assembling talent since the Meiji era; Kiyomaro judged the Keikain house couldn't staff a group that size.
- **Sakurako** arrives "just happening to be in the neighborhood" and hands Runa a large envelope from her grandfather, President Iwazaki: "It's gibberish to me, but you'll understand it, won't you?"

**Historical references**
- **General Energy Online = Enron. Very high confidence.** Real: Enron built itself on gas and then electricity trading, used round-trip transactions to inflate volumes, concealed debt in special purpose entities, was reported by a financial magazine in 2001, and collapsed in December 2001. It also lobbied heavily for electricity deregulation, including in Japan. "Blackship Energy" invokes the **Black Ships** of Perry's forced opening of Japan — a pointed name for an American energy company's Japanese vanguard.
- **Iwazaki Motors = Mitsubishi Motors. Very high confidence.** Real: the recall cover-up was exposed in 2000, involving decades of concealed defect reports and internal data manipulation; a separate 2002 incident in which a **truck wheel detached and killed a pedestrian** exposed further concealment. Runa's slip is foreknowledge of an event still a year away. The novel's Iwazaki Motors is likewise a member company of the Mitsubishi-coded zaibatsu.
- **Futaki Yodoyabashi Bank** = the Sumitomo Mitsui merger (Vol. 2's mapping). The "Cowardly Yodoyabashi" grudge echoes real friction in Japanese main-bank relationships during the crisis.
- **Real:** the **Council on Economic and Fiscal Policy** and Koizumi's "big-boned policies" (*honebuto no hōshin*), which genuinely did invert the policy-then-budget sequence and weaken MOF.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* The Shirokane dinner is a deliberate inversion of two volumes of expectation. The main Keikain family, set up since Vol. 1 as the cold house that discarded her father and isolated her, turns out to be simply decent — and Kiyomaro's reason for selling the group is not greed or fear but a sober assessment of managerial capacity, which is exactly the flaw Tachibana identified in Runa in Vol. 2 Ch. 7.
- *Strongly supported:* The Enron thread completes a pattern running through all three volumes: Vol. 1's *tobashi* at Ichiyama Securities, Vol. 2's mark-to-market fight, and now round-tripping and SPEs. The series is building a single argument about **accounting as the load-bearing structure of capitalism** — and the Vol. 2 glossary already told us where it ends, at Lehman.
- *Plausible:* Runa's loss of legal autonomy at exactly the moment she gains a family is the volume's cruelest structural joke.

## Chapter 13 — The Gentlemen's Pact

**Narrative position:** POV of the three boys. Closes the volume by showing them acting independently for the first time.

**What happens**

# Part II — Economic and Historical Analysis

## §1. The volume's thesis on foreknowledge

Volume 1 treated foreknowledge as a clean edge: know the future, buy the bottom. Volume 2 began complicating it — Runa noticed her own interventions had inflated asset prices above remembered levels. Volume 3 breaks it completely, and does so deliberately.

She spends months and enormous resources on prevention. She distributes the nuclear intelligence globally rather than trading on it. She arranges nationwide disaster-preparedness drills through political favors banked over two volumes. She warns a northern alliance general and saves his life. She stops a dirty bomb in Tokyo.

And the attacks happen anyway — **worse** than she remembered, because the mobilization she caused pulled attention toward nuclear scenarios and the Midwest, leaving two additional aircraft to reach the Pentagon and the White House.

This is a coherent and fairly sophisticated position: in a complex system, **intervention redistributes risk rather than removing it**. The book states it plainly through Angela — "we have to think of these diversion attacks as collateral damage" — and then makes Runa live with the arithmetic.

It also resolves the Cassandra metaphor properly. Vol. 2's title implied the classical curse of not being believed. Vol. 3 inverts it: she *was* believed, by the CIA, by Russian intelligence, by the Japanese government, by her own staff. The lament is that being believed was not sufficient.

## §2. Enron, and the series' running argument about accounting

The GEO material is technically accurate and fits a pattern the series has been building since Volume 1.

**Round-trip transactions:** "Multiple companies collude to repeat orders from each other and create fake sales." Real — Enron and other energy traders used simultaneous offsetting trades to inflate reported volumes and revenue without economic substance.

**Specified purpose companies:** debt from failed foreign ventures moved off balance sheet. Real — Enron's special purpose entities are the textbook case of off-balance-sheet concealment.

**The market-maker position:** Runa's read is the sharpest thing in the chapter. GEO's business model is to become "the dealer in Japan's energy market casino, and the dealer always has an edge in the game." That is a correct description of why Enron wanted deregulated markets — not to compete in them, but to intermediate them.

Set this beside the earlier volumes and a single argument emerges:

| Volume | Accounting mechanism | Real case |
|---|---|---|
| Vol. 1 | *Tobashi* — shuffling losses across reporting dates | Yamaichi Securities, ¥260bn |
| Vol. 2 | Book value vs. mark-to-market; cross-shareholdings as hidden capital | The real Japanese accounting big bang |
| Vol. 3 | Round-tripping; off-balance-sheet SPEs | Enron |

And Volume 2's glossary already named the endpoint: the reforms triggered by LTCB's collapse "**wasn't enough warning to prevent the collapses of Enron or Lehman Brothers Holdings Inc.**" The series is arguing that the 2008 crisis its first chapter opens on is the terminal case of a disease it has been diagnosing since 1997 — that what is *reported* is a different thing from what is *true*, and every crisis is a moment when the gap becomes unsurvivable.

## §3. The petrodollar thread

Chapter 9's currency discussion is the most geopolitically ambitious material in the series so far, and it is handled carefully.

The chain: the dollar is no longer convertible to gold, so its hard-currency status rests substantially on oil being priced in it. Iraq, Libya and others announce euro-denominated oil sales. The euro launches 1 January 2002 into the EU's single market. Dollar primacy is threatened. "They would need to beat a formidable enemy in order to regain that honor."

The book handles this responsibly. It does not assert the petrodollar theory of the Iraq War as fact; the glossary explicitly labels it "**a conspiracy theory about the Iraq War**." What the narrative asserts is only the verifiable part: Iraq really did switch to euro pricing in 2000, and Iraq really was invaded in March 2003. The causal link is left as something the characters believe, not something the book endorses.

The same care appears in the glossary's note on the "axis of evil" speech: working backward from January 2002, "the leadership of the American government must have been full of people who were already targeting Iraq immediately after the terrorist attacks." That is inference clearly labeled as inference.

## §4. The control problem, and why Kiyomaro's answer matters

Volume 2's Ch. 7 had Tachibana diagnose Runa's structural flaw: "he, Ichijou, and Toudou were the only real pawns Runa had… She was buying too many companies and expanding her businesses too fast, and they were losing control."

Volume 3 supplies the answer from the person best placed to give it. Kiyomaro sold the Keika Group to Iwazaki **not** to dump bad debts — Runa had already cleared those — but because "it was better for us to leave the work up to other people… The Iwazaki zaibatsu has been around since the Meiji era, and they now manage a great number of talented people."

This is a genuine and underappreciated point about conglomerates: the binding constraint on a group's size is not capital but **trustworthy managerial depth**. Runa has effectively infinite money and perhaps five people she can rely on. Iwazaki has a century of institutional talent formation. Kiyomaro concluded that a family which could not staff a group that size should not own one.

It also reframes the entire series. Runa has spent three volumes acquiring things faster than she can govern them, and the book has now had a sympathetic character say so to her face.

## §5. Where the volume strains

- **The nuclear-terrorism plot is a large invention** layered onto real events, and it does a lot of convenient work: it explains why her foreknowledge failed, it justifies the private army, and it supplies the Iraq setup. It is well constructed, but it is the volume's biggest departure from the documentary mode the series usually maintains.
- **Two extra aircraft striking Washington** is a significant and somber alteration. The book handles it soberly rather than sensationally, but it is an alteration to a real atrocity, and readers should know it is one.
- **The author's own afterword undercuts continuity claims.** He writes out of order, adds sections afterward, and his editor found mistakes post-publication. Apparent inconsistencies in this volume may be exactly that rather than deliberate ambiguity — which is a genuine limit on how much interpretive weight any single detail can bear.
- **The Karafuto Crisis explanation in Ch. 3 partly rewrites Vol. 1's account.** It is a clarification rather than a contradiction, but readers coming from Vol. 1 with the impression of a divided home islands will need to revise it.
- **The security apparatus has outrun plausibility.** Seventy maids, four rotating bodyguard units, a Hind on standby, a police-station tunnel, and powered armor is a considerable distance from the Vol. 1 household of four.

# Part III — Synthesis

## Alternate-history ledger

**Preserved:** 9/11 itself, the Twin Towers and Pentagon strikes, the president airborne and directing from Air Force One, the initial chaos of false hijacking reports, the NYSE halt, markets in freefall; the assassination of the northern alliance general on 9 September; the Bamiyan destruction; the Gujarat earthquake; the landlocked-Afghanistan overflight problem; Pakistan's military-intelligence duality; the Kursk sinking; the Russian media mogul's flight; Enron's round-tripping, SPEs, magazine exposure and collapse; the Mitsubishi Motors recall cover-up; Koizumi's big-boned policies; Yahoo! BB's free-modem campaign; the euro's launch.

**Altered:**
- **Two additional aircraft strike the Pentagon and the White House** (in reality the fourth plane crashed in Pennsylvania).
- **A nuclear-terrorism plot** is added as the attacks' strategic context, including a Tokyo dirty-bomb attempt, a Colorado nuclear-waste train hijacking, and two missing Soviet warheads routed through the former Romanian secret police.
- **The northern alliance general survives**, warned by Runa.
- **The Indian parliament attack is moved from December to September** — the glossary says so outright.
- **Israel's ten-year concealment of a stolen warhead** is fictional, and forces real diplomatic consequences in-story.

**Wholly fictional:** the Karafuto Crisis and Northern Japan; the second February 26 Incident; Kitakaba Security and the armed maid corps; the Offshore Town Act and the Kisarazu tanker city; the Russian coup that wanted Runa as figurehead.

## On the "what is Runa?" question

Volume 3 advances your thesis considerably, and in a direction that favors the deprivation reading over the half-fiction one.

The spotlight moment at the party is the key line: "I had always longed to stand in this light in my past life, but now it was like the harsh rays of judgment." The thing she wanted most in her previous life — recognition, an audience — has become the instrument of her guilt.

And the villainess frame is now visibly failing to hold. She still invokes it reflexively ("This was just something a villainess had to do" about the field trip), but it no longer licenses anything. She doesn't use it to justify ruthlessness; she uses it to justify **paying for a school trip**. Vol. 2 ended with her asking what she was if she stopped being a villainess. Vol. 3's answer, mostly unspoken, is that the frame has already shrunk to a verbal tic.

What replaces it is worse and more human: "As the person who'd been unable to stop it, what right did I have to experience such normalcy?" That is not a villainess's logic. It's survivor's guilt.

The single most important beat for your question is Eiichi telling her it's okay to cry, and her realizing she had **forgotten to**. She did not suppress grief; the category had stopped being available to her. His closing line — "now I know you're a kid, just like the rest of us" — is the first time in three volumes anyone successfully locates the child underneath.

*Strongly supported:* the frame is now residual rather than operative, and what actually drives her is unresolved grief from her previous life compounded by new guilt from this one.
*Plausible:* the financial dissociation persists structurally (she still can't govern what she buys), but the emotional dissociation has broken.
*Speculative:* the nightmare, in which the Keikain family collapses and she takes a devil's hand, may be the actual mechanism of her foretold downfall — and if so, it is the thing she saved Nakamaro to prevent.
