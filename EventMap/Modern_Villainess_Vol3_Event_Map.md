# Modern Villainess Vol. 3 — Complete Event Map (Third Edition)
### Chapter-by-chapter, with economic analysis, the 9/11 arc, and a background primer on Afghanistan, Pakistan and the "loose nukes" world

**Third-edition changes (8 October 2026).** This edition comes from a complete, end-to-end reread of the volume. The earlier editions rested on an uneven first pass, and the reread found real gaps — some of them in the places that carry the volume's argument. What changed:

1. **Chapter 12 — two "open questions" were already answered in the text.** Earlier editions stopped at Sakurako's envelope and listed its contents and the "third party" behind Iwazaki Corporation as unresolved. Both are resolved later in the same chapter: the envelope holds a plan to break up the Keika Group American-style, and the third party is **General Energy Online** (the novel's Enron), trying to cover its margin calls. The chapter's ending — the Moonlight Fund's ¥35 billion "wedding gift," Runa's refusal to save GEO, "I would rather eat before I get eaten" — was missing entirely. Rewritten in full.
2. **Chapter 12, first half.** The outcome of the Iwazaki Motors summit (Runa declines; **Ayukawa Motors** buys the company with Keika Bank's money; Koizumi takes the credit) and the **Kanbe–Takenaga phone call** are added.
3. **Chapter 1.** The Professor Kanbe seminar scene — the "economics of genius" and Runa's answer to it — was missing. It is the volume's thesis question. Restored.
4. **Chapter 2 rewritten.** It contains the *first* "What comes next is a job for adults" (months before 9/11), the sentence in which Runa names what will later be "connected to my downfall," the Gulf War commander who now calls her "Boss," and the trade that gets the Shinjuku Shinkansen built.
5. **Chapter 3 corrected.** No Russian officer briefs Maefuji. The Karafuto history is delivered in a *Golgo 13*-style hiring scene that may be the manga Maefuji is reading; the case itself is a would-be bomber killed on the night of Runa's birthday and a cover-up Angela arranges with the police. Earlier editions had also attached each chapter's glossary to the following chapter in this stretch; fixed.
6. **Chapters 4, 10 and 11 rewritten** from full reads: Angela's "you could become a monster" and her vanished husband; the Mount Takao prayer; the fox bride at Fushimi Inari and the game's last image of Runa at Kansai Airport; the sweep the adults run while she is away.
7. **Chapter 13.** The pact itself (the boys agree to earn their own money before confessing their feelings) and the fact that the company they shorted *was* GEO are added.
8. **The Chapter 7 trader.** Chapter 12 independently confirms that Runa's group took losses while she lay unconscious and was criticised for being "lost at sea." The Moonlight Fund reading is now *strongly supported, short of confirmed*; the text's "he" still stands against it.
9. **New analysis:** Part II §9 (one genius and a million prodigies), §10 ("a job for adults" — the curated child), §11 (naniwabushi against rank-and-yank). Part IV's tables are corrected to match.

**Revision after the full reads of Volumes 2, 4 and 5 (8 October 2026).** Four statements in this edition were overtaken by what the later (and earlier) volumes say. They are corrected in place and marked:

- **Chapter 8's column is this world's history, not "a road not taken."** Vol. 5 Ch. 5 has the narrator state as fact that Afghanistan became "a war of annihilation, in which chemical and biological weapons had been used and Indian mercenary soldiers sent in," and names the practice (the "Nagashima Doctrine"). The Ch. 8 entry, the ledger and the open-questions table are revised.
- **The Iraq pretext is not made "an in-world truth."** Vol. 4 Ch. 3 says the White House "already knew that Iraq had no such weapons" and built its case anyway, using the loose-warhead evidence Runa supplied. The wording in Ch. 6 and in the ledger is refined.
- **Necessary context added (9 October 2026):** this world never had Hiroshima or Nagasaki (Vol. 4 Ch. 11). Notes in Chs. 3 and 6 explain why that makes the nuclear plot and the Manchurian War's nuclear standoff central.
- **The adoption dates from June 2000** (Vol. 2 Ch. 5). Ch. 12 is where its legal consequence is stated and used.
- **Runa's preparations for an Iraq war start in February 2000** (Vol. 2 Ch. 5: the Saudi railway "along the southern border of Iraq… before the eventual Iraq war would wipe it out"), not in this volume. Chs. 2 and 6 should be read with that in mind.

**Coverage:** every chapter, glossary and the afterword has now been read in full.

**Second-edition changes (retained).** The Chapter 7 trader downgraded from "strongly supported" (see item 8 above for where it now stands); Chapter 8 recognised as a column about a "Kandahar Massacre" (then judged a road not taken — **reversed**; see the revision note above); the Chapter 9 ferries; Chapter 5 rewritten; the Russian president, Typhoon Danas, the Plumbat affair, Enron's real Japan venture and other identifications; Part II §6–§8 and the Part III primer.

**Scope:** Prologue + all 13 chapters, all glossaries, afterword, plus synthesis. Built from the EPUB (Tofuro Futsukaichi, illus. KEI; Seven Seas English ed.; Japanese original 2021).

**Coverage period:** Spring to winter 2001, confirmed by the afterword.

**A naming note.** Kasugano **Asuka** (Vols. 1, 2, 4, 5) is rendered "Asuna" in parts of this volume, sometimes within a single chapter; this map uses Asuka. Kazuki Shiori was "Katsuki" in Vol. 2. Nagamori Kaori also appears as "Kaoru."

**Structural note — a major format change.** Volume 3 abandons the single-protagonist structure almost entirely. It opens with a **2channel-style message board thread**, then rotates through the POVs of a new maid/secretary, a police director, a trading-company employee and a beat inspector, alongside Runa's own chapters. Two chapters are pure documents (recollection transcripts; an opinion column). The chapter count jumps from 8 to 13 while the word count stays similar — this is a mosaic volume, and the mosaic has a purpose: half of what matters now happens where Runa cannot see it (Part II §10).

**On the author's own admission:** the afterword states outright that he writes "the parts they want to first and works out the pacing of the overarching story later," doesn't write developments in order, and that his editor "buried their head in their hands once we reviewed my mistakes after publication." He explicitly invites readers who "sensed that something was off" to attribute it to this. That is worth carrying into any continuity reading of this volume.

# Part I — Chapter-by-Chapter Map

## Prologue — TV Live Watch pt. 2001

**Narrative position:** An anonymous message-board thread reacting live to a late-night TV drama, *Imperial Guard*, dramatizing a real incident from Runa's life. No narration.

**What happens**
- Posters note the drama's budget exceeds a prime-time production because "you've got the entire Keika Group backing you," and that Keika approached production directly, cutting out ad agencies.
- They recognize Runa playing herself, from the Teisei Department Stores posters.
- A recurring poster, **ID: lunakeikain** — evidently Runa herself, or someone very close — supplies insider commentary throughout.
- The "**Akihabara Daimyo's Maid Procession Incident**" is recounted: a noble girl tried to sneak into a maid café; her ex-CIA secretary responded with motorcycle outriders, sunglassed maids, and an attempt to rent an entire building rather than a shop, producing a street spectacle and a public argument with her secretary.
- The drama depicts a **gunfight and an inside betrayal** — bribed with stacks of ¥10 million in cash, laid down one at a time. The criminal organization turns out to be **Russian intelligence**.
- Posters explain the three-way jurisdictional deadlock: personal security handles kidnapping, the Public Safety Bureau claims it because the organization is Russian, and the **maid corps** blocks both by invoking noble privilege.
- The legal architecture is laid out: nobles retain **family laws** (trying their own members internally), **diplomatic immunity** inherited from the House of Peers, and the **Privy Council**. A maid acting as proxy for a duke's daughter can obstruct police interference. "It's like taking on a daimyo family, just without the land."
- The **second February 26 Incident** is recalled: triggered when an author called for "the fulfillment of the Showa Restoration," with the imperial police implicated in Anpo protest suppression after the Special Higher Police were broken up.
- The public already knows the outline of her wealth: "she's got the Romanov family treasure and is super rich too, with trillions in assets." Of the bribed insider, lunakeikain writes: "I respect commoners who don't fall for that."
- The Keika–Iwazaki merger is reported as proceeding: Keika Pharmaceuticals → Keika-Iwazaki Pharmaceuticals; Keika Chemicals → Keika-Iwazaki Chemicals; Keika Corp and Keika Storage absorbed by Iwazaki Yusen and Iwazaki Logistics. What Iwazaki actually wants is **Keika Holdings, then Akamatsu Corporation** — the latter because it's a major transporter of Russian crude and Iwazaki is deep into Karafuto. Asked why Iwazaki bothers with Keika's dull businesses, lunakeikain answers: "Hint: Political marriage."

**Revelations**
- Runa now commands an armed private force and a maid corps with quasi-legal immunity.
- The nobility's legal privileges are far more extensive than previously shown, and were **designed by her grandfather** (see below).
- A Russian intelligence operation against her has already happened and been dramatized.

**Historical / worldbuilding references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** "Diplomatic immunity… When the House of Peers turned into the House of Councilors, diplomatic immunity was enacted to avoid chaos in the National Diet, as nobles wanted a way to have special privileges while escaping the responsibility of defeat. **Runa's grandfather is a real demon for coming up with that idea.**" The privilege that protected her father from arrest in Vol. 1 was Hikomaro's own invention.
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** "Privy Council… It may appear to have no real power, but its existence is what allowed the nobles to exist until today. Combined with diplomatic immunity, this allows nobles such as Runa's parents **to continuously commit crimes**."
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the second February 26 Incident is an acknowledged borrowing — "I laughed when I looked up '**Kerberos Saga**' and saw that it had its own version of 2/26." (Mamoru Oshii's Kerberos series, which does contain an alternate-history uprising.)
- **Real:** the Anpo protests; the real February 26, 1936 coup attempt as the template.
- **Plausible (my identification, third edition):** "that author" who called for "the fulfillment of the Showa Restoration" is this world's **Mishima Yukio**, whose real attempt to rouse the Self-Defense Forces to a coup ended in his suicide on 25 November 1970. A poster adds that the revolt's slogans were "zaibatsu dissolution" and "breaking down the privileged classes" — the programme Koizumi pursues by legal means thirty years later.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* Opening in an anonymous forum completes a progression — Vol. 1 opened inside Runa's head, Vol. 2 in a CIA file, Vol. 3 in public gossip. Each volume moves the camera further out.
- *Plausible:* The lunakeikain poster is a small, sharp joke: the most powerful person in the country participates in her own fandom anonymously.
- *Strongly supported (third edition):* The thread is also a table of contents. Anonymous posters predict that Iwazaki really wants Keika Holdings and then Akamatsu — the plot of Chapter 12 — and name the second February 26 Incident that Chapter 11's inspector is still chasing.

## Chapter 1 — Ichijou Erika, Graduate of Professor Kanbe's Seminar, and Her New Job

**Narrative position:** Third-person on Ichijou Susumu's daughter, early 2001. A deliberately ordinary entry point into a now-enormous organization — and, through her professor, the first statement of the volume's thesis question. *(Third edition: the Kanbe seminar scene, missing from earlier editions, is restored.)*

**What happens**
- **Erika.** Born and raised in Sakata, Yamagata; her parents met at Far Eastern Bank. She joined her father in Tokyo for university and remembers him struggling with the apartment loan. After Keika absorbed the bank he became the youngest board member of a Japanese megabank in history. Their house was Runa's gift on his promotion to CEO: "I'll pay for this house as a work expense. Sorry it's just a secondhand one we obtained through a nonperforming loan!"
- **The job hunt.** Her father has "arranged a job" — personal maid and secretary to Keikain Runa. Erika resolves not to use the connection, then finds every other employer asking the same question: "Is your father CEO Ichijou of Keika Holdings?"
- The Keika Group holds **no recruitment events**: it grew by buying distressed firms, so it always has surplus staff to redeploy. The few open positions go to people with connections.
- **The rooftop.** On a Teisei Department Store roof Erika overhears a blonde girl — she takes her for a foreign tourist — planning a koi pond, and blurts out that it will get dirty: koi are omnivores that wreck a pond's ecosystem, and stagnant water breeds mosquitoes. Runa shrieks. Erika proposes a **biotope**: a hibachi basin, aquatic plants, Japanese rice fish.
- A limousine arrives at the Ichijou house days later. Runa bows to the unemployed student: "**I have a request. Please allow me to be ordinary through you.**"
- **Professor Kanbe's seminar.** Erika's economics professor at a private university is a TV commentator and a friend of **Minister Takenaga Nobutame**. His seminar gives attendance credit for a welcome party and a graduation party; the student organiser must rent a hotel hall, pay for it with donations and finish with a surplus. Excused absences require a watch worth over ¥100,000 and a tailor-made suit. His graduates get jobs.
- **His research is "the economics of genius."** "Until now, the eras of mass production and consumption have made prodigies more valuable than geniuses. But as that model declines, the more a single genius will be able to change the world." He backs Takenaga's strategy of "strengthening the strongest in society instead of bailing out the weak," and is known for the "radical theory that if a single genius emerged out of the sacrifice of a million prodigies, it would have a positive effect on the income and expenditures of the country."
- He tells Erika that "the model of the Japanese economy has been on the verge of change for the past few years due to a single genius." When she mentions that she is "the third person [Runa has] personally scouted, including my father," he works out that Runa recruited a bank executive when she had barely started school, and concludes that the "Her Little Majesty" rumours are true — he "had found the one genius of his research whom he believed was worth the sacrifice of a million others." His hands shake.
- **The party.** Erika, unable to read a room, simply asks her employer for a hotel. Runa discounts the Shinjuku Keika Hotel and the staff over-deliver. The narration explains why: "In the young girl's past life, finding a job had been so difficult that most students could hardly think of things like graduation parties… she was granting Erika an opportunity for something she'd missed out on herself."
- **Runa's speech to the seminar.** "The theory that one genius outweighs a million prodigies probably applies to a world I'll come to see with my own eyes, and there's part of it that I can't ignore… However, those million prodigies will never be without value. At the very least, I understand that the Keika Group wouldn't be able to function without those million prodigies… Take care of yourselves, and even if you end up as cogs in the wheels of companies and societies, you'll still have your determination… I believe that the Keika Group's mission is to work hard so that all of you can be accepted." Kanbe sees "a smile that he would never forget."
- **The maid corps from inside.** Roughly **seventy maids** on four shifts, run on site by hotel maid trainer Nagamori Kaori (27, married, with a two-year-old). About fifty are Runa's personal maids — mostly **armed bodyguards from Kitakaba Security** — with a few in hotel service and about twenty staffing **Café Vesuna** on the tower's ground floor, some recruited from Akihabara. Some have Russian features; many have military experience, evident in how their shoes squeak in unison. The morning meeting ends with "Safety first!"
- Saitou Keiko is now head maid; Tokitou Aki and Katsura Naomi are assistants. Erika is paired with Keiko partly as matchmaking for her plan to marry and retire. The more capable staff call her "**Miss Runa's Clown**."
- **The garden.** Erika is given the open-air garden on the roof of Kudanshita Keika Tower, over a hundred metres up — vermiculite soil (one-tenth the weight of water), shallow-rooted trees, a deliberately shallow pond, all to manage structural load. Her real job, she realises, is to have tea with Runa, listen, answer back, and laugh. The garden becomes "**Erika's Garden**," Runa's favourite place, open only to intimates.

**Revelations**
- The organization has become a closed system that cannot recruit normally.
- Runa's companionship is now a staffed function — and she asked for it in those words.
- The man who supplies the Koizumi government's intellectual framework has identified Runa as the proof of his theory.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** hibachi basins in biotope construction; "Safety first" (*goanzen ni*) attributed to Sumitomo Metal Industries; and a wry note that "dressing in a nice suit and watch is a way of altering reality."
- **Real background:** Runa's past-life job market is Japan's "employment ice age" — the cohort that graduated from the mid-1990s to the mid-2000s into a frozen hiring market.
- **Plausible (my identification):** "Kanbe" nods to "Kanbei," the pen name of economist and TV commentator Yoshizaki Tatsuhiko, whose *Tameike Tsuushin* column this volume's own glossary cites as its source for bad-debt figures (Ch. 2). The character's theory is the author's invention.
- **Speculative echo:** the theory resembles Samsung chairman Lee Kun-hee's "genius management" slogan of the early 2000s — "one genius will feed 100,000 people."

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The chapter states the volume's question.** Is an economy that sacrifices a million ordinary talents to produce one genius a good bargain? Kanbe and Takenaga say yes. Runa — the supposed genius — tells a room of graduates that the million are what make anything function and that her group exists so they can "be accepted." Chapter 12 is the payoff: Kanbe's phone call with Takenaga, and Runa's fury at a plan to sack half her employees (Part II §9).
- *Strongly supported:* Her answer is autobiographical. In her previous life she was one of the million — a graduate of the frozen job market. She is defending the people she used to be.
- *Strongly supported:* Erika is a control sample. She is the only person in the volume who meets Runa without knowing who she is, and she is hired for exactly that — she talked to Runa like a person. "Clown" is apt in the old sense: the court's licensed truth-teller.
- *Plausible:* The garden is the volume's answer to Vol. 2's Avanti café — a deliberately constructed small, human place inside an inhuman structure.

**Connections:** Kanbe returns in Ch. 12 and lectures at length in Vol. 4 Ch. 9. Runa's "cogs" speech is the positive statement of what she refuses in Ch. 12's restructuring plan. The request to "be ordinary through you" belongs with Koizumi's "you're a child" (Ch. 2) and the Mount Takao prayer (Ch. 4).

## Chapter 2 — Keikain Runa's Birthday

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV, spring 2001 (fifth grade). *(Rewritten in the third edition from a full read.)* It opens as a domestic interlude and turns into the volume's political keystone: it holds the first "job for adults," the one sentence in which Runa names the mechanism of her own downfall, and the trade that gets the Shinjuku Shinkansen built.

**What happens**

*The household*
- Over ten new servants have been hired. Saitou Keiko is head maid, with Tokitou Aki and Katsura Naomi as assistants. **Anisha Egorova** is, in Tachibana's words, "more of an observer than a maid since she worked for an Eastern intelligence agency"; **Angela Sullivan** (ex-CIA) is the reason she came, and the two will not look at each other.
- A clash over dining ("Masters and servants are supposed to eat separately!" / "So you want her to eat all her meals in total solitude?!") ends in a compromise: the original household eats with Runa; the two intelligence officers attend without eating. Erika, hired this spring, eats with her anyway.
- A new driver, **Watabe Shigema**, is a once-famous violinist found busking outside Teisei and scouted after Runa sang with him. The older drivers are on the main Keikain family's payroll.
- **Kudanshita Keika Tower** is a redevelopment of "the building that used to house the offices of a credit bank." It will be group headquarters, Moonlight Fund base and her home from the summer holiday.

*The private party at Avanti*
- The school cast is laid out in passing (table below).
- Gifts: a sunstone necklace (Kaoru); a mandarin-scented charm bag that Hotaru has "blessed" — the other two look away from her grin; a crane-game plush of a yellow electric mouse; a music box playing the "Waltz of the Flowers" (Lydia); a fountain pen "for autographs" (the boys).
- Runa tells the boys she will "go tit-for-tat" with the Council on Economic and Fiscal Policy and build a **Shinjuku Shinkansen** — the "Shinjuku Geofront Project," about ¥1 trillion. Tokyo Station is saturated by the northern lines; Governor Iwasawa supports it; the groundwork was laid under the previous cabinet and only the money is missing.
- Yuujirou, whose father is entangled with him, warns her about Takenaga: "That man can make everyone do all sorts of things. Be careful."
- Afterwards Tachibana and Angela give her an adult perfume: "Someday, you'll grow up to be the kind of young lady a fragrance like this is meant for. But until that day, we will stay here by your side." They pinky-promise. Runa: "How I wished that days like this would last forever, even if I knew such a wish would never come true."

*The formal party, Shinjuku Keika Hotel*
- The guests ignore the birthday girl and talk politics. **The Foreign Ministry secret-fund scandal**, in this world's version: money from the prime minister's office went to nobles serving as figurehead ambassadors, shielded by noble immunity. The public is furious.
- **The Deputy Prime Minister Installation Act** makes the post official with its own staff, removes its claim to succeed the prime minister, and makes the holder chairman of the Privy Council. Runa reads it as Koizumi using the scandal to attack "the zaibatsu and their close-knit noble families," get a handle on the Privy Council, and box in Izumikawa: "The prime minister was truly a political demon."
- **Bad debts.** Takenaga's next targets are Gowa Osan Bank and Honami Bank. In-world figures: ¥10.8 trillion that the banks have disclosed and are ready to write off; ¥38.4 trillion of "risk management claims (the scary stuff)"; ¥90 trillion of lending to troubled companies. The ¥28 trillion gap between the first two is what the Financial Services Agency will make the banks reserve against, with about ¥80 trillion of public money ready to nationalise those that break. Keika Holdings is exempt — already nationalised and cleaned. "I want to cry. But the economic situation is still better than it was in my past life."
- Runa enters in a Teisei princess dress (her request to wear her school uniform was refused) and sings; Watabe leads the orchestra.
- In uniform among the guests: **the Desert Hero** — this world's Gulf War commander — "alongside the CEO of a private military company at his public debut," with US and Japanese officers.
- **The US Deputy Secretary of State** has flown in "just for my party," with a birthday card from the president. In a private room (Tachibana, Angela and Eva, the serving CIA officer placed as a maid) he thanks her for the tip on Pakistani arms flows into Afghanistan (Vol. 2).
- She asks how strong Pakistan's military really is, and he tells her, off the record, about **Kargil**. "I was speechless. I didn't know any of that at all." Pakistan's army must keep arming the fighters it raised or they will not follow it; those fighters do not grasp nuclear deterrence; "any spark on the border could light the flames of nuclear war."
- She asks whether Washington talks only to Pakistan's government — not its army, not the insurgents. His silence is the answer. **"The Pakistani government was unable to control its military groups, which in turn could not regulate the insurgents they'd helped create. This difference in perception would later be connected to my downfall."**
- The US asks her to open a channel to the Pakistani side through Akamatsu's contacts. She agrees to send humanitarian goods only — no weapons — and to have Akamatsu's refusal of arms sales blamed on American pressure. She does not acknowledge his thanks: "My answer to him would come in autumn."
- **Nepal.** She notes that arms supplied to India are moving into the Himalayas. On China: "That will be decided by the Manchurian people" — Manchuria had its first-ever change of government in 2000, and the US has hardened since the Hainan collision.
- With Izumikawa, former prime minister Fuchigami, Governor Iwasawa and Kiyomaro ("my foster father") she proposes moving officials temporarily into NPOs that Akamatsu will send to Nepal as "overseas workers," because the scandal has frozen the secret funds: "This is what the secret funds are for in the first place, and yet we can't even use them."
- **The Desert Hero walks in: "Do you need something from me, Boss?"** He now runs her private military company. His first job: take trained men to Nepal for the Japanese government, unofficially, to gather intelligence and protect investigators of an upheaval in which the US and India may both be involved — "You don't have to harm the Stars and Stripes, but don't let them bring shame to it either." He will "gather up some guys I trust in Sakata" tomorrow.
- **Her price: "The Shinjuku Shinkansen."** Iwasawa supplies city approval, Fuchigami his hold over the general contractors, Izumikawa pressure on the cabinet before the upper-house election. Asked about joining America's anti-China course, Izumikawa says only: "Ask the prime minister."
- In the waiting room she complains to her friends that this "definitely isn't a job that an elementary school kid should be handling." Mitsuya: "Complain while you can."
- **Koizumi arrives unannounced** at the end of a party thrown by and for his opponents. Eiichi: "Don't be scared, Runa. We'll stare him down for you." The prime minister hands her a bouquet. When she tries to take him aside to report what the Americans said, he pats her head: **"There's nothing you need to concern yourself with. What comes next is a job for adults."**
- Runa: everyone else who knew what she was "tried to use the situation for their benefit, although sometimes they at least apologized for doing so. But Prime Minister Koizumi was the first person who saw what I was and scolded me for it. 'You're a child, so that's what you have to be.' Those words shot straight through me like a bullet." After he leaves she murmurs: "**But that's not possible for me…**" She has to shake off "the spell" by recalling "the horrible experiences from my past life that this 'actor' was set to bring about."
- **Aftermath.** Two US carriers enter the East China and Yellow Seas; the captured American pilot is released; Manchuria declares nothing; Koizumi's approval rises. In Nepal, her company's presence exposes sloppy covert work and the US and India pull out — India laid the trap and the US walked into it while trying to free its pilot. Angela smiles broadly.

**Revelations**
- Koizumi — her declared future opponent — is the first adult to refuse what she offers and tell her to be a child. She experiences it as being *seen*.
- Runa has a named blind spot: she did not know about Kargil. Her foreknowledge covers what an ordinary Japanese adult would have absorbed, no more.
- Her private military company is now led by an American four-star hero and is used by the Japanese state for deniable work.
- The Shinjuku Shinkansen exists because she traded a covert operation for it.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the *real* bad-debt figures, with a cited source (Tameike Tsuushin, "Kanbei's Irregular Statements," 20 June 2001): sixteen major banks disclosed ¥18.0306 trillion as of March 2001; ¥150 trillion of lending to troubled companies; "supposedly, they paid off around 100 trillion yen in bad debts." (One glossary line repeats the first figure with a 2009 date — apparently garbled.) Set beside the chapter's in-world numbers — ¥10.8 trillion disclosed, ¥90 trillion to troubled companies — the author is quantifying Runa's effect: her Japan carries roughly 40% less bad debt than ours did. Also glossed: the **Kargil War** ("Pakistan was preparing to use nuclear weapons"); the **mujahideen** "who would later form the Taliban and al-Qaeda"; **Nepal**'s royal killings that year as the trigger for the monarchy's fall; the Deputy Secretary of State who "would later write a report with his name on it" (**Richard Armitage**); and Runa's song, Arai Akino's "Campos Neutros."
- **Real, altered:** the **Hainan Island incident** (a US EP-3 and a Chinese fighter collided on 1 April 2001; the 24 American crew were held eleven days). The novel stretches the standoff into the Koizumi cabinet (formed 26 April), reduces the crew to a "pilot," and adds a Manchurian independence scare.
- **Real:** the 2001 Foreign Ministry secret-funds scandal; **Tanaka Makiko** as Japan's first female foreign minister (the novel's "first civilian and the first woman" — which implies that in this world the post had been a noble preserve); the Nepalese royal massacre of 1 June 2001.
- **Plausible (my identifications):** the Desert Hero is modelled on **Norman Schwarzkopf**; Manchuria's "first-ever change of government in 2000" maps onto Taiwan's first transfer of power that year; "Honami" is Mizuho and "Gowa Osan" is UFJ (Sanwa + Tokai), though Vol. 2 gave the latter's components different names; the "credit bank" building at Kudan matches the real Nippon Credit Bank, whose head office stood there.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The downfall sentence is the series' control problem stated as geopolitics.** A government that cannot control its army; an army that cannot control the fighters it created. Runa says this "difference in perception" — outsiders dealing with the top layer as if it commanded the rest — will be "connected to my downfall." She heads a three-layer structure of exactly this kind: herself, her lieutenants, and an armed corps recruited from a defunct state's soldiers (Part II §4, §10).
- *Strongly supported:* **"A job for adults" begins here, not on 9/11.** In Chapter 6 the line reads as tenderness. Its first use is a refusal: he will not take her briefing. The repetition is deliberate, and Vol. 4 Ch. 9 turns it into policy.
- *Strongly supported:* She cannot do what he asks. "But that's not possible for me" is not defiance; a few pages earlier she has dispatched mercenaries to the Himalayas because the state's own channels were frozen.
- *Plausible:* The perfume scene and the Koizumi scene are a pair. Her own people promise to stay "until" she grows up; her opponent tells her to be a child now. Both place her adulthood in the future, and she knows it is already here.
- *Plausible:* "Actor" is exact. Koizumi's politics is theatre (the author says so in Vol. 4's afterword), and Runa — a trained performer — recognises a better one.

**Connections:** The would-be bomber killed outside this party is Ch. 3. The put options bought "that same day" are Ch. 5. The Shinjuku Geofront becomes a terrorist target in Chs. 7 and 11. "Job for adults" returns in Ch. 6. "My answer… in autumn" points at 11 September, after which Akamatsu supplies the northern alliance with tanks as well as food (Chs. 6, 9). The Desert Hero's men gather in Sakata, Ichijou's home town and the site of Keika's chemical combine (Ch. 12).

## Chapter 3 — Director Maefuji's Unsolved Case Files

**Narrative position:** Third-person on **Maefuji Shouichi**, now a Director in the Metropolitan Police's Public Safety Bureau — the officer established in Vol. 1 as Runa's eventual arresting officer — and his subordinate, Inspector **Natsume Kentarou**. Set on the night of Runa's formal birthday party (Ch. 2). *(Corrected in the third edition: earlier editions wrongly had a Russian officer brief Maefuji.)*

**What happens**

*The cold open — a hiring scene*
- Pure dialogue, no names. A professional says, "Let's hear your conditions." A Russian client shows him two photographs: "a sniper from the commonwealth of Chechnya," and the sniper's target — a girl.
- The client explains at length why Russia wants her alive (below), asks him to "eliminate the sniper who is after the girl" and to "leave some signs behind that we were the ones who prevented her assassination," and offers $2 million into a Swiss account. "Fine…I'll do my best."
- Cut: "Sorry, but could you toss this magazine out for me?" Maefuji gets out of a patrol car. The glossary identifies "the manga" he was reading as ***Golgo 13***.
- The book does not say whether the opening is the manga's story or what really happened. Standing over the body, Maefuji "thought back to the manga he'd been reading in the car. It seemed like fiction, but the corporate party being held at the same time as the crime didn't seem like coincidence."

*What the client says — the Karafuto Crisis*
- Karafuto was split after the Russo-Japanese War: south to Japan, north to Russia.
- **Japan surrendered to the Allies in WWII, then declared war on the Axis powers**, preserving many national interests despite its defeat.
- The Soviet secretary general resented this. When Japan entered the **Manchurian War**, the USSR invaded Karafuto and set up the **People's Democratic Republic of Northern Japan**.
- The West won the Manchurian War, but near its end the Soviets tested a nuclear weapon. America defended Manchuria and **acknowledged the Northern Japanese government** — to avoid nuclear war, and "to penalize Japan for their surrender in World War II." This is the Karafuto Crisis. *(Added 9 October 2026: the threat worked without use. Vol. 5 Ch. 5 has Okazaki say the Soviets "nuked both friend and foe" in that war, which conflicts with this account. Either way, this world never had Hiroshima or Nagasaki (Vol. 4 Ch. 11). Its nuclear order rests on a threat nobody has seen carried out on a city — see the Vol. 5 map, Part II §3a.)*
- *New detail:* South Karafuto became Northern Japan; **North Karafuto** was an autonomous region that Northern Japan administered until the Berlin Wall fell. The whole territory then reverted to Japan — "but Russia insists that North Karafuto belongs to them."
- **Why Chechens would shoot a Japanese girl.** Russia "won" the first Chechen War (1994–96) and was humiliated; the second began in 1999. Chechen fighters and allied Islamist networks want to break the deadlock by lighting a fire in the East: Japanese–Russian friction unsettles Manchuria, China moves, the US is drawn in, NATO tenses, and Russia can no longer concentrate on Chechnya.
- "They caused us a lot of pain in Afghanistan, and we warned the United States of their threats." The professional's reply: the US trained those fighters, and will not accept a warning "especially when it's reported from Russia."
- **Why Russia protects her.** She stands high in the Romanov succession and has powerful supporters; if she died, "we don't know how the oligarchs who strongly oppose the president or the conservatives might act." And: "**she is in control of many Russian debts that materialized after the financial crisis. The assurance that she and her fund purchased these debts is supporting our current national loans.**"

*The case*
- A body in a Shinjuku alley: "Marcus Gordon," 43, British, carrying a press pass. Born in Pakistan, naturalised through adoption. Broken neck. His camera is smashed and holds no film. Reporters are already there — "someone had leaked information."
- The alley has no view of the Shinjuku Keika Hotel, so he was killed elsewhere. The Cabinet Information Research Office has already asked for a report. Maefuji: "I've already been told that I can't have anything to do with the Keikain Dukedom."
- **Angela arrives** "as a representative of the Keikain Dukedom." Maefuji: "You didn't kill him, did you?" — "Of course not."
- Natsume notices what is missing: night-vision equipment. The man was no paparazzo.
- Angela offers Kitakaba Security's footage from every sniping vantage point around the hotel — "an offer of a backroom deal regarding a murder case." Maefuji takes it. The police will announce that a foreign tourist was killed in a robbery around midnight — the day *after* the party — with no mention of the Keikains. "**The most convenient facts, as opposed to the truth, were corrupting his decision.**"
- Angela's condition: "I have to ask that you never tell my lady about this." Russian intelligence once nearly kidnapped Runa "and they may be behind this incident too. Only this time, their goal would be to save her." Thinking aloud, she advises checking the victim's religious background and extremist ties.
- Maefuji: "Who exactly are you loyal to?" — "**I'm loyal to Miss Runa, for as long as our contract lasts.**"
- He tells Natsume to join the bodyguard unit attached to Runa: "the Keikain Dukedom hates me."
- **Outcome:** officially unsolved. Later, explosive powder is found in the soles of the victim's shoes and he is "declared a terrorist suicide bomber"; that autumn he is linked to radical Islamists.

**Revelations**
- Someone tried to kill Runa at her birthday party, someone else killed the attacker first, and her own secretary and the police agreed to keep it from her.
- The Russian state depends on her as a creditor: her holdings of crisis-era debt underpin its current borrowing.
- North Karafuto is disputed between Japan and Russia — the seed of the "Northern Karafuto" diplomacy in Vols. 4–5.
- **A correction to Vol. 1's picture.** Vol. 1's narration left the impression that the Japanese home islands were partitioned. The partition was of **Karafuto/Sakhalin**, by Soviet invasion during the Manchurian War. Japan's switch of sides in WWII explains why it kept its peerage, zaibatsu and Privy Council.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the **Chechen War** as "the nightmare of the Soviet Union and Russia after their Afghanistan intervention," with Afghan-veteran fighters "giving glimpses of the terrorist networks that would form in the future"; **British immigration** from Pakistan and exposure to extremism; ***Golgo 13*** (Saito Takao, Shogakukan).
- **Real:** the two Chechen wars (1994–96; from 1999). "Let's hear it" is the signature line of Golgo 13's hired gun, Duke Togo, and the Swiss account is his standard fee arrangement.
- **Plausible echo (my identification):** explosives in the shoe soles recall **Richard Reid**, the British "shoe bomber" who tried to blow up a transatlantic flight on 22 December 2001. The novel moves the motif forward to spring.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The chapter is about how official truth gets made.** "Truth didn't matter here. The rules of this world were to follow the most convenient interpretations of the facts." A killing is re-dated and re-labelled within minutes, by agreement between a private household and the police, and the officer who signs off is the one the game casts as Runa's nemesis.
- *Strongly supported:* **Runa never learns any of it.** This is the second time in two chapters that adults decide what she will know (Part II §10).
- *Strongly supported:* Angela defines her loyalty as a contract with an end date. It is honest, and it is the opposite of Tachibana's.
- *Plausible:* The *Golgo 13* frame is a statement of method. The series tells real history through pulp genres; here the pulp may be literally true inside the story, and the detective cannot tell which.
- *Plausible:* Russia's motive is a creditor's: the president protects the girl who holds his debt. Chapter 6's "you bet it all on the Russian president" is the same relationship seen from her side (Part II §8).

**Connections:** The put options bought on the day of this attempt are traced in Ch. 5, where Okazaki calls it a possible "rehearsal." Natsume reappears as Runa's police shadow in Chs. 4 and 11. Angela's high-alert landing at Andersen (Ch. 4) is the same threat seen from the child's side.

## Chapter 4 — The Last Days of the Den-en-chofu Manor

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV, with one section from Amane Mio's. Seven vignettes from the months before the move to Kudanshita, running from the start of fifth grade to the summer of 2001 and overlapping Chs. 2–3. *(Rewritten in the third edition from a full read.)* Everything looks like school-life comedy. Almost every scene has a security detail in the frame, and three of them carry the volume's argument.

**What happens**

*1. Clubs, and a "kendo" that isn't*
- Upper graders may join clubs. Eiichi takes track and field, Yuujirou kendo, Mitsuya the "going-home club." Runa picks kendo — less attention than track, and "I was almost kidnapped once. I feel like I should learn enough to be able to defend myself."
- Her teacher is the maid **Kitagumo**, a former Northern Japanese soldier. In a real attack, "heart, technique, and strength don't matter at all… You have one weapon you can use, my lady. It is your voice." Runa now has "enough bodyguards for them to observe each other and prevent betrayals."
- Mitsuya looks it up on his PHS: she has been learning **Systema**. She joins as a once-a-week ghost member and wins the city tournament anyway; the club's intake jumps the next year.

*2. Guam — Angela's lesson*
- Angela, "apparently afraid that my dedication to Eastern martial arts would divert my loyalty to that part of the world," proposes a day trip: "a shooting tour at American military bases."
- On the jet: "**Becoming an adult is the same as abandoning opportunities. You can do anything, my lady, which means you could become a monster.** One reason the United States allows important people from many countries to visit for cultural exchange is to prevent the rise of dictators." And: "You've already amassed a great fortune and have brought multiple Japanese corporate groups under your control… You're now studying military hand-to-hand combat. Once you can do everything on your own, why even listen to the opinions of others?" Runa: "It seemed that she saw me as a very dangerous person. At this point, I agreed with that assessment."
- Angela's practical point: half-learned combat breeds the idea of taking on a gunman alone; firing a real gun teaches "that you can't do everything." Runa: "I don't think people under the protection of others should ever have to fire a gun." Angela agrees.
- The private jet lands directly at **Andersen Air Force Base**, which is on high alert, soldiers with weapons drawn around the aircraft. "Angela, at what level, exactly, am I being targeted?" — "Please read between the lines."
- Angela (East Coast, Democrat, pro-gun-control) and Eva ("Well, I'm Texan") explain American guns: the police are two hours away and "aren't trustworthy," the issue is "tied to the climate of racism," and for some women guns are what "balance the scales." "It was how they reacted to learning just how powerless both they and society were."
- She fires a Beretta 92, swims, and comes home with a tan.

*3. The Akasaka mini-marathon*
- Teisei sponsors a TV special; the host has invited her personally. She greets every dressing room, starting with the green room: "the people at the top already know who I am, so I thought I should start by greeting the people who don't know me… I can just act like an ignorant child. I'll be taking full advantage of that perk."
- Angela is meanwhile negotiating with a station executive about "defamatory coverage of me." Anisha asks why not simply threaten to pull the sponsorship. Runa: "**The people at the top in this country don't have any influence. It's the ones on the ground who hold the real power.** The Japanese military organization during the Pacific War had just recently proved this point, and now the mass media industry was dealing with the same issue." Producers, newscasters and hosts hold power through real-time decisions; "one host even boasted of personally causing the downfall of three different cabinets. These were the people who were after me." Too much pressure "would solidify my status as a villain."
- Anisha, ex-KGB: "In my hometown, orders from leaders have always resulted in action." Runa: "But this is freedom. It's democracy. You can say what you like, but so can other people."
- The man who sets the handicaps asks her favourite TV show. "At the time, I didn't understand the weight and finality of those words." Her answer: "I think documentaries are the most interesting shows on TV."
- She gets a head start and one lap fewer; anyone who passes her wins ¥100,000 from Teisei. Asked what she hopes for: "Victory." She wins, the show hits its highest viewership ever, and — as she had arranged through Anisha beforehand — every participant gets the ¥100,000 anyway.
- Her winner's interview, "It was nothing!", chosen "to make me sound more like a villainess," becomes the most-quoted phrase of the year. "I had no idea this moment would come back at the end of the year to mortify me."

*4. Angela, a VCR and a Tora-san film*
- Angela loves Japanese films; tonight's is one that "involves guests, a heroine, and a travel destination, then it ends in the usual way. To Japanese people, it's a reminder of what daily life is like." Runa, "one-fourth Japanese," has seen it on TV and remembers little.
- "It's just that Americans can't resist a 'homeland' story." Angela's ancestor came on the *Mayflower*; her family are East Coast "farmers" — Old Money. She worked on Wall Street in the 1980s, "got swept up in the movement for women's rights," had a daughter. Her husband, who had posed as a Wall Street businessman, "just never came home."
- "I probably love this movie because the main character comes home in the end… In my case, he never came back."
- Runa deduces that he vanished in Japan. Angela: "I suggest keeping that brain in check, since detectives who are too clever sometimes end up getting killed by criminals." Then she tells it: Japan, mid-bubble, "in harsh conflict with the socialists in Karafuto"; her husband undercover; "I joined the CIA to discover the truth, came to this country, and now I'm watching a movie here with you."
- Runa checks her history books: 1989, the fall of the Berlin Wall. "Somewhere amid that dark history, Angela's husband disappeared. It was also when my father, Keikain Otsumaro, betrayed his country. Connections between people weren't strictly things of the here and now."

*5. Soccer*
- The Quartet substitutes for a flu-hit school team against a real club side; Runa keeps goal. They reorganise at half-time and still lose 0–2: "everything came down to group synthesis, not individual talents." The forward who scored has been invited to the junior leagues and offers "an autograph from a future World Cup player."
- Runa has Tachibana donate to a soccer organisation: "Maybe I could build a team whose talent would one day go on to win the World Cup. I'd never been able to witness such a thing in my past life."

*6. Mount Takao*
- A school hike. Kitakaba Security deploys **seven squadrons** over every trail with a base at the summit; Inspector Natsume follows in a police car. Anisha — a former KGB honey-trap agent, "the most maid-like" of the staff — would have liked to close the mountain to the public.
- Half of every other class quits at the cable-car station. Eiichi and Runa stage enough enthusiasm that theirs cannot: "Peer pressure was very effective in the caste system of Japanese classes… We forced the decision on them as soon as it was made."
- Hotaru is seen in two places at once and has acquired a stoat ("Definitely not a fox spirit"). Kazuki Shiori is absent, "sick."
- At Yakuo-in, brushing back her blonde hair: "Though I looked a lot like a foreign tourist, I was fundamentally Japanese. As a minority both inside and outside Japan, it was hard not to sense the malice coming from the character known as Keikain Runa."
- **The prayer:** "God… No, Buddha, I suppose. Why do I exist? I can't play the role I was given as a villainess. This country is so much like the one from my past life, yet I won't meet the same fate I did there. Where am I supposed to go? What am I supposed to do?… Is it really all right for someone like me to exist? Am I allowed to change history?"
- **The summit.** Fuji, the Kanto plain, Tokyo's towers. She cries. "I had found myself doubting my very life, which felt like something loaned to me. I'd viewed this borrowed life of mine as a role I needed to play, but the world was teaching me just how tiny my existence really was. 'So it's all right for me to live freely after all…'" Eiichi: "Right. We have our freedom, at least as long as we can smile like this."
- Then everyone takes the cable car down.

*7. Mio's three "sisters," and a haunted house*
- From Mio's side: Runa is the eldest, who "sometimes acted immaturely, but adults bowed their heads to her and called her 'Your Little Majesty'"; Asuka, a politician's daughter, reads people better than Runa and — Mio believes — "secretly saw Keikain Runa as an example of someone not to imitate"; Hotaru is "probably the cleverest."
- Runa, who cannot bear Japanese horror "due to her real-life fantasy experience of reincarnation," takes them to a Teisei haunted house and bolts at the first scare. Angela has had the staff take the ghosts out.
- Mio and Hotaru walk through. At the end a ghost in a white sheet points them to the exit and vanishes. Staff, overheard: "A ghost? But we took all the ghosts out so they wouldn't scare the young lady." Mio says nothing.

**Revelations**
- Runa's own American secretary regards her as a potential dictator and says so to her face — and Runa agrees.
- She is under a level of threat that puts a US air base on alert. She is not told why (Ch. 3).
- Angela joined the CIA to find a husband who disappeared in Japan around 1989 — the year Runa's father committed treason. Runa suspects a link. It is not resolved in this volume.
- Runa has been living her second life as "a role." On the mountain she decides she may "live freely."

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** Systema; the KKK; Texas and the NRA; Star Sand Beach behind Andersen; the **Akasaka Gochoume Mini-Marathon** from TBS's *All Star Kanshasai*; the film is ***Otoko wa Tsurai yo*** (the Tora-san series); the Pilgrims; "Old Money."
- **Real:** *All Star Kanshasai* was hosted by Shimada Shinsuke from its first broadcast; the handicapped mini-marathon is its signature event. Yakuo-in on Mount Takao (599 m) dates to the eighth century; the Octopus Cedar is real.
- **Plausible (my identification):** the host who "boasted of personally causing the downfall of three different cabinets" is **Tahara Soichiro**, credited in Japanese media with ending three premierships (Kaifu, Miyazawa, Hashimoto) through his interviews on *Sunday Project*.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **Angela states the series' counter-thesis.** Adulthood is the *narrowing* of what one can do; a person for whom nothing narrows is a monster in waiting. It is the reverse of Kanbe's celebration of genius (Ch. 1), and it comes from the adult who knows Runa's operations best. The cure Angela reaches for is American — exposure, exchange, a gun in the hand so the child learns its limits.
- *Strongly supported:* **"The ones on the ground hold the real power"** is Runa's theory of Japan, and she offers the wartime army as her precedent. It recurs through the series: Iwazaki Motors' departments falsifying data their executives never saw (Ch. 12), and her own apparatus acting in her name (Chs. 3, 9, 11).
- *Strongly supported:* **The adults curate her childhood.** The ghosts are removed, the difficulty adjusted, the bag carried, the mountain garrisoned — and the bombing attempt concealed. The haunted house is the joke version of Chapter 3, with the same punchline: something real gets through anyway.
- *Strongly supported:* **The prayer is the clearest statement yet of the identity question.** She calls her life "loaned," her self "the character known as Keikain Runa," her conduct "a role." The resolution is modest and physical — a view, a classmate's hand — and it does not last the chapter: they ride the cable car down.
- *Plausible:* The film choice is the theme in miniature. Tora-san always comes home; Angela's husband did not; Runa's father betrayed the home he had; and Runa is a "foreign-looking" girl insisting she is "fundamentally Japanese." Everyone in the room is an exile from something.
- *Plausible:* Soccer gives the Quartet its first clean defeat, by an organised team of ordinary players — a small rebuttal to the genius thesis, and Runa's response is to fund the system rather than a star.

**Open threads:** The payoffs promised for the "documentaries" answer and for "It was nothing!" coming back "at the end of the year" do not arrive in this volume. Angela's husband and 1989.

**Connections:** Angela's warning anticipates Koizumi's intervention in Vol. 4 Ch. 9. The Takao prayer belongs with "Please allow me to be ordinary through you" (Ch. 1), "But that's not possible for me" (Ch. 2) and "It wasn't for anyone else, but for me" (Ch. 12). Natsume's presence follows from Ch. 3.

## Chapter 5 — Okazaki Yuuichi's Job

**Narrative position:** A composite chapter — a TV interview with Tachibana, a look inside the Moonlight Fund's New York operation, Okazaki's backstory in his own voice, and a message-board thread on the July 2001 House of Councilors election. It introduces the man who will deduce Runa's secret in Ch. 6. *(Rewritten in the second edition from a full read.)*

**What happens**
- **The interview.** Tachibana, managing director of Keika Railway, explains the railway strategy: the original target was Shin-Tokiwa Railway; KYOSHO Rapid Railway was bought "as a way of gaining some know-how"; Kagawa Railroad came with the Sougou bailout; the Shikoku Shinkansen, Shin-Osaka platform expansion and **Shinjuku Shinkansen** followed. Pressed on political leverage: "**And despite all that, we lost the presidential election.**" The room falls silent.
- Asked why the group spends so much, he says: "We essentially won the lottery and had no core business… we also wanted to be seen as trustworthy. That's how the railway came to be." He puts the Keika Group's assets at over ¥10 trillion by 2000 — from IT stocks, Russian bonds and rising crude — with about ¥1 trillion already in railways and over ¥2 trillion including the Shinjuku Shinkansen.
- On cross-shareholdings, he says released shares go to whoever can afford them, and at the Keika Group's scale "the only groups capable of raising that amount of money would be American hedge funds." He defends Kitakaba Security as preparation for coming gated-community and security-industry laws and a revived railway police. The broadcast cuts to a scandal over ministry slush funds paid to noble diplomats.
- **Wall Street.** Keika Holdings' New York branch doubles as a Moonlight Fund base. The money cycle is spelled out: dollar profits from US tech stocks buy Russian resources; the resources are sold in Japan for yen; the yen buys up Japanese bad debts, mostly at former Northern Japanese companies. Akamatsu trades, Keika Shipping transports, Keika Chemicals refines, Keika Holdings handles currency and insurance.
- The traders favour cheap, long-held stocks over short-term trades, and Keika Holdings has invested heavily in "an algorithmic trading system that was far ahead of what other financial institutions had." The Moonlight Fund sold most of its IT stocks before the crash. *(Setup for the Ch. 7 trader.)*
- Wall Street gossip: Runa has special seats at the new president's inauguration and "had a hand in the Florida results"; her planned California water business with General Energy Online is stalled because Akamatsu is cautious; right-wing Russian politicians and oligarchs want to make her head of the Romanov family "and send Russia into chaos."
- **The put options.** In New York, Okazaki pays a Chinese information dealer, who reports that put options were bought through multiple funds on the day of an **assassination attempt on Runa at her birthday party** — the would-be bomber of Ch. 3, of which Runa herself was never told — someone shorting her companies in expectation of her death. The money traces through London to Singapore, a channel for Middle Eastern money via Malaysia and Pakistan; Okazaki reads grudges from the Asian currency crisis behind it.
- The motive: Okazaki — and through him Runa — "squashed the Russian coup d'état," whose chaos would have helped Chechen guerrillas tied to Islamic extremists. Worse, another huge batch of put options has just been bought: the attempt on Runa "may have just been a rehearsal."
- **Okazaki's story (2000).** A lover of risk who joined Matsuno Trading because it seemed about to fail, he wins $1 million at a no-limit blackjack table in an underground Macau casino — and cashes out only $10,100, so the house cannot touch him. At the mafia-run restaurant he presents business cards for Toudou and Tachibana, both legends in Hong Kong: Toudou as the dealer who supported the Far East's oil prices from Hong Kong, Tachibana as the "**Hungry Wolf from the Far East**," who managed and restricted the drug trade around the US military during the Vietnam War and then handed the network to the Hong Kong underworld.
- Two tells point to Moscow. Ex-Northern Japanese weapons are fetching high prices because Russian arms exports have dried up, and a Russian "seat-filler" is staging big gambling losses — a laundering trick in which the casino keeps a cut of a fake "loss" and quietly returns the rest. Wealthy Russians are moving assets out. The old boss confirms nothing but offers Okazaki a job; Okazaki learns the details of the coup plot and phones Toudou — after which he goes to Afghanistan.
- **The election thread.** On 29 July 2001 a message board (with "lunakeikain" posting) follows the ruling party's House of Councilors victory: independents fielded in Karafuto, Tokyo, Chiba and Kanagawa join the Izumikawa faction, Izumikawa takes the credit, and the party secretary-general looks sour.

**Revelations**
- Tachibana has an underworld past in Hong Kong tied to the Vietnam-era drug trade.
- Runa's side helped defeat the Russian coup, and Islamic extremists retaliated with an assassination attempt hedged through put options.
- The same money is positioning for something bigger — the 9/11 setup.

**Historical references**
- **Real, with an alteration:** the put options echo the real rumour that someone with advance knowledge bet against airline stocks before 9/11. The 9/11 Commission found no evidence that anyone with advance knowledge profited; the novel makes the rumour true in-world.
- **Real:** the July 2001 House of Councilors election, a Koizumi-era landslide for the ruling party; the Asian currency crisis's damage to Malaysia and Indonesia; Macau's growth as a casino city after its 1999 return to China.
- **Plausible echo:** the slush-fund scandal over payments to diplomats recalls Japan's real 2001 Foreign Ministry secret-fund scandal (the translation says Ministry of Finance).
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the Hong Kong underworld's porous border with ordinary society.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* Tachibana's "we lost the presidential election" is the book's answer to accusations of omnipotence. Vol. 2's Shinkansen was explicitly built to buy the Shikoku vote, and the vote was lost anyway.
- *Strongly supported:* Okazaki is introduced as a gambler who wins by refusing to take the house's money in the house's own building — the instinct he brings to reading Runa's bets in Ch. 6.
- *Plausible:* The casino's staged "losses" are a fake trade of the same family as GEO's round-trips in Ch. 12; the volume sets two casinos side by side (Part II §6).

**Connections:** The birthday attempt is Maefuji's unsolved case in Ch. 3. The coup tip is the "check" Okazaki raises in Ch. 6 (Part II §8). The put-option "rehearsal" pays off on 11 September. The algorithmic trading system bears on the Ch. 7 trader. The California water deal is cancelled in Ch. 9.

## Chapter 6 — Cassandra's Lament

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV. The volume's centre, and the payoff to Vol. 2's cliffhanger. Spring to 12 September 2001.

**What happens**
- Runa moves into **Kudanshita Keika Tower** and is walked through its defenses: a second basement connected to Kudanshita Subway Station and, through an unmarked iron door, to the **basement of the Kudanshita police station**; duplicate facilities on the roof "in case of a prolonged battle where one end of the building fell into enemy control"; the purchase of the Kyomei Bank building across the street for dormitories, also basement-connected for reinforcements.
- Security chief **Nakajima Atsushi** — former captain and spy-ring commander for Northern Japan, expert in counterterrorism — requests permission to carry weapons. Runa refuses: follow the law. The legal workaround is that weapons are stored under the police station, costing at least five minutes in an emergency.
- Her compromise is a **reinforced armored skeleton**: Northern Japanese army equipment for cold, blast and shrapnel protection and heavy-weapon operation, abandoned after the Imperial Police were disbanded following the second February 26 Incident, and revived internationally for CBRN protection after "the 1995 terrorist attacks by a new religious movement."
- Three escape plans are prepared, including a **Hind helicopter on permanent standby** at Tokyo Heliport, thirty minutes out.
- **Okazaki returns from overseas with a report: a northern alliance general's captive says "They've got a nuke."** Runa immediately orders it distributed through every available channel — CIA (Angela, Eva), former Eastern intelligence (Anisha, Kitagumo Ryouko), and Tachibana. "This was simply too risky to keep to myself."

## Chapter 8 — Column: The Hell of Afghanistan

**Narrative position:** A short in-world opinion column with no narration, written from a vantage point after the Iraq War began — that is, from at least a year and a half after the surrounding chapters. *It is not real-world background; it is a flash-forward inside the novel's world.*

**What happens (as the column tells it)**
- The "Coalition of the Willing" intervenes in Afghanistan's civil war and the northern alliance wins. The inexperienced new government faces anti-American backlash; Pakistani volunteers build a base in southern Afghanistan and launch a counteroffensive.
- US forces drive the insurgents from Kabul, then shift to Iraq. The coalition funds public order, and the UN puts approved private military companies — staffed by ex-Russian, ex-Northern Japanese and ex-Indian soldiers — in charge of the provisional government's security.
- As America signals it will leave, most Afghans rally to the Pashtuns, and Pakistani volunteers help recapture Kabul. The PMCs welcome it: the Indians want revenge for Kashmir and the parliament attack, the Russians for the Soviet war.
- **The Kandahar Massacre:** southern Afghanistan is attacked with chemical weapons and other weapons of mass destruction — poison gas and cluster warheads — leaving 300,000 dead "according to the official numbers." The world condemns it in words while absorbed by 9/11 and the anthrax attacks; Americans, Russians and Indians each find it a satisfying "revenge."
- Iraq, seeing the slaughter, seeks weapons of mass destruction, which becomes the pretext for the Iraq War. Graffiti in Kandahar — "America has slaughtered our people!" — is answered by a second line, probably a PMC soldier's: "You and your brothers will be wiped out with nukes."
- The records are classified. Since "only a nation can use a weapon of mass destruction," the column theorizes the US evidence came from the Indian government. One PMC rumoured to have devised the justification is **Kitakaba Integrated Securities** — Runa's firm — which formally denies it.

**Canon status — this world's history *(corrected after Vol. 5)***
- **For:** Vol. 5 Ch. 5, in Runa's own narration and stated as fact: "the 'Nagashima Doctrine' — the act of bombing areas to destroy guerilla fighters alongside other local inhabitants — had formed, and Afghanistan had been reduced to an irreparable scene of genocide"; "Afghanistan's war of annihilation, in which chemical and biological weapons had been used and Indian mercenary soldiers sent in, was starting to function as a tool of fear." Vol. 4 Ch. 3 places her military company inside the UN-licensed arrangement under which Indian irregulars operate. The column's main claims — annihilation by area weapons, Indian irregulars, private companies under licence, sealed records — are therefore canon.
- **What Ch. 9 actually rules out** is narrower than earlier editions took it to be. Eva's "we want to, but we have nothing to fire at," and the narration's "nothing of value to be gained by blowing up Afghanistan," concern **nuclear** use in late 2001. The column describes chemical weapons and cluster warheads, later. The two fit.
- **What stays the column's own claim:** the name "Kandahar Massacre," the figure of 300,000 dead "according to the official numbers," the graffiti, and the rumour that Kitakaba devised the legal justification (which the firm denies). No other chapter repeats them, and the column's hedges — "it was said," "rumored," "theorized" — are its own.
- *Strongly supported:* the column is placed here, out of time and without comment, so that the reader carries through Chs. 9–13 a fact the eleven-year-old narrator does not yet have: what the war she is provisioning will become. Earlier editions of this map read the absence of any echo in Vols. 3–4 as evidence that it never happened. Vol. 5 shows the silence is the point — "the truth that I'd tried to be involved in that war no longer existed."

**Historical references**
- **Real:** the anthrax letters, mailed from 18 September 2001 — "seven days later," as the column says; the "Coalition of the Willing," a real term best known from the 2003 Iraq coalition, here applied to Afghanistan; the Pashtuns as Afghanistan's largest ethnic group and the Taliban's base.
- **Fictional (but canon inside the novel):** the massacre, the UN-licensed PMC security regime, and the recapture of Kabul as described.

**Connections:** Kitakaba's mercenary recruitment in Ch. 7; Eva's "nothing to fire at" in Ch. 9; Koizumi's "a child is trying to help kill people" (Vol. 4 Ch. 9); Okazaki's diagnosis and Runa's nuclear deterrence reasoning (Vol. 5 Ch. 5).

## Chapter 9 — The Moonlight Fund's Intelligence Meeting

**Narrative position:** POV of Okazaki and the intelligence staff, without Runa.

**What happens**
- **Late 2001.** The US now operates from Mazar-i-Sharif air base; the northern alliance has split its forces between Herat and Kabul. US special forces drop their PMC disguise and hand the tanks and weapons back to the alliance — but keep the Japanese microwave curry and cup noodles that Akamatsu (through Okazaki) had stockpiled in Tajikistan and Uzbekistan. The food becomes battlefield currency, the US orders it in bulk, and Teisei Food Services' sales spike, to Runa's puzzlement.
- In the Moonlight Fund's Tokyo trading room — which Runa rarely enters because of the cigarette smoke; she is briefed in her own office, which the staff call "the throne room" — Eva and Okazaki study a map of Iraq. Okazaki, "as someone from the country that enraged your people at Pearl Harbor," holds his tongue: for America "revenge was a form of national honor," and Japan has been paying for 1941 in the Manchurian, Vietnam and Gulf wars ever since. "We need an enemy"; Afghanistan is "just the opening act." Asked whether the US will nuke Afghanistan, Eva says: "**We want to, but we have nothing to fire at.**" The narration confirms the US had been on the verge of using nuclear and other weapons of mass destruction in Afghanistan, held back only because "there was nothing of value to be gained by blowing up Afghanistan."
- **The Nagashima Doctrine.** In this timeline, Japan used an anti-guerrilla massacre doctrine in Vietnam, named after Oda Nobunaga's annihilation of the Nagashima Ikkō-ikki. After the Viet Cong destroyed a JSDF-held area in the Tet Offensive, the controversy brought down a cabinet; the US kept researching the doctrine, which proved "greatly effective" in the Gulf War. Okazaki concludes the US plans the same in Iraq.
- **The ferries.** US forces disguised as PMCs gather in Bahrain and seek long-term use of Keika's high-speed Tokyo–Hokkaido ferries, the *Diamond Actress* and *Diamond Prima Donna* — a sign of a coming ground war in Iraq. Rather than replacing four ~14,000-ton ferries with two larger ones, Keika adopts the "**Let's use our aging boats carefully and forever**" plan. Angela: "Do it fast, then. Before the euro gains real power." *(Corrected: the first edition called these tankers.)*
- The currency analysis: the dollar remains a hard currency because oil is priced in it. **Iraq, Libya and other anti-American states have declared they will deal in euros**, threatening that status. Libya has behaved peacefully since the attacks; Iraq has not. Okazaki jokes about the nuke they found; Angela only smiles. Iraq, seeking nuclear weapons, "would make for an easy enemy in the shadow of the terrorist attacks."
- The US contacts the PLO to prevent Palestine becoming a complication as it did in the Gulf War; Israel is forced into large concessions after the stolen-warhead revelation.
- **Anisha returns from Moscow.** The oligarchs who opposed the Russian president are now cozying up to him; the military is nearly consolidated.
- **The failed Russian coup is explained:** after a nuclear submarine sank in the Arctic, the defense minister was replaced, knew his generals would follow, and attempted a coup. Anti-president oligarchs and "the media mogul known as the puppet master in Russian politics" funded it. They were exposed; the generals were overthrown and the mogul fled. **The centerpiece they had wanted for the new administration was Keikain Runa.**
- The Russian request, relayed through Keika's channels because the Foreign Ministry is dysfunctional: *"Don't make your mistress the ruler of Russia or any neighboring country."* Runa is apparently called **"the final oligarch"** by the Russians. Moscow also wants government-level talks on the Karafuto question and will be "flexible" if that one request is honoured — the opening of the Northern Karafuto diplomacy of Vols. 4–5.
- Okazaki and Angela agree to call Tachibana and "decide what we should and shouldn't tell the mistress."
- Angela spots the news: a reputable American financial magazine reports suspicion of accounting fraud at **General Energy Online**. Okazaki privately realizes Runa's earlier, unexplained order to cancel a joint California water deal with GEO means she knew. He says only: buy up all the crude you can — if there's war in Iraq, prices can only rise.

**Revelations**
- Runa was nearly installed as a figurehead ruler of Russia by a failed coup.
- Her own staff now filter information before it reaches her — the Vol. 2 pattern, escalated.
- She knew about Enron in advance and acted on it without explanation.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the **Kursk** submarine sinking (real, August 2000), with the author noting he read about it in *Golgo 13*; the Russian media mogul who "just happened to lose his position at this time" (Boris Berezovsky, who really did flee Russia in 2000–01); the **euro** entering circulation 1 January 2002 and the real conspiracy theory that the Iraq War was meant to protect the dollar; **Hokkai Brent** and **WTI** in crude futures; and the pointed observation that working backward from the January 2002 "**axis of evil**" speech, American leadership "must have been full of people who were already targeting Iraq immediately after the terrorist attacks," with the real invasion beginning 20 March 2003.
- **Real:** Iraq's actual 2000 switch to euro-denominated oil sales under the oil-for-food programme.
- **The Russian president is never named** — in this or any of the five volumes. His real counterpart in 2000–01 is **Vladimir Putin** (acting president from 31 December 1999, elected March 2000, in office to 2008); in Vol. 1's 1998 it was Boris Yeltsin. The defence minister really was replaced in March 2001 (Igor Sergeyev by Sergei Ivanov), though not over the Kursk, and there was no coup. "The oligarchs who oppose the president were cozying up to him" matches Putin's real subordination of the tycoons. See Part II §8.
- **Real:** Mazar-i-Sharif fell to the northern alliance on 9 November 2001 and became a US hub. Oda Nobunaga annihilated the Nagashima Ikkō-ikki in 1574; the doctrine named after it is fictional, from Satou Daisuke's alternate-history novel *Seito* (per the glossary).
- **Plausible echo:** Anisha's remark that Moscow's message came through Keika's channels "considering the state of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs right now" fits the real turmoil under Foreign Minister Tanaka Makiko in 2001. *(My identification.)*

## Chapter 10 — The Lady's Field Trip: Elementary School Edition

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV, mid-November 2001 (the hotel television reports the fall of Herat and fighting for Kabul). Tonal relief after the 9/11 arc — and, under the sightseeing, the chapter that shows two versions of her future and has her look away from a third thing she has built. *(Rewritten in the third edition from a full read.)*

**What happens**

*The trip*
- The Kyoto field trip was nearly cancelled on safety grounds after the attacks. Runa pays for all of it — "I was the main reason for the safety risk in the first place."
- A chartered eight-car shinkansen: 100 students, 10 teachers, **30 bodyguards, 10 maids and 3 police** — 153 people. They ride ordinary reserved seats so everyone travels together.
- **Her newspaper.** The front page reports pressure on the president of **Furukawa Telecoms** — "even though I was actually the one doing the pressuring." If the company fails in the mobile-phone price war she wants it joined to the newly restructured **Shiyo Electric**. Buried at the foot of the article: a bankrupt Osaka firm will be rescued and merged into Echigo Engineering.
- Lunch at the Kyoto Keika Hotel is stand-up soba, which her classmates have never eaten; she lectures on Kansai and Kanto broth and they follow her lead. "My lifestyle always makes me so hungry for this kind of food!"
- **Day one:** Sanjusangen-do, Kiyomizu-dera, Ginkaku-ji. At a souvenir shop: "I can get my hands on most things already. The true grace of traveling is enjoying the scenery." At Ginkaku-ji Yuujirou traces the Onin War back through the shogunates and Runa caps it — "It probably all comes down to the Battle of Baekgang." Her thought in the Togu-do: "This was the building they left behind after years of killing and being killed. As their descendants, we now belonged to one of the world's economic superpowers. What were we going to leave behind?"

*The hotel lobby, 6 a.m.*
- Television: **General Energy Online** "has come under fire for its accounting fraud scandal," is linked to prolonging California's blackouts, and its sale to a rival is "unstable." Then Afghanistan: the Northern Alliance has freed Herat; fighting at the Salang Pass; bombing along the Khyber. "Afghanistan could now safely be left alone."
- She orders Angela to "research **current-value accounting** in America and its regulations. This is probably going to be used as an opportunity to pass some sort of law in Japan too." Its Japanese champions, "particularly in the IT industry," have "been relying on Minister Takenaga."
- To Asuka she explains it as "a new way of making money without any cash."
- **Asuka:** "you're really not normal. You're a genius. I'm proud to be your friend, but it's okay to be more like a normal girl." Her father, a second-generation Diet member at "minister's age," is grateful for the Shikoku Shinkansen and wants it extended: "he'll probably come to kneel to you." Asuka respects him for being willing to.
- Hotaru taps their shoulders without even the ex-CIA secretary noticing. Angela: "My lady, are you sure that girl is human?"

*Fushimi Inari*
- Before they leave, Hotaru presses a bamboo-wrapped parcel on Runa — inarizushi, it turns out.
- **Kurimori Shizuka** makes conversation to win favour. Her family's zaibatsu — Edo-period Niigata merchants in shipping, farming and fishing — was nearly destroyed by the bubble and saved when Ichijou made it Teisei's exclusive seafood supplier ("Burns aren't fatal wounds"). "This history was why Kurimori-san couldn't act as an equal around me. I still wished that she could treat me more casually."
- After the group prays by a pond on the mountain, Runa hears an echo, turns — and is alone among the torii. "What kind of fantasy story is this? However, as someone reincarnated into an otome game world, maybe I had no business asking that question." She enjoys it: "it had been so long since I'd been able to walk alone like this."
- A sun shower — a "fox's wedding." **A bride in a white kimono and a fox mask climbs past her and lifts the mask: "The woman was me as an adult."** She smiles and walks on. A groom is ahead of her; Runa tries to see who it is and finds she cannot move.
- Eiichi's shout brings her back. She is standing at the summit; to her guards she had simply vanished. Angela: "I don't believe in the occult. It's the twenty-first century… Sleeping gas…?"
- The parcel in her pocket has become a clear **Cintamani stone**. Hotaru speaks — Runa has not heard her voice "in so long": "**Aren't you glad you carried it with you?**" (School legend says a day on which Hotaru speaks brings good fortune.)

*Day two, afternoon*
- **Nijo Castle.** The noble children are all descended from one side or other of the Restoration. The Keikains are "burgeoning nobility" created for the Showa era on Meiji-statesman blood; the Asagiris are court nobles whose blood the Iwazaki zaibatsu sought; the **Kaihouins** are impoverished court nobles "descended from sorcerers" whose family business was "banishing vengeful spirits."
- **Kinkaku-ji.** "I wonder why they burned Kinkakuji?" Mitsuya: "Probably because it touched eternity?" Runa: "This world continues to go through change. With it changes justice, evil, beauty, ugliness, and everything else. But this lone temple before me now fills me with feelings of eternity. That must have been why the man burned this temple."
- **Ryoan-ji.** From any spot one stone is hidden. Yuujirou: "'To know when one has enough.' But what makes it enough?" Runa steps aside to where she can see his hidden stone: "If you can't see them on your own, then what if we look together?"
- **Ninna-ji.** Eiichi makes her buy a protective charm after her "abduction." She buys two four-leaf-clover charms and gives him one; both hang them on their phone straps.
- **Arashiyama.** A girl slips past the guards for an autograph; a shop is selling Runa merchandise made without permission, and there is more in arcade crane games. She signs for about a hundred girls. The photographer: "You're seen as a fashion icon for girls your age."

*Day three — Runa's own itinerary*
- **Expo '70 Commemorative Park**, because of an anime film they watched the night before. "I love that organization and what they believe." She sings there and birds gather. Kaoru: "You sure can be strangely obsessive about the past, Runa-san."
- **Kansai International Airport.** Her cover is a photo shoot for the Keika airline. The real reason: "**In the game, there was a single image of me leaving Kansai International Airport on some sort of journey after my downfall.**" "You could see the back of a young girl standing all alone in the departure lounge… She only had one bag with her… I wondered where she went in the end." Eiichi: "You're acting weird today."
- **Osaka Bay, from the boat to Kobe.** "An ark city. It looks like they're finally starting construction." An offshore platform of linked tankers for former Northern Japanese citizens who "have been getting rejected when they try to live on the mainland," movable "if they really have to." Its residents will be "the elites within that class"; the "master plan" runs from boathouses and water slums to the ark's lower tiers, its upper tiers, and finally new housing on the mainland. "**But I didn't want to think about that, so I casually began to sing.**"
- Eiichi: "You really sing so sadly about the past, Runa, but I like that about you." She gives him the song's title — the name of the film's secret society.

**Revelations**
- The game's ending has an image: Runa alone at Kansai Airport with one bag, destination unknown.
- A supernatural agency in this world shows her another: herself, grown, as a bride — with the groom hidden and her body frozen when she tries to look.
- Hotaru is not an ordinary child. Her house exorcised spirits, she foresaw the encounter, and she equipped Runa for it.
- Runa is already moving on Furukawa Telecoms, as Eiichi will guess in Ch. 13.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the film is ***Crayon Shin-chan: The Adult Empire Strikes Back*** (2001); the songs are the Carpenters' "**Yesterday Once More**" and "(They Long to Be) Close to You"; the Cintamani stone is one of four things the Inari foxes hold, "a symbol of Inari's virtue"; Mishima Yukio's *The Temple of the Golden Pavilion*; the Shikoku 88-temple pilgrimage; the Otowa Waterfall; "minister's age" (five election wins); bootleg merchandise as a yakuza sideline; the California blackouts as "one of the indirect causes of the downfall of Enron"; and "regulation of current-value accounting" as the **Sarbanes-Oxley Act** — which "still didn't work for Lehman Brothers."
- **Real, and it dates the chapter:** Herat fell to the Northern Alliance on 12 November 2001 and Kabul the next day; Enron's rescue merger with Dynegy was announced on 9 November and collapsed on 28 November.
- **Real:** "current-value accounting" is **mark-to-market** accounting — the method that let Enron book the whole projected profit of a long contract on the day it was signed, hence "making money without any cash." Japan was phasing in market-value accounting for financial assets at exactly this time.
- **Real:** Kinkaku-ji was burned by a novice monk in 1950; Ryoan-ji's water basin carries the inscription "I learn only to be contented"; the 663 Battle of Baekgang was Japan's great defeat in Korea, after which it rebuilt itself on Tang models.
- **Mappings:** Furukawa Telecoms and the "Ashio zaibatsu" are Fujitsu and the Furukawa group, and Shiyo Electric is Sanyo (both established in earlier volumes). *Plausible, my identification:* "Echigo Engineering" is Niigata Engineering. In reality Niigata Engineering itself sought court protection in late November 2001 with debts of about ¥227 billion; in the novel it is healthy enough to absorb another firm — one more sign of a milder post-bubble.
- **Translation slips:** the text has "Ashikaga Yoshimatsu" for Yoshimitsu, puts the Kinkaku-ji arson "at the start of the Showa era," and switches between "Asuka" and "Asuna."

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **Nostalgia is this chapter's subject, and Runa is on the villains' side.** The secret society in *The Adult Empire Strikes Back* seduces adults into living in an idealised Showa past and wants to stop the twenty-first century from arriving. A woman reborn to prevent Japan's lost decades says "I love that organization and what they believe," sings its name twice, and is told twice that she is obsessed with the past. Kinkaku-ji supplies the dark version: the temple is burned *because* it stands outside a world in which "justice, evil, beauty, ugliness" all change.
- *Strongly supported:* **Two futures, neither legible.** At the airport she studies the ending she is trying to avoid and admits she never knew where that girl went. At the shrine she is shown an ending she cannot inspect. The book withholds the same thing from both — who is beside her.
- *Strongly supported:* **She looks away from the ark city.** A tiered, towable housing platform for a class of people the mainland will not take is the product of her own lieutenant's law (Ch. 7, Part II §7). It is the one moment in the volume where Runa declines to think, and the text marks it.
- *Strongly supported:* The ratio is still the point — 110 children and teachers to 43 escorts. She buys back one ordinary childhood experience and can only do it by militarising it, and the only time she walks alone is when a fox takes her.
- *Plausible:* "Look together" is the friendship's answer to the limits of anyone's sight — offered to Yuujirou, whose question is about knowing when to stop wanting. It is also precisely what she does not do with her foreknowledge.
- *Plausible:* Every friend on this trip is also a dependent or a rival house — a rescued client (Kurimori), a constituency (Asuka), the game's future betrayer watching "indifferently" (Kazuki Shiori). Asuka is the only one who tells her to be "a normal girl."

**Connections:** Ch. 7's mercenary briefings named the Salang Pass, the Khyber and Herat; this chapter's news shows those jobs were carried out. The "ark city" follows the Offshore Town Act (Ch. 7) and is questioned by Ono (Ch. 11). Furukawa Telecoms returns in Chs. 12 and 13. The vanishing costs Natsume an apology letter (Ch. 11). The KIX image is Runa's fullest description of the game's ending until Vol. 5 Ch. 9.

## Chapter 11 — When the Lady Is Away...

**Narrative position:** Third-person on **Inspector Ono Kenichi**, head of the Kudanshita police station across the street from Runa's tower, during the days of the field trip. Short, and structurally the hinge of the volume's back half: it shows what the adults do with her absence. *(Rewritten in the third edition from a full read.)*

**What happens**
- The Metropolitan Police announce "a thorough investigation into the **failed terrorist attack on the Shinjuku Geofront**."
- **How the plot was found:** security companies now employ hackers, "calling them detectives," who gather intelligence on anti-government groups. The targets protest the hacking, "but the world wasn't looking at terrorism with any mercy." The plotters had worked out of a boathouse in the bay district, so every such area is to be searched.
- **Ono's career.** A non-career officer who expected to retire as an assistant inspector, he sat the inspector's exam because "this year's changes in the law" made bodyguards, bounty hunters and detectives **subcontractors of the police**, creating demand for inspectors to supervise them. He is Maefuji's drinking companion.
- He is nominally in charge of the Kitakaba Security presence at the tower: four bodyguard units in rotation, the armed maids "as a platoon of their own," and the company's Tokyo branch office. Kitakaba staff are "presumptive government workers" whom the law has not yet caught up with. They send canned coffee daily; the officers refuse it and invite them to "dispose" of it on the premises.
- Kitakaba is "reviewing their security arrangements" because "the young lady was spirited away"; Natsume has written his first apology letter.
- Ono on Runa: he knows "there were two sides to everything the young lady did," yet "never saw her as such a major player" — she once turned up in person with housewarming soba and left crestfallen when it was refused.
- On his friend: "I hear Maefuji is involved in this investigation. He must have chosen his strategy while the young lady was gone."
- **Ono's suspicion.** The offshore towns were never necessary: "They could have used failed resorts or housing developments at a much lower price, yet the government went with the offshore towns anyway." And "that girl is pouring money into the railway industry in places where it's a mess of land bordering land." The Geofront's centrepiece is the Keika-sponsored Shinjuku Shinkansen. Keika Holdings' old bad debts "were the result of land flipping and involved many violent gangs. **The businessmen and politicians connected to Keika may have been shadily cleaning things up so that the girl could take over.**"
- The narrator confirms it: "Inspector Ono's instincts weren't far off. The investigation led Keika Holdings to provide information about money laundering, and Keika Railway filed a claim against the land flipping they'd experienced as part of the Shinjuku Geofront. **Keikain Runa, who was currently on a field trip, knew none of this.**"
- **His real quarry:** "Only traces remain of the imperial police who caused the Second February 26 Incident. They're our target. Don't let the JSDF get away with our prey again!" That incident was "the greatest shame in police history"; the military had to restore order.
- **The dilemma he names:** after the 1995 cult attack the police built counter-terrorism units, but private military companies raise a question — "**What if the PMCs also became the imperial police?** They had to closely watch the movements of the nobles with their unique ability to operate outside of the local law, and the person at the very top turned out to be the girl, Keikain Runa."
- The JSDF takes part quietly; a destroyer patrols off Haneda in case anyone flees by sea.
- **Results:** a dozen gangs, radical groups of right and left and religious extremists are arrested on unrelated charges; smuggled goods and drugs are seized; the headline find is "a massive cache of Eastern weapons and ammo." The police are criticised for failing to arrest the group suspected of storing it. "At the same time, urban deployment restrictions were relaxed, and the housing problem for second-class citizens would have its day in the political spotlight."

**Revelations**
- Runa's organisation has a past it is laundering: Keika's bad loans came from gang-linked land speculation, and Keika is now turning informant and presenting itself as a victim — without her knowledge.
- The police regard her private forces as a possible second "imperial police," and regard her as the person to watch.
- Her existence has restructured a police career ladder: a new supervisory grade exists because her guards do.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** "the inspector's wall" — the promotion exam's pass mark falls when demand for inspectors rises; and a note that the best gift for police is a letter.
- **Real:** the 1995 Aum Shinrikyo sarin attack; bubble-era *jiage* (land-sharking by gang-linked operators) and the failed resort developments that followed Japan's 1987 Resort Law — the cheaper options Ono has in mind.
- **Fictional, with a source:** the Second February 26 Incident, borrowed from Oshii Mamoru's *Kerberos Saga* (Prologue glossary). The Prologue says it was "kicked off when that author talked about 'the fulfillment of the Showa Restoration'" — *plausibly* this world's version of **Mishima Yukio**'s 1970 call for a coup, which gives the Kinkaku-ji conversation in Ch. 10 a second edge.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **This is the third "job for adults."** Koizumi declines her briefing (Ch. 2); Angela and Maefuji bury an attempt on her life (Ch. 3); now Maefuji times a city-wide sweep, and Keika's managers settle the group's criminal inheritance, for the week she is away. The chapter title says it.
- *Strongly supported:* **The sweep is Japan's post-9/11 security turn, in miniature and privatised.** Intelligence gathered by contractors who are hackers in all but name; mass arrests on whatever charges fit; the military inside domestic policing; restrictions on "urban deployment" loosened. Its ground is the waterfront where second-class citizens live. Runa paid for the first version of this apparatus herself (Ch. 6).
- *Strongly supported:* Ono is the only outsider in the volume to ask who benefits from the offshore towns, and the narration says he is right.
- *Plausible:* The "imperial police" worry is the downfall sentence of Ch. 2 from the state's side. An armed body answerable to a noble house, outside ordinary law, is precisely the thing a government cannot be sure the top layer controls.

**Connections:** The Geofront attack was commissioned in Ch. 7's last mission briefing. The detectives, bounty hunters and hackers were hired with Moonlight Fund money in Ch. 6. The offshore towns: Ch. 7, Ch. 10, Part II §7. The "imperial police" fear becomes Koizumi's move against noble privilege in Vol. 4.

## Chapter 12 — The Rise and Fall of the Electricity Disruptor

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV, with one third-person insert; autumn to December 2001. The volume's second major real-world arc — Enron — braided with a control crisis inside her own group. *(Rewritten in the third edition. Earlier editions stopped at Sakurako's envelope and reported its contents and the "third party" as open questions. Both are answered in this chapter.)*

The chapter runs in three movements: Runa steps back and lets Koizumi take a win; she discovers that stepping back made her prey; she decides to hunt.

**What happens**

*1. Iwazaki Motors — letting the prime minister win*
- **Big-boned policies.** The Council on Economic and Fiscal Policy now sets policy first and builds the budget around it, reversing the Ministry of Finance's old leverage. Runa's shinkansen lands on the chopping block and becomes a controversy for Takenaga and the cabinet. "This administration was good at making the news."
- A headline: "Secret Recall of Iwazaki Motors!" Runa lets slip a past-life memory — "It could be really bad if a truck's tire went flying or something." The article says nothing about tyres. Eiichi pins her to the library wall — "Runa, we're friends, aren't we?" — until she tells him everything. The hidden recall is doors and brakes; the truck wheel is still in the future.
- Her diagnosis: "they've been doubling their books and adjusting the data… since all the departments were most likely doing the same thing, **the higher-ups don't understand that the data is inflated**."
- The government wants to break Iwazaki Motors out of the zaibatsu as a showcase and force a merger with **Teia Motor**. Teia's financing is awkward: its main bank, Futaki Yodoyabashi, earned the name "**Cowardly Yodoyabashi**" by cutting Teia off in a past crisis; Gowa Osan is mid-purge; Teia has saved so much it is called "**Teia Bank**," but funding the deal itself would invite the question of whether it needs the Futaki zaibatsu at all — which Takenaga, for whom "economics came down to profits," would treat as proof of his case.
- Runa: "I don't think we can clean up this mess by ourselves. Let's bring in some adults too."
- **The summit** at a Meguro restaurant: Keika (Runa, Ichijou, Tachibana, Nakamaro); Teia (Eiichi and his father Shuuichi); Asagiri Kaoru; **President Iwazaki Yashirou** of Imperial Iwazaki Bank; Yuujirou and Mitsuya as onlookers; Deputy Prime Minister Izumikawa as third party.
- Izumikawa confirms through the transport ministry that "none of Iwazaki Motors's data is reliable." Tachibana adds that Keika's parts subsidiary has seen abnormal orders from Iwazaki dealers — defects being fixed quietly at dealerships, with free oil changes to bring customers in. Kaoru: "If the executives didn't know… they're incompetent. If they did and covered it up, then they're guilty, correct?"
- Iwazaki asks first for zaibatsu relief, then for Runa's companies to take Iwazaki Motors. She refuses both — "If that were possible, I would have volunteered to help Ayukawa Motors too" — revealing to her classmates that she had been approached over the Ayukawa Motors bailout and turned it down. Eiichi: "in a different world, Runa could have been our rival."
- **Her proposal:** "Defying the Koizumi administration is pointless. Instead, why not let him take the credit?… The Koizumi regime gets to dismantle a zaibatsu and the Iwazaki zaibatsu doesn't have to raise the money to save Iwazaki Motors… The prime minister becomes indebted to Deputy Prime Minister Izumikawa, and I avoid getting involved in this headache."
- **The deal:** Iwazaki Motors leaves the zaibatsu and its executives resign. **Ayukawa Motors**, backed by its new foreign capital, buys it; **Keika Bank finances the purchase**; Keika's parts company takes over the cut-loose subcontractors; Teia stays inside the Futaki zaibatsu. Koizumi and Takenaga announce a triumph of zaibatsu dismantling; "it never became known that Deputy Prime Minister Izumikawa had worked behind the scenes."
- Before witnesses, President Iwazaki declares that "the Iwazaki zaibatsu owes a debt to the Keika Group" — which makes Imperial Iwazaki Bank "think twice about trying to acquire Keika Holdings."
- Her unspoken reason for generosity: "Iwazaki Motors meant nothing to us with Furukawa Telecoms on the horizon." Aloud: "I'll lay low for a while as I've been in the spotlight." Mitsuya: "I hope I'll be able to say things like that when I grow up."

*Insert — Professor Kanbe's telephone*
- Takenaga calls: "At my very best, I'm just a foolish playwright who dances on the stage." Could Keikain Runa be an example of the professor's thesis?
- Kanbe: "Indeed. She's the one great genius who more than outweighs the worth of a million prodigies or a billion commoners. But Minister, let me warn you as a researcher. **Your boss, the prime minister, is certainly on the same intellectual level as she is. Problems will arise if the two ever end up on separate paths. If both end up ruined, the twenty-first century will look grim for this country.** You're in a position to regulate both the prime minister and the girl. Be sure not to pit them against each other."
- Takenaga will pass it on. "The world was in a panic over terrorist organizations, but in Japan, a new political war had quietly begun."

*2. General Energy Online — discovering she is prey*
- Toudou and Okazaki report that crude is sliding toward $20 and that **Blackship Energy**, GEO's Japanese arm, has appeared in Keika's trades.
- **How Keika's oil business works.** "As payment for the Russian bonds, we accepted crude oil from the Russian government that was meant to go to Europe." It is sold in Europe; the equivalent is bought from Gulf producers and shipped to Japan — a swap that removes most currency risk. The voyage takes a month, so falling prices hurt; the position is hedged with natural-gas options.
- **Round-trip transactions:** "Multiple companies collude to repeat orders from each other and create fake sales. We think the natural gas we purchased is being used in that process." GEO is suspected of accounting fraud, of parking the debts of failed foreign ventures in special-purpose companies, possibly of *tobashi*.
- Runa's reading: GEO built itself by trading gas and then electricity, and wants "**to become the dealer in Japan's energy market casino, and the dealer always has an edge in the game**." GEO is behind Washington's pressure on Koizumi to open the power market.
- **The control crisis.** Tachibana, Ichijou and Katsura Naoyuki arrive together: Runa has been **formally adopted into the main Keikain family** *(the adoption itself dates from June 2000 — Vol. 2 Ch. 5; what is new here is that its legal effect is being used)*. Kiyomaro is her legal guardian, and "actions are currently underway to remove the Moonlight Fund from your control."
- Until now her three lieutenants acted on "their own judgment" — in fact on her orders, which had no legal force — so "the buck stopped with those three." Now Kiyomaro can direct the fund and would answer for it.
- The instigators are not the Keikains but "the Iwazaki zaibatsu's three pillars and the Iwazaki Corporation."
- **Kaoru explains Iwazaki.** A holding company that does not manage; a collegiate body of member firms; three pillars — Heavy Industries, the Corporation, the Bank — and therefore factions. Two of the three now owe Runa. "The Iwazaki Corporation is indirectly telling you, Runa-san, that they're discussing this now." So: "there's a third party whispering in the ear of the Iwazaki Corporation." Kaoru has guessed who and cannot say: "I want you to know that I'm your friend."
- **Toudou:** the prize is Akamatsu's Russian crude. The Moonlight Fund holds Akamatsu's majority, so the only route to it runs through Kiyomaro's new authority. Whose orders would he follow? "Legally, Kiyomaro-sama's. Emotionally, yours, my lady. But I have faith that you're not that foolish."
- **The Shirokane dinner.** Kiyomaro and Nakamaro ask her to move in. "Seeing you collapse made me realize something, Runa. You're a child standing up against the world at such a young age. It's all right to let me and Nakamaro take on some of your burdens." They know people are trying to take what is hers. Why haven't they taken it themselves? "**I don't want to be the kind of terrible father who steals his child's belongings.**" Nakamaro tells of friends whose relatives "kept" their New Year's money and never returned it.
- **Why Kiyomaro sold Keika to Iwazaki** — not to shed bad debts ("weren't you the one who handled that?") but because "it was better for us to leave the work up to other people… The bigger a business gets, and the more companies you buy up, the more people you need. And you need people you can trust to manage it all." Runa's own admission: "All I did was curb the power of those companies with buyouts and bailouts; other than that, I was an outsider to them."
- **Sakurako** drops in "just happening to be in the neighborhood" with an envelope from her grandfather: "It's gibberish to me, but you'll understand it, won't you?"
- In the car, Tachibana answers the loyalty question: "I work for the Keikain family, so I cannot disobey any of their orders, including yours. However, I ordered my granddaughter Yuka to work for you personally, my lady. If such a time ever comes, please make use of Yuka instead of me."
- She opens the envelope. "My drowsiness was instantly replaced with a surge of anger."

*The envelope*
- Its title: "**Plans for management reorganization after the absorption of the Keika Group**" — "large-scale restructuring with an American style of detailed management."
  - Sell Akamatsu's resource division to Blackship Energy; Iwazaki Corporation absorbs the rest; cut 20% of Akamatsu's staff and pass the gains to shareholders.
  - Merge Keika Holdings into Imperial Iwazaki Bank, which takes 70% of management.
  - **More than half the personnel on both sides to be let go.**
  - The sweetener: the profits flow to the Keikain family as dividends.
- **The third party is General Energy Online.** Its share price has collapsed; the electricity market it created now demands more collateral — "a margin call." GEO has money in the Moonlight Fund. "Adding Angela-san to the company allowed the United States government to peek at our accounts," and GEO "is donating a great deal of money to the current administration."
- **Iwazaki's interest:** Imperial Iwazaki Bank holds ¥35 billion of GEO's credit; Blackship buys Keika's gas to sell on to Iwazaki Corporation; and Sakata's chemical combine with its own power station is exactly what a power-market entrant wants.
- **The opening they used:** some Iwazaki leaders and some Keikains had always disliked her position, and it "all came to a head on the day of the terrorist attacks." "The real problem is people's opinion that the Keika Group was **lost at sea from the moment you fainted until you woke up again**." Her lieutenants limited the losses, but: "Wouldn't their losses have been even smaller if Keikain Runa had been awake?" A grade-schooler at the top "only emphasized the uncertainty."
- How serious was Iwazaki? "Probably halfway or so… the Iwazakis were testing to see if you would react."
- **Ichijou's reading:** the point is not that she showed weakness. "**It's the fact that everyone wants to help you.** … You're an elementary school girl leading a massive business group… That's exactly why you fainted when the terrorists attacked." Under the plan the family would still clear at least ¥200 billion a year of the group's ¥450 billion profit "just to live an easy life" — "assuming, of course, that you're not ignoring your own desires."
- Whose orders would Ichijou follow? "Yours, my lady. I don't belong to the Keikain family, and I wouldn't be sitting here right now without your support."
- She asks Erika: would you join a company where the best of five gets a bonus and the worst is fired? "Definitely not… wouldn't it be stressful to always be competing like that?"
- **Her decision.** "If you knowingly took a job under those conditions, perhaps you would need to take responsibility. But if the company was purchased and changed to that kind of system, was it really okay to call it 'bad luck' and leave it at that?… That was the future I least wanted to see. It was why I purchased Hokkaido Kaitaku Bank and got involved in politics and the economy. After all my good faith, was this result supposed to make me happy?! Give me a break!" She slams her fists on the papers. "**I was going to defy the times once again. It wasn't for anyone else, but for me.**"

*3. The fall of GEO — choosing to hunt*
- News: General Energy Online files for Chapter 11. Japanese institutions held ¥100 billion of its credit; money-management funds holding its paper are "losing their principal investments."
- At Shirokane, President Iwazaki bows to her. The Moonlight Fund has bought Imperial Iwazaki Bank's ¥35 billion of GEO credit — "**Let's call it a wedding gift for Sakurako-san.**" The rival Futaki Yodoyabashi books a ¥33 billion loss; Imperial Iwazaki books none. The paper is worthless, but the fund "had managed to hammer out a return that made up for the losses."
- Iwazaki: "This is the second time you've helped us." Her price: "**I want to oust the president of Furukawa Telecoms.**" She does not mean to buy it, but its failure would damage Shiyo Electric; if Keika Holdings and Imperial Iwazaki Bank jointly declare no confidence, its weak main bank, Honami, will not defend it.
- **His question:** she could have saved GEO — an energy major with branches across two countries, "close ties to the American president," twenty thousand jobs. Iwazaki Corporation and the US government both asked. Why didn't she?
- **Her answer**, stroking her blonde hair: "I may look different on the outside, but I'm still a Japanese person. I love things like duties of humanity and *naniwabushi*. I probably would have saved General Energy Online if their employees came and knelt down before me for help, but they never did. Instead, I received that report." GEO's leaders "sold off their stock options before the collapse, abandoning almost twenty thousand employees"; their plan even provided for shorting Iwazaki companies if the zaibatsu were dissolved.
- "I didn't take any action, which meant that **I hadn't shorted General Energy Online** before the collapse either… I didn't want to go along and end up in the same place as them."
- Iwazaki: "And that's why you're going to reach your hand out to Furukawa Telecoms, not Iwazaki Motors?" — "There's work to be done now before the loans go bad."
- "Who was it that said the economy was eat or be eaten? I was almost eaten just by refusing to act. Now it was time to make moves. **I would rather eat before I get eaten.**"
- He agrees on one condition: "be sure to get permission from your father and Nakamaro-kun." "I pretended not to see the sad look on the president's face at that moment. Only I knew how much worse things would get if I didn't act now."

**Revelations**
- The "third party" is GEO, and the route by which it found her money runs through her own CIA-trained secretary. The American alliance is also a window.
- Runa's faint on 9/11 had a price: it let rivals argue that a group headed by a child is "lost at sea" whenever the child is down.
- The main Keikain family is sincere. Kiyomaro holds legal power over the fund and declines to use it.
- Her three lieutenants give three different answers on loyalty: Toudou (law to Kiyomaro, heart to her), Tachibana (the family — "use Yuka"), Ichijou (her).
- She returns to the offensive, against Furukawa Telecoms, and the one elder who owes her most looks sad about it.

**Historical references**
- **General Energy Online = Enron. Very high confidence.** Real: Enron grew from gas trading into electricity, inflated volumes with round-trip trades, hid debt in special-purpose entities, was questioned in the financial press through 2001, saw its rescue by Dynegy collapse on 28 November, and filed for Chapter 11 on 2 December 2001 with roughly 20,000 employees. Its executives had sold large blocks of shares beforehand. "Blackship" invokes Commodore Perry's Black Ships.
- **Confirmed by the glossary, and real:** Enron's ranking system sorted staff into five tiers and pushed out the bottom — the "rank and yank" that Runa's question to Erika paraphrases.
- **The numbers track the real ones.** Bank of Tokyo-Mitsubishi reported ¥30.6 billion of exposure to Enron and Sumitomo Mitsui about $210 million; the novel's Imperial Iwazaki Bank holds ¥35 billion and Futaki Yodoyabashi loses ¥33 billion. On 29 November 2001 Nikko Asset Management announced that its MMF would fall below par because of Enron bonds — Japanese money funds "losing their principal," exactly as the news item says. (The translation's "known debts… 40 billion yen" is surely a slip for a far larger figure.)
- **Enron in Japan, for real.** Japan opened retail electricity sales to large customers in March 2000. Enron had set up a venture, **E Power**, sold 20% of it to **Orix**, founded **Enron Japan** in May 2000 and was reported to be planning a power plant. The novel's claim that GEO lobbied Washington to lean on Koizumi is not verified.
- **Iwazaki Motors = Mitsubishi Motors. Very high confidence.** Real: the recall cover-up exposed in 2000 (decades of hidden defect reports); the 2002 truck-wheel accident that killed a pedestrian — Runa's slip.
- **Ayukawa Motors = Nissan** (its founder was Aikawa/Ayukawa Yoshisuke; "new foreign capital" is Renault, 1999). **Altered, and telling:** in 2000 it was DaimlerChrysler that took a third of Mitsubishi Motors; Nissan took its controlling 34% only in 2016, after a later scandal. The novel brings the 2016 ending forward fifteen years.
- **Futaki Yodoyabashi** = Sumitomo Mitsui; **Honami** = Mizuho, then fresh from a three-bank merger.
- **Real:** the Council on Economic and Fiscal Policy and Koizumi's "big-boned policies" (*honebuto no hōshin*), which did put policy ahead of the budget.
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** margin calls; MMFs; and a present-day aside that deregulating electricity after the 2011 earthquake "resulted in many problems."
- ***Naniwabushi*:** a sung-narrative genre whose stock theme is *giri* and *ninjō* — obligation and human feeling. In business slang it means settling matters by loyalty and appeal rather than by contract.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The chapter is an argument about passivity.** She cooperates with the reformers, keeps a low profile, declines to exploit a collapse she foresaw — and learns that a plan to dismember her group was drafted while she lay unconscious. Her conclusion, "I was almost eaten just by refusing to act," is the hinge on which Volume 4 turns.
- *Strongly supported:* **What she is defending is employment.** The envelope enrages her for one reason: more than half the staff would go. She names it as the reason she bought a failing bank as a small child. This is the past life speaking — she was one of the people such plans discard (Part II §9, §11).
- *Strongly supported:* **Two moral economies.** GEO's is tiers, options and exits. Hers is *naniwabushi*: she rescues those who ask and stand by their people, and lets fall those who would not kneel but reached for "the hand that already saved them." It is paternalism, and she knows it; she says it while touching the hair that marks her as foreign.
- *Strongly supported:* **She keeps her hands clean a second time.** She did not rearrange her assets before 9/11 (Ch. 6) and did not short GEO. Chapter 13 shows the boys doing exactly what she refused to.
- *Strongly supported:* **Kanbe's warning is the plot of the next two volumes.** Runa and Koizumi are peers; Takenaga stands between them; if they are set against each other and both fall, Japan's century is lost.
- *Strongly supported:* The Shirokane dinner inverts two volumes of expectation. The cold main house turns out to be decent, and Kiyomaro's reason for selling — a family that cannot staff a group should not own one — is the flaw Tachibana saw in Runa in Vol. 2 (Part II §4).
- *Plausible:* President Iwazaki's "sad look," and his condition that she get the adults' permission, belong with Angela's warning (Ch. 4) and Koizumi's scolding (Ch. 2): an elder watching a child choose the predator's road.
- *Plausible:* Losing legal autonomy at the moment she gains a family is the volume's cruellest structural joke — softened by the fact that the family will not use what the law gave it.

**Connections:** The trader's story in Ch. 7 and its "few hundred billion" loss (see that entry). Okazaki noticing her unexplained cancellation of the California water deal (Ch. 9). The newspaper in Ch. 10 and Eiichi's wager in Ch. 13 (Furukawa Telecoms). The nightmare in Ch. 6, where a broken Runa takes "the devil's" hand to save the family — the envelope is the first hand actually offered. Kanbe's seminar (Ch. 1).

## Chapter 13 — The Gentlemen's Pact

# Part II — Economic and Historical Analysis

## §1. The volume's thesis on foreknowledge

Volume 1 treated foreknowledge as a clean edge: know the future, buy the bottom. Volume 2 began complicating it — Runa noticed her own interventions had inflated asset prices above remembered levels. Volume 3 breaks it completely, and does so deliberately.

She spends months and enormous resources on prevention. She distributes the nuclear intelligence globally rather than trading on it. She arranges nationwide disaster-preparedness drills through political favors banked over two volumes. She warns a northern alliance general and saves his life. She stops a dirty bomb in Tokyo.

And the attacks happen anyway — **worse** than she remembered, because the mobilization she caused pulled attention toward nuclear scenarios and the Midwest, leaving two additional aircraft to reach the Pentagon and the White House.

This is a coherent and fairly sophisticated position: in a complex system, **intervention redistributes risk rather than removing it**. The book states it plainly through Angela — "we have to think of these diversion attacks as collateral damage" — and then makes Runa live with the arithmetic.

Chapter 12 adds a quieter coda. She also knew Enron's future — and used the knowledge only to step away (the cancelled water deal, no short position). The abstention did not protect her: the dying company came for her group anyway. Foreknowledge unused is not safety either.

It also resolves the Cassandra metaphor properly. Vol. 2's title implied the classical curse of not being believed. Vol. 3 inverts it: she *was* believed, by the CIA, by Russian intelligence, by the Japanese government, by her own staff. The lament is that being believed was not sufficient.

## §2. Enron, and the series' running argument about accounting

The GEO material is technically accurate and fits a pattern the series has been building since Volume 1.

**Round-trip transactions:** "Multiple companies collude to repeat orders from each other and create fake sales." Real — Enron and other energy traders used simultaneous offsetting trades to inflate reported volumes and revenue without economic substance.

**Specified purpose companies:** debt from failed foreign ventures moved off balance sheet. Real — Enron's special purpose entities are the textbook case of off-balance-sheet concealment.

**The market-maker position:** Runa's read is the sharpest thing in the chapter. GEO's business model is to become "the dealer in Japan's energy market casino, and the dealer always has an edge in the game." That is a correct description of why Enron wanted deregulated markets — not to compete in them, but to intermediate them.

Set this beside the earlier volumes and a single argument emerges:

| Volume | Accounting mechanism | Real case |
|---|---|---|
| Vol. 1 | *Tobashi* — shuffling losses across reporting dates | Yamaichi Securities, ¥260bn |
| Vol. 2 | Book value vs. mark-to-market; cross-shareholdings as hidden capital | The real Japanese accounting big bang |
| Vol. 3 | Round-tripping; off-balance-sheet SPEs | Enron |

Chapter 10 supplies the piece that made Enron's numbers possible: "current-value accounting" — mark-to-market — which Runa explains to a classmate as "a new way of making money without any cash." Booking a contract's whole projected profit on the day it is signed is what let GEO report earnings it had not collected; Runa at once has Angela study the American rules, expecting Japan to copy them. The Ch. 10 glossary names the sequel: the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, which "still didn't work for Lehman Brothers."

And Volume 2's glossary already named the endpoint: the reforms triggered by LTCB's collapse "**wasn't enough warning to prevent the collapses of Enron or Lehman Brothers Holdings Inc.**" The series is arguing that the 2008 crisis its first chapter opens on is the terminal case of a disease it has been diagnosing since 1997 — that what is *reported* is a different thing from what is *true*, and every crisis is a moment when the gap becomes unsurvivable.

## §3. The petrodollar thread

Chapter 9's currency discussion is the most geopolitically ambitious material in the series so far, and it is handled carefully.

The chain: the dollar is no longer convertible to gold, so its hard-currency status rests substantially on oil being priced in it. Iraq, Libya and others announce euro-denominated oil sales. The euro launches 1 January 2002 into the EU's single market. Dollar primacy is threatened. "They would need to beat a formidable enemy in order to regain that honor."

The book handles this responsibly. It does not assert the petrodollar theory of the Iraq War as fact; the glossary explicitly labels it "**a conspiracy theory about the Iraq War**." What the narrative asserts is only the verifiable part: Iraq really did switch to euro pricing in 2000, and Iraq really was invaded in March 2003. The causal link is left as something the characters believe, not something the book endorses.

The same care appears in the glossary's note on the "axis of evil" speech: working backward from January 2002, "the leadership of the American government must have been full of people who were already targeting Iraq immediately after the terrorist attacks." That is inference clearly labeled as inference.

## §4. The control problem — now stated five ways

Volume 2's Ch. 7 had Tachibana diagnose Runa's structural flaw: "he, Ichijou, and Toudou were the only real pawns Runa had… She was buying too many companies and expanding her businesses too fast, and they were losing control." Volume 3 returns to that flaw from five directions. *(Expanded in the third edition.)*

**1. Kiyomaro's answer (Ch. 12).** He sold the Keika Group to Iwazaki **not** to dump bad debts — Runa had cleared those — but because "it was better for us to leave the work up to other people… The Iwazaki zaibatsu has been around since the Meiji era, and they now manage a great number of talented people." The binding constraint on a conglomerate's size is not capital but **trustworthy managerial depth**. Runa has effectively infinite money and perhaps five people she can rely on.

**2. Her own admission (Ch. 12).** "Without Tachibana, Ichijou, and Toudou, I would never be able to control the companies I managed… All I did was curb the power of those companies with buyouts and bailouts; other than that, I was an outsider to them." She owns the group and does not run it.

**3. The legal fiction (Ch. 12).** Her orders never had legal force. Her lieutenants acted on "their own judgment," which also meant "the buck stopped with those three." Adoption gives the authority — and the liability — to Kiyomaro. Asked whom they would obey, the three give three answers: Toudou, the law (Kiyomaro) over his feelings; Tachibana, the family — "make use of Yuka instead of me"; Ichijou, her. The inner circle is not one bloc.

**4. Her theory of Japan (Ch. 4).** "The people at the top in this country don't have any influence. It's the ones on the ground who hold the real power." Her precedent is the wartime army; her examples are television producers and, in Ch. 12, a car company whose every department falsified data its executives never saw.

**5. The geopolitical mirror (Ch. 2).** "The Pakistani government was unable to control its military groups, which in turn could not regulate the insurgents they'd helped create. This difference in perception would later be connected to my downfall." A top layer the world negotiates with; a middle layer with its own interests; an armed bottom layer recruited for one war and still there after it. Runa's organisation has that shape: herself; lieutenants and intelligence officers who "decide what we should and shouldn't tell the mistress" (Ch. 9); and a private army of a dead state's soldiers that the police already fear could become a second "imperial police" (Ch. 11).

*Strongly supported:* the book wants these read together — it puts the word "downfall" on the fifth. *My interpretation:* her fall will not come from an enemy outwitting her but from something done in her name, by people she cannot see, for which she will answer.

## §5. Where the volume strains

- **The nuclear-terrorism plot is a large invention** layered onto real events, and it does a lot of convenient work: it explains why her foreknowledge failed, it justifies the private army, and it supplies the Iraq setup. It is well constructed, but it is the volume's biggest departure from the documentary mode the series usually maintains.
- **Two extra aircraft striking Washington** is a significant and somber alteration. The book handles it soberly rather than sensationally, but it is an alteration to a real atrocity, and readers should know it is one.
- **The author's own afterword undercuts continuity claims.** He writes out of order, adds sections afterward, and his editor found mistakes post-publication. Apparent inconsistencies in this volume may be exactly that rather than deliberate ambiguity — which is a genuine limit on how much interpretive weight any single detail can bear.
- **The Karafuto Crisis explanation in Ch. 3 partly rewrites Vol. 1's account.** It is a clarification rather than a contradiction, but readers coming from Vol. 1 with the impression of a divided home islands will need to revise it.
- **Translation and editing noise is unusually high.** Names drift (Asuka/Asuna, Kaori/Kaoru, Kazuki/Katsuki, Imperial/Mikado Iwazaki Bank); figures slip ("three-hundred-ton" tankers, GEO's "40 billion yen" of debt); Kure becomes "the Wu region"; an adoptive father becomes a "father-in-law." Single words in this volume deserve less trust than usual, which matters for readings that hang on one of them — the trader's "he" above all.
- **The security apparatus has outrun plausibility.** Seventy maids, four rotating bodyguard units, a Hind on standby, a police-station tunnel, and powered armor is a considerable distance from the Vol. 1 household of four.

## §6. The dealer's edge — why Enron wanted to be the house

**The line (Ch. 12).** Toudou and Okazaki suspect the gas they buy is feeding round-trip trades, where "multiple companies collude to repeat orders from each other and create fake sales." GEO built its empire this way in gas, copied it in electricity, and is pushing to open Japan's power market with US government pressure behind it. Runa concludes that GEO wants "to become the dealer in Japan's energy market casino, and the dealer always has an edge in the game."

**The casino idea.** A casino doesn't need to predict who wins. Every game carries a built-in house edge — in blackjack, the player acts first and loses on a bust even if the dealer would have busted too — so the house profits from volume, and it writes the rules. A market maker is the financial version: it stands ready to buy and sell, takes a small spread on every trade, and sees everyone's orders.

**Enron really was the house.** On EnronOnline, launched in November 1999, Enron was the counterparty to every trade: it bought from the seller, added a margin and sold to the buyer — thousands of trades worth billions of dollars a day. That gave it three edges:
- **The spread** on every deal.
- **A view** of the whole market's orders.
- **Revenue.** It could book the full value of each trade as revenue, which made Enron, on paper, one of America's ten largest companies. Round-trips inflated the figure further.

**Why electricity is the ideal casino.** Power can't be stored cheaply, supply must match demand every second, and in the short run people barely cut use when prices rise. When supply is tight, a small, well-timed withdrawal can send prices soaring. California's deregulated market proved it in 2000–01; Enron's own memos describe "Death Star" and "Ricochet," the latter a literal round-trip of power out of the state and back in to dodge price caps.

**Runa's objection** is the last clause of her thought: "electric power was a core part of infrastructure in modern society, which was why it was so heavily regulated." A house that rigs the game is one thing at a roulette table, and another when the chips are a country's lights.

**The volume's two casinos** *(plausible)*. Ch. 5 put Okazaki in a real casino, where he beat the house and then handed back almost everything — you don't walk out with the house's money in its own building. The same scene explained casino laundering through staged "losses," a fake trade of the same family as GEO's round-trips.

## §7. The Offshore Town Act

**What it is.** A fictional law to build floating "gated communities" on the sea for Karafuto's "second-class citizens" — former Northern Japanese citizens and migrant workers who are rejected on the mainland, priced out of city housing, and crowding into Tokyo Bay boathouses that police treat as crime hotbeds.

**How it builds across two volumes:**
- **Vol. 2 Ch. 8 (news extract).** Takenaga floats American-style gated communities at the Council on Economic and Fiscal Policy; Ichijou suggests a city on a "mega-float," which grows into a special ward accepting Karafuto migrant workers. Human-rights groups compare it to Dejima.
- **Vol. 3 Ch. 7 (news extract).** Proposed by Ichijou; accelerated by 9/11 and a criminal headquarters found in a Tokyo Bay boathouse; trial city at Kisarazu for 10,000 people, built from tankers (almost certainly 300,000-ton supertankers, not the "three-hundred-ton" of the translation), homes on top and utilities below, ¥20 billion; sought after by "the wealthier second-class populace."
- **Vol. 3 Ch. 7 (job briefing).** The law has passed; contractors are hired to break up protests "against the removal of second-class citizens" before they become "a second Sakata Conflict" (unexplained in the English text).
- **Vol. 3 Ch. 10.** An "ark city" rises in Osaka Bay, movable "if they really have to," with a ladder from boathouses to lower tiers, upper tiers and the mainland. Runa won't think about it and sings "Yesterday Once More."
- **Vol. 3 Ch. 11.** Inspector Ono asks why cheaper failed resorts weren't used; the narration says his suspicions of Keika-linked land clean-ups "weren't far off." The waterfront is swept for the Geofront plotters, and afterwards "the housing problem for second-class citizens would have its day in the political spotlight."

**The real ingredients:**
- **Dejima** — the artificial island in Nagasaki harbour where Japan confined Dutch traders from 1641 to 1859: the archetypal segregated enclave.
- **Kowloon Walled City and Gunkanjima** — the glossary's own models: Hong Kong's lawless, ultra-dense enclave (demolished 1993–94) and the crowded coal-mining island off Nagasaki (abandoned 1974).
- **Postwar boat-dwellers** — the glossary notes the boathouses "remained for quite some time after the war."
- **The Mega-Float** — real: in 2000 Japan built a 1,000-metre floating runway test structure in Tokyo Bay.
- **Kisarazu** — the Chiba end of the Tokyo Bay Aqua-Line (1997), whose Umihotaru rest stop is itself an artificial island.

**What it signals:**
- *Strongly supported:* Ch. 7 pairs it with colour-ranked Keika ID cards (the glossary cites the dystopian RPG *Paranoia*) and the Sybil employment supercomputer. Together they sketch a soft caste system built out of good intentions — by Runa's own group.
- *Strongly supported:* It is Ichijou's idea. The adults' designated "pure" member proposes a segregation project in good faith, and a security crisis turns a contested proposal into law.
- *Plausible:* Ono's question — why not cheaper failed resorts? — points to other interests: shipbuilding and construction contracts, containment of a population that can literally be moved, and Keika-linked land deals.
- *Speculative:* An offshore, legally special district for Karafuto people anticipates Vol. 5's revelation that the game's Runa planned to make Karafuto a tax and law haven.

## §8. Runa and the Russian president

**Never named.** None of the five volumes names him; the only explicit mention is Okazaki's line about the check in Ch. 6. The real holders of the office:
- **Boris Yeltsin**, 1991 to 31 December 1999 — Vol. 1's 1998.
- **Vladimir Putin**, acting from 31 December 1999, elected March 2000, in office to 2008 — Vols. 3–5.
- **Dmitry Medvedev**, with Putin as prime minister, by September 2008 — the date of Vol. 1's opening scene.

**Vol. 3's clues match Putin's first term:** the Kursk sinking (August 2000); a defence minister replaced (real: Sergeyev by Ivanov, March 2001 — the novel ties it to the Kursk and invents a coup); oligarchs "cozying up" to the president; and the "puppet master" media mogul who fled, Boris Berezovsky (left Russia in late 2000; the glossary winks that he "just happened to lose his position at this time"). In the novel the coup's planned figurehead was Runa, with her Romanov blood — hence Moscow's request, "Don't make your mistress the ruler of Russia or any neighboring country," and her nickname, "the final oligarch."

**Vol. 1's Russian layer, newly identified.** The oligarch behind Runa's kidnapping worked with a "previous prime minister" who had been "the minister of the gas industry in the Soviet Union and ran a conglomerate which owned a huge natural gas company" — **Viktor Chernomyrdin** (Soviet gas minister, founder of Gazprom, prime minister 1992–98, whose comeback the Duma rejected in September 1998). The "Moscow Declaration" is real (Obuchi and Yeltsin, November 1998); the Moonlight Fund's $10 billion oil-repayable loan is invented.

**Moscow's side of the same ledger (Ch. 3).** The Russian client in the *Golgo 13* scene wants Runa kept alive for two reasons: her Romanov standing, and the fact that "she is in control of many Russian debts that materialized after the financial crisis. The assurance that she and her fund purchased these debts is supporting our current national loans." She is the anchor investor in the Russian state's credit, and it guards her accordingly.

**What "you bet it all on the Russian president" means.** That loan makes Runa one of the Russian state's biggest creditors, repaid in oil. A coup could have torn the debt up. Paying a blank check for the coup tip therefore staked everything on the president surviving, at a moment when that was far from certain — a bet that "casts shadows in the opposite direction," because its shadow points back from the future. Okazaki found the tip in Ch. 5's Macau casino, where staged gambling "losses" showed wealthy Russians moving money out.

## §9. One genius and a million prodigies — the volume's thesis question

*(New in the third edition.)*

**The proposition (Ch. 1).** Professor Kanbe's "economics of genius": mass production rewarded the merely able; the internet age lets one extraordinary person move the whole economy; therefore a country does better "if a single genius emerged out of the sacrifice of a million prodigies." He is Minister Takenaga's friend and agrees with "strengthening the strongest in society instead of bailing out the weak."

**What it corresponds to.** This is a novelistic sharpening of real arguments. Economists had described "superstar" markets since the early 1980s — technology lets the best performer serve everyone, so rewards concentrate at the top. Japan's reform debate of 2001–06 asked whether to keep a system that protected the many (lifetime employment, main banks, the "convoy") or to let capital and talent flow to winners; the era's vocabulary of "winners' group" and "losers' group" comes from it. Kanbe states the winners' case without the usual cushioning.

**The book's replies, in order:**
- **Runa (Ch. 1):** part of it "I can't ignore" — she knows what the twenty-first century's platform companies will look like — but "those million prodigies will never be without value… without so many prodigies and other people, society itself wouldn't function." The Keika Group's "mission" is that ordinary graduates "can be accepted."
- **Angela (Ch. 4):** a person who can do anything "could become a monster"; adulthood is the giving-up of possibilities; why would someone who can do everything "listen to the opinions of others?"
- **A soccer pitch (Ch. 4):** four gifted children lose 0–2 to an organised team. "Everything came down to group synthesis, not individual talents."
- **Kiyomaro (Ch. 12):** scale needs people you can trust, and those take generations to accumulate.
- **Kanbe himself (Ch. 12):** the genius exists — and so does a second one, the prime minister. "If both end up ruined, the twenty-first century will look grim for this country." Even the theory's author sees that a society resting on one or two people is fragile.
- **The envelope (Ch. 12):** the theory as a spreadsheet — half the staff out, the gains to the owners. Runa's reaction is rage.
- **The boys (Ch. 13):** they recognise profit-seeking as "a separate talent" they lack as heirs, and decide to acquire it.

**The irony the reader holds and Kanbe does not.** *Strongly supported by the text:* Runa is not a genius. She is an ordinary woman — a casualty of the frozen job market — with memories of the future and a body the game gave "cheat-like specs." Kanbe's prize specimen is a measurement error. *My interpretation:* that is the author's comment on the theory. The policy conclusion — sacrifice the million — is being drawn from someone who is one of the million, armed with hindsight; and the first thing she does with her advantage is try to keep the million employed.

## §11. *Naniwabushi* against rank-and-yank — what her capitalism is for

*(New in the third edition.)*

**Two models meet in Chapter 12.**
- **GEO's plan for Keika:** sell the valuable division, merge the bank away, dismiss more than half the staff, send the gains to the owners. Its house style is Enron's — tiers, options, and executives who sell before the fall.
- **Runa's practice:** buy the failing firm, clean its debts, keep its people, redeploy the surplus. "That was the future I least wanted to see. It was why I purchased Hokkaido Kaitaku Bank."

**The real debate behind it.** Economists have argued for two decades over Japan's choice. One side: banks kept lending to "zombie" firms that should have closed, tying up labour and capital and slowing recovery. The other: mass layoffs in a country with thin unemployment insurance and a seniority labour market destroy lives and skills that never come back — the generation frozen out of hiring is the standing example. The novel is on the second side, and it knows the first side's case: Kiyomaro, Kanbe and Takenaga all make parts of it, and none is a fool.

**What the book lets us see about her side** *(my reading, from the text)*:
- **It is personal rule.** She saves those who ask — "if their employees came and knelt down before me" — and stand by their people. The standard is loyalty, and the judge is her. Her nickname is "Her Little Majesty," her office "the throne room."
- **It closes doors.** A group that never lays off cannot hire; its few openings go "to people with connections" (Ch. 1).
- **It makes clients, not equals.** Kurimori "couldn't act as an equal around me" (Ch. 10); a Diet member will "come to kneel" for a railway line.
- **Its kindness has tiers.** The same group issues colour-ranked identity cards and floats housing platforms for people the mainland will not take (Ch. 7, Ch. 10).

*Strongly supported:* the volume sets her against American-style restructuring and gives her the sympathetic lines. *Plausible:* it also shows, without comment, that her alternative to the market is not equality but patronage — a feudal answer to a capitalist problem, given by a duke's daughter. That tension is what Koizumi's "breaking down the privileged classes" will press on in Volume 4.

# Part III — Background Primer: The World of Chapter 6

Most of Chapter 6 isn't technically in the Middle East. Its battlefield is Central and South Asia — Afghanistan, Pakistan, India and the ex-Soviet states — with Russia's Caucasus and the Middle East proper (Iraq, Israel, Turkey, plus Libya in North Africa) in the background. Runa gives the chapter's whole explanation in one line: "This is happening because of the end of the Cold War and the Gulf War. You could say that the debt from those times is being paid now."

## Afghanistan, 1979–2001

- In December 1979 the Soviet Union invaded to prop up a failing communist government. Afghan resistance fighters — the *mujahideen*, "those who wage jihad" — fought for a decade.
- The US (through the CIA) and Saudi Arabia funded and armed the resistance through Pakistan's military intelligence agency, the ISI. Thousands of foreign volunteers came too, among them Osama bin Laden, whose network of foreign veterans became al-Qaeda ("the base") in 1988.
- The Soviets left in 1989 and the USSR dissolved in 1991. The Afghan factions turned on each other; the 1992–96 civil war wrecked Kabul.
- The Taliban ("students") — Pashtun religious students, many raised in refugee seminaries in Pakistan, backed by the ISI — took Kandahar in 1994 and Kabul in 1996, imposed an extremely harsh version of Islamic law, and by 2001 held about 90% of the country. Bin Laden returned in 1996; al-Qaeda trained thousands in Afghan camps.
- The holdouts were the Northern Alliance in the northeast — mostly Tajik, Uzbek and Hazara fighters under Ahmad Shah Massoud. The Pashtuns, the largest group, live in the south and east and straddle the 1893 Durand Line into Pakistan; the alliance's Tajiks and Uzbeks have kin across the northern border, which is why Tajikistan and Uzbekistan become the supply route.
- *Pashtunwali*, the Pashtun code of honour, obliges hospitality and protection to guests; it is often cited, with ideology and money, as one reason the Taliban refused to hand bin Laden over.
- The Ch. 2 glossary's claim that the mujahideen "would later form the Taliban and al-Qaeda" simplifies: al-Qaeda grew from the foreign volunteers, the Taliban from a younger generation of refugee students.

## Why terrorism spread in the 1990s — Runa's "debt"

- **Cold War debt:** the anti-Soviet war trained, armed and connected a generation of militants, who dispersed to Algeria, Bosnia, Kashmir and Chechnya.
- **Gulf War debt:** after Iraq's 1990 invasion of Kuwait, US troops stayed in Saudi Arabia, home of Islam's two holiest cities — the centrepiece of bin Laden's 1996 and 1998 declarations of war, alongside sanctions on Iraq and US support for Israel.
- **The Soviet collapse** left weak states, porous borders and badly guarded weapons — the "loose nukes" fear.
- **Escalation:** the 1993 World Trade Center truck bomb, the 1998 US embassy bombings in Kenya and Tanzania, the 2000 USS *Cole* attack, then 9/11.
- **What it is not:** Islam is the faith of nearly two billion people; violent jihadism is a modern political ideology on its fringe whose main victims have been other Muslims, and most Muslim governments and scholars condemned 9/11. The chapter's "Iran, their religious adversary" reflects the Sunni–Shia divide: Iran is Shia; the Taliban and al-Qaeda are Sunni extremists hostile to Shia Muslims.

## Pakistan, India and the bomb

- Born in the 1947 partition, India and Pakistan have fought several wars, mainly over Kashmir; both tested nuclear weapons in May 1998. At Kargil (1999) the US feared Pakistan was readying nuclear-capable missiles.
- Pakistan's double game: the army ran the country (Musharraf was army chief and made himself president in 2001, as the glossary says), and the ISI had backed the Taliban; after 9/11 Musharraf sided with the US — overflight, bases, the port of Karachi — despite strong domestic sympathy for the Taliban.
- The Indian Parliament attack (13 December 2001) triggered India's largest mobilisation since 1971; on 28 December Pakistan warned the US it might pull 30,000–40,000 troops off the Afghan border.

## Russia, Chechnya and "loose nukes"

- Chechnya, in the North Caucasus between the Black and Caspian Seas, fought Moscow in 1994–96 and again from 1999, drawing foreign volunteers from the Afghan network.
- The West feared Soviet nuclear material would leak; in 1997 Alexander Lebed claimed dozens of portable devices were unaccounted for. Al-Qaeda really did seek nuclear help, meeting Pakistani nuclear scientists weeks before 9/11.

## On the "what is Runa?" question

Volume 3 advances your thesis considerably, and the full read sharpens it. The volume poses the question from outside — Kanbe calls her a genius, Angela a possible monster, Koizumi a child — and lets Runa answer from inside, four times.

**"Please allow me to be ordinary through you" (Ch. 1).** Her first request of Erika is for access to ordinariness at second hand. She does not think she can have it directly.

**"But that's not possible for me…" (Ch. 2).** Told to be a child by the one adult who refuses to use her, she says — after he has left — that she cannot. She has just sent soldiers to Nepal.

**The Mount Takao prayer (Ch. 4).** This is the most direct statement in three volumes: "Why do I exist? I can't play the role I was given as a villainess… Is it really all right for someone like me to exist? Am I allowed to change history?" She describes her life as "loaned," "borrowed," "a role I needed to play," and herself in the third person as "the character known as Keikain Runa." Her relief at the summit — "So it's all right for me to live freely after all" — comes from feeling small, not from an answer.

**"It wasn't for anyone else, but for me" (Ch. 12).** After two volumes of acting for the family, the employees, the country, she claims a motive as her own — and the motive is the protection of people like her former self.

Around those four:
- The spotlight on 11 September: "I had always longed to stand in this light in my past life, but now it was like the harsh rays of judgment." What she wanted most in her first life has become the instrument of her guilt.
- "As the person who'd been unable to stop it, what right did I have to experience such normalcy?" That is survivor's guilt, not a villainess's logic.
- Eiichi telling her it's okay to cry, and her realising she had **forgotten to**. "Now I know you're a kid, just like the rest of us" is the first time anyone locates the child.
- The villainess frame itself, now a verbal tic. She invokes it to explain paying for a school trip and choosing a brash line for television.
- "Though I looked a lot like a foreign tourist, I was fundamentally Japanese" (Ch. 4), and again to President Iwazaki, touching her hair: "I may look different on the outside, but I'm still a Japanese person" (Ch. 12). Twice she asserts an inner identity against an outer one — the same structure as an ordinary woman inside a duke's daughter.

*Strongly supported:* she experiences her second life as a part assigned by someone else, doubts her right to it, and is moving — unevenly — toward claiming it. The frame is residual; what drives her is unresolved loss from her first life compounded by new guilt from this one.
*Strongly supported:* what the world reads as genius is foreknowledge, and she knows it; Kanbe's admiration is a misreading she cannot correct (Part II §9).
*Plausible:* the financial dissociation persists structurally (she still cannot govern what she buys), but the emotional dissociation broke on the mountain and again in Eiichi's classroom.
*Speculative:* the nightmare, in which the Keikain family collapses and she takes a devil's hand, may be the actual mechanism of her foretold downfall. Chapter 12 shows her being offered a hand of that kind — an easy life for the family in exchange for the group — and refusing it in a rage.
