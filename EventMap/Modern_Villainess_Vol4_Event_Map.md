# Modern Villainess Vol. 4 — Complete Event Map (Second Edition)
### Chapter-by-chapter, with the WCI operation as arithmetic, the defeat arc, the war she built, and the argument about who may hold power

**Second-edition changes (8 October 2026).** This edition replaces the first entirely. It comes from a complete, end-to-end read of the volume — all twelve chapters, every glossary, the message-board thread, the cast list and the afterword. The first edition had read Chapters 2 and 9 only in part and gave seven of the twelve chapters one or two lines each. It got the volume's outline right and much of its substance wrong or missing. What changed:

1. **Chapter 2's ending was missing, and it reverses the chapter.** The first edition stopped with Runa standing down from the Furukawa Telecoms fight. In the text she then learns that her defeat was **staged by her own guardians** (Tachibana, Ichijou, Nakamaro, Angela) with the rival bidder and Washington; is dared by Okazaki to "just go for it"; raises $20 billion of Gulf money, levers it twenty times, **forges a whistleblower's e-mail** to bring down WCI on her own timetable, and clears about ¥4 trillion. She wins the company — and three adults scold her for it.
2. **Chapter 3** (two lines before) is the White House's reading of that operation, and establishes three facts the rest of the series depends on: Washington knows and tolerates her; she had **already begun building the logistics of an Iraq invasion**; and the White House "already knew that Iraq had no such weapons."
3. **Chapter 5** (one line before) is the longest chapter in the book. It contains the student-council election she buys, her own sociological reading of the "villainess" as "a sacrifice," Tachibana's "you just have to get married," Kanbe's "you are a 'gifted' child… you can avoid taking responsibility," and the Salt Lake City scene in which **she tells the American president when and how to invade Iraq**.
4. **Chapter 6** (two lines before) is the reorganisation of the group, with every lieutenant's exit date and "I was all alone in the first place."
5. **Chapter 9 is rewritten** from a full read: Kanbe's lecture and the Kanbe–Takenaga meeting; the seven-point programme; the fact that Runa had *already decided to step back* when the blow fell; the mechanism (her father's Sakata land deal, Katou, a dawn raid on Tachibana); Kiyomaro's use of immunity; Angela's "Eva would kill you before you even crossed the international date line"; the dream of the game's reform vote; and all three phone calls.
6. **Chapter 10** (one line before): the two leaders agree to "stain our hands with blood" and that "we need her to become Cassandra."
7. **Chapter 11 was mischaracterised** as a "domestic chapter." It is the chapter in which she is shown a paper calculating the death of twenty-three million Iraqis, stops eating, tells the story of her first life, and is brought back by a video she recorded in Volume 1.
8. **Chapter 12 is completed:** the Saudi railway's full design and sale; the Ichijou–Okazaki lunch (the exits, the loyalist faction, "the Ministries," her qualifications); the last line.
9. **Chapters 1, 4, 7 and 8** are mapped properly for the first time.
10. **Part II is new** (ten sections); the first edition's "accounting-fraud trilogy" table and "currency-lag" analysis are kept and corrected inside it.
11. **Forward references to Volume 5** have been added where that volume changes how this one reads — above all in Chapter 11.
12. **Back-references to Volume 2** (added after that volume was reread in full): the Saudi railway's origin in a real oil-concession negotiation and Runa's own statement of why she built it (Ch. 12, Ch. 3); the four earlier warnings that Koizumi's verdict repeats (Ch. 9); the game's reform plot and the "sacrifice" thought (Ch. 5).
13. **Necessary context — the missing demonstration** (added 9 October 2026, from a reader's observation): Chapter 11 contains the one sentence that establishes that this world never had Hiroshima or Nagasaki. It now has its own revelation and significance entries, and the ledger records it.

**Coverage:** every chapter, glossary, interstitial document, the cast list and the afterword have been read in full.

**Scope:** 12 chapters, all glossaries, "Keika Railway Thread #38," afterword, plus analysis and synthesis. Built from the EPUB (Tofuro Futsukaichi, illus. KEI; Seven Seas English ed.).

**Coverage period:** the afterword says "from the winter of 2001 to the spring of 2002." The text runs from mid-December 2001 to late summer 2002 (Chapters 11–12).

**Naming notes.** Katsuki Shiori (Vols. 2 and 5) appears as "Kazuki" in places. Noble legal privilege is rendered "diplomatic immunity" throughout the English text; this map says "noble immunity" where the sense requires. Koizumi's slogan appears once as "no reform and no growth."

**Content note.** Chapter 11 contains an invented American plan for the mass killing of Iraqi civilians, which the text attributes to "a Jewish think tank… supported by the government of Israel." No such plan existed. The map flags the attribution where it occurs and discusses it in Part II §10.

**Structural note.** Like Volume 3, this is a mosaic: a banker's memoir (Ch. 1), a Washington briefing (Ch. 3), the boys alone (Ch. 4), a comic interlude in sound effects (Ch. 7), a television transcript (Ch. 8), a telephone call (Ch. 10) and a business lunch (Ch. 12) surround four long chapters in Runa's voice (2, 5, 6, 9) and one of collapse (11). The design is cumulative: the same lesson — that she holds power without an office in which she can be held to account — is delivered three times, by her guardians (Ch. 2), by a television studio (Ch. 8) and by the prime minister (Ch. 9), each time less gently.

**The volume's thesis, stated by the author.** The afterword is unusually direct. The subject is **neoliberalism** and "the Koizumi theater politics": "as someone who lived through that era, I couldn't write about the theater politics negatively in the end." He credits the administration with "two undeniable achievements" — ending the disposal of bad debts, and building international relations "with the Japanese-American relationship as the nucleus" — and lists its faults ("weak members, abandoning local regions, and a sycophantic diplomacy with America"), adding: "what I want to emphasize is how the people of that time chose those things."

On the defeat, for which he "received many criticisms":

> "Even if the child in question wanted it, would you really make a child still in elementary school sign an order for murder?… It's the kindness of the adults who don't allow that child to make such a choice, and also their responsibility as the adults who hold the current era on their shoulders. There's no such thing as a child who hasn't been scolded by an adult. In short, that's what this volume is about."

He writes in 2022, lists the shocks of his lifetime from the Berlin Wall to the war in Ukraine, and declines to judge the present: "I want you to please reflect on this year sometime later, first when it's become the past, and then when it becomes history."

# Part I — Chapter-by-Chapter Map

## Chapter 1 — The Banker Reminisces

**Narrative position:** A first-person memoir by an unnamed former Far Eastern Bank loan-screening officer, written in early 2002 and looking back over 1997–2001. No Runa on the page. It retells Volume 1's bank rescues from the back office, with the mechanics Volume 1 left out, and establishes the Keika achievement as settled history before the volume starts taking it apart.

**What happens**
- **The narrator.** He saw Far Eastern Bank's real books: a former second-tier regional bank and the Keika Group's "institutional bank," dragged down by Far Eastern Developments and Far Eastern Hotels. He credits the rescue scheme to Ichijou and Tachibana and to "the Moonlight Fund's easy access to quick profits" — Runa is never named.
- **Why Far Eastern was the vessel.** Once cleaned, with ex-Ministry of Finance men in its management, it was "the perfect vessel" for absorbing other institutions — which is why he came to suspect "the whole thing was rigged by the Ministry of Finance."
- **The Keika Rules, restated in four points:** force out all executives; punish wrongdoing; send all bad debts, *tobashi* included, to the Resolution and Collection Corporation; Bank of Japan special loans for stability.
- **Why three firms had to be saved at once.** Financial institutions live on the call market; being refused there means your peers have judged you unable to repay. Sankai Securities, Ichiyama Securities and Hokkaido Kaitaku Bank had all been cut off — "the fuses of their destruction had been lit" — and short sellers had moved in. Saving only the two brokerages would have concentrated every short seller on the bank.
- **Why nobody at the ministry saw it.** Banking and securities were supervised by separate sections that did not compare notes. Far Eastern's proposal made the link "common sense." The ministry also feared Far Eastern would otherwise "simultaneously detonate two financial institutions."
- **Why the reverse merger.** Ichiyama was hiding losses, paying *sōkaiya* and keeping debts off the books — not a firm the central bank could lend to. So it was merged into Sankai and rescued "under the pretense of only saving Sankai." The ministry, "under heavy criticism for bailing out the mortgage loan industry with taxpayer money, must have seen this as a godsend."
- **The "matryoshka doll."** Each rescue repeated one manoeuvre: sell the failed bank's bad debts to the RCC at market price (booking the loss), then merge so that unrealised gains on the other side are realised at the merger and "sweep away the losses." Far Eastern and Hokkaido Kaitaku became Keika Bank; then the Long-Period Credit Bank absorbed Keika Bank as the legal survivor while keeping the Keika name; then Nihon Credit Bank, the same way.
- **The ¥8 trillion was mostly for show.** "We generally didn't use the 8-trillion-yen loan." Government deposits replaced fleeing deposits; the special loans, "unsecured and unlimited," were "something to display." He calls them "the anesthesia" under which the surgery had to be finished.
- **What paid it back.** Keika Securities' stock gains; the Moonlight Fund ("a hundred billion yen in a year like it was nothing"); and "the finishing blow," the syndicated purchase of Russian bonds after the 1998 crisis, whose gains let them repay the Bank of Japan ahead of schedule.
- **Hokkaido.** Local firms held Kaitaku shares; buying them during the bid kept those holdings from becoming waste paper and "probably saved the entire Hokkaido economy." Hokkaido police arrested Kaitaku executives for aggravated breach of trust "while the anesthesia… was still taking effect." Subsidiaries (non-banks, real estate, venture capital, resorts) were "a Pandora's box," all sent to the RCC.
- **Who else was carried.** Teisei's stores gave Hokkaido farm and sea produce a route to Tokyo; Akamatsu traded it; AIRHO flew tourists. **Maruima Department Stores** joined the Teisei group after ousting its president. **Amazaki Construction**, a general contractor with two thousand Hokkaido subcontractors, was kept alive with railway work, starting with the Shikoku Shinkansen — and Hokkaido's political world repaid it by electing Izumikawa Taichirou.
- **After the rescues:** Keika Holdings, "Japan's first megabank and bank holding company," added Kyomei Bank, Marumaru Mutual Fire and Marine, Kadono Life and Kawai Life. Kyomei quietly became a "superregional" bank across Tokyo, Osaka and Hokkaido.
- **The house is divided.** Ex-ministry men lost their way home when the scandal split off the Financial Services Agency; ex-Far Eastern staff were few and less able; ex-LPCB and ex-NCB staff "felt inferior"; ex-Kaitaku and Kyomei staff fought over the succession; and the ex-Ichiyama people were "a band of demons, waiting vigilantly to strike." A Hokkaido faction pushes its own candidates against CEO Ichijou.
- **Ichijou's answer:** import talent from foreign firms and stage a succession race. The current frontrunner is **Angela Sullivan**, who had an offer from "Silver Woman Securities" before Tachibana hired her.
- **What is coming:** Keika Holdings must list its shares and stop being the group's institutional bank — "You're gonna sell out to foreign money?!" — and the group's ties to Fuchigami and the Hashizume faction are "why the Keika Group isn't getting along with the current Koizumi administration."
- **His own exit.** Merged branches meant surplus staff sent to client firms on "one-way tickets." He left for an IT start-up and kept its funding alive through the crash. His verdict: grateful, "whatever their intentions were" — and "now that the Koizumi administration is toughening up on the processing of bad debts, it's possible that everything under Hokkaido Kaitaku Bank would crumble if the same thing happened today."

**Revelations**
- The BOJ's ¥8 trillion was a confidence device, barely drawn. *(This answers the objection, raised in the Vol. 1 map, that the figure was implausibly large: it was never meant to be spent.)*
- The rescues were repaid largely by the Russian bond trade — the position that makes Runa a creditor of the Russian state (Vol. 3 Ch. 3).
- Keika Holdings is five or six former institutions that still think of themselves separately, and Ichijou holds it together by setting outsiders against insiders.
- Even the people Keika saved were then shed: the "one-way ticket" is the group's own quiet restructuring.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the real institutions behind the rescues — "the banking section ended up taking on Hokkaido Takushoku Bank, the Long-Term Credit Bank of Japan, and the Nippon Credit Bank, while the securities section took on Sanyo Securities and Yamaichi Securities"; institutional banks and second regional banks defined; *sōkaiya* as racketeers "often with ties to the yakuza" (correcting Vol. 1's softer gloss); interest-bearing debts of ¥94 billion for Maruima and ¥100 billion for Amazaki. (The body text's "9.4 trillion" for Maruima is a slip; the glossary figure is the sensible one.)
- **Real, used accurately:** the call market as the place where a failing institution is first cut off (Sanyo Securities' default there in November 1997 began the real chain); the Ministry of Finance's vertical division; the 1998 split-off of financial supervision; the arrest of Hokkaido Takushoku executives for aggravated breach of trust (real: 1999); merger accounting that lets one side's unrealised gains absorb the other's losses.
- **Plausible (my identifications):**
  - **Maruima Department Stores = Marui Imai**, Sapporo's leading department store, whose main bank was Hokkaido Takushoku and whose president was removed in 1997 after a failed diversification. In reality it limped on and entered court-led rehabilitation in 2009.
  - **Amazaki Construction = Chizaki Kogyo**, the Hokkaido general contractor closest to Takushoku (地崎 "earth-cape" → 天崎 "heaven-cape" — the series' usual inversion).
  - **Silver Woman Securities = Goldman Sachs** (gold → silver, man → woman).
- **Altered:** in reality none of these firms was rescued this way. Takushoku's sound loans went to North Pacific Bank; LTCB and NCB were nationalised and sold; Yamaichi closed.
- **Uncertain:** Kyomei Bank and the three insurers have no single clear counterpart.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The chapter fixes the baseline the volume will attack.** Koizumi's programme — fast disposal, market discipline, listing, foreign capital — is aimed at exactly the structure described here, and the narrator says outright that the rescue could not be repeated under it.
- *Strongly supported:* **It is the Keika model's own cost accounting.** Nobody was fired; surplus staff were "sent out" instead. The memoir is written by one of the people the model could not keep — and he is grateful anyway. Set beside Vol. 3 Ch. 12's rage at a plan to dismiss half the staff, it shows the difference between Keika and its enemies as one of degree and manners.
- *Strongly supported:* The call-market passage is the series' clearest account of how a financial panic actually kills: not a queue of depositors but peers refusing overnight money.
- *Plausible:* Runa's invisibility is the point. To a mid-level banker the whole thing was Ichijou, Tachibana and a fund; the "one genius" of Vol. 3's Professor Kanbe does not appear in the institutional memory at all.

**Connections:** Vol. 1 Chs. 2 and 9 (the rescues, the Russian bonds); Vol. 2 Ch. 2 (Ichijou refusing to plant Far Eastern loyalists — the factions he feared are all here). Angela's candidacy returns in Ch. 12; the listing of Keika Holdings in Chs. 6, 9 and 12; the regional banks the narrator worries about arrive in Ch. 2.

## Chapter 2 — The Lady's Pursuits: Horses, Furukawa Telecoms, and Conflict

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV with one third-person section; mid-December 2001 to early 2002. The volume's longest plotted chapter and the first half of its argument: the adults closest to Runa try to cage her gently, and she breaks out with the largest bet of her life. *(Rewritten in the second edition. The first edition stopped at Runa standing down and missed that she then reverses the outcome.)*

The chapter runs in five movements: a horse; a staged defeat; the truth behind it; the counter-bet; the scoldings.

**What happens**

*1. Hong Kong — a horse and a viewing*
- Runa flies to Hong Kong for the **Hong Kong Vase** at Sha Tin, having talked Kiyomaro and Nakamaro into it, to see one horse run his last race: a perennial second- and third-placer who has never won a G1 but beat "the champion of the World Series Racing Championship" in Dubai.
- In the nobles' seats she meets **Karl Lothringe** and his grandson **Franz** — "leading European aristocrats from the game's backstory," a house revived by postwar Austria; Europe's blue bloods now fill the EU's bureaucracy and diplomacy, one reason Japan's nobles operate freely abroad. Franz was written as a study-abroad love interest and cut from the game.
- Runa: "Don't you think the journey means more than the destination?" Franz: "Results are all that matter." Her thought: "as a villainess doomed to defeat, I couldn't help but sympathize with the horse."
- The horse wins. She cries — "not about the race… about that horse's story" — and becomes the owner of his offspring. "I never imagined what big things that horse would accomplish." Offered the country's most fashionable sire, she picks a chestnut instead because he is "lovely to look at."
- **The real errand.** With the Foreign Ministry failing, the government uses the Keikains as a back channel: Angela trades information with a contact, Tachibana meets his own in the Hong Kong salons, and the old man at the track was no coincidence — Europeans want a read on Tokyo and Washington through her. The market for Eastern weapons and ex-Northern Japanese mercenaries is rising with the fighting in Afghanistan.
- Tachibana: "I believe it would be wise for you to avoid learning too much about this topic." Runa: "They'd tried to distance me from adult work since the day the airplanes hit those towers. I appreciated it, but it also frustrated me."
- World-building in passing: Hong Kong has been returned by "Communist China," whose capital in this world is **Nanjing**.

*2. The takeover bid — "We'll be defeated"*
- On the flight home: the **Machishita Fund**, with the American **Iron Partners**, announces a takeover bid for **Furukawa Telecoms** at ¥2,438 (¥1,950 plus 25%), seeking 33.4% — a veto stake. Machishita says on television that the company is really controlled by **Teikoku Telephone**, whose former director-general's son was the president that "the banking groups, starting with Keika Holdings," had just forced out; shareholders were ignored and diluted.
- At Keika Holdings in Kayabacho, Ichijou briefs her, Nakamaro and Tachibana. **Shinden Bank**, the Ashio zaibatsu's main bank, is failing and wants to join Keika (about ¥600 billion under the Keika Rules); the Ashio zaibatsu cares more about the bank than about Furukawa Telecoms.
- Behind Shinden come **Sendai City, Echigo Central, Wakayama Shiawase, Shinmin and Imperial Aiwa** banks — "these banks were all struggling in 1997, but your work in preventing the financial crisis allowed them all to survive. That didn't eliminate their bad debts." Koizumi's accelerated disposal is now pushing them over.
- Tachibana: regional banks cannot simply be emptied into the RCC; each needs engagement with local firms and politics. "**We had the money, just not the manpower for it.**"
- Angela: the fund is backed by Iron Partners, itself controlled by the American securities house **Charles & Edward**; the prize is Furukawa Telecoms' stake of just under 40% in **Furukawa Automatic Machine Manufacturing**, the leader in machine tools and robots. And the money and political weight behind it all belong to **Teikoku Telephone** — "one of Japan's ten largest conglomerates."
- Runa asks what happens if they fight on all fronts. Ichijou: "**We'll be defeated.**" The Moonlight Fund earns dollars and turns them into yen only through shipped crude, with a lag; and the ¥2 trillion **Shinjuku Shinkansen** was paid as a lump sum for a discount. Delaying the bank rescues is impossible: "We've only been able to do as we please because it's always ended up benefiting the people."
- She agrees to stand down and to shield **Shiyo Electric** instead (Keika holds 37%; a buyback lifts that). Asked what she wants Shiyo for, she puts her PHS on the table: small screens and batteries will become the handsets of the future; with Furukawa's telecoms position Japan could set the world standard instead of falling into "Galapagos Syndrome." "**This is how we bring the world under our control.**"

*3. Next door — the farce*
- Third person, Ichijou and Nakamaro: "It's not like we can tell her that Tachibana-san and Secretary Angela know this was all a calculated move by our own people."
- What really happened: Teikoku Telephone answered the ousting of its man by having **every systems developer — Japanese, then foreign — cancel Keika Holdings' core-banking order.** Ichijou and Tachibana, with Nakamaro's permission, sued for terms; Angela worked Washington. Teikoku's single condition: "**Get that young lady under control.**"
- The four of them then staged the takeover bid with Teikoku, the fund and Iron Partners, and used the regional banks and the Shinkansen payment as the reason she could not afford to fight. "It was all a cover story whipped up to prevent the young lady from opposing them."
- **Why they did it.** Angela: "The young lady has too much potential. She might even become a dictator at this rate." If Runa stays this close to the Republican administration, the Democrats will punish her when they return. Russia fears she could split its government and end as the head of "a massive Russian-Japanese autocratic empire"; with real power she "could very well end up having her blood spilled." The four swear one aim: "**May Runa be guaranteed a good, happy life.**"
- **The Hong Kong meeting was a marriage viewing.** "We had Runa meet with the European nobles in Hong Kong, as was forcibly demanded of us. Given her current status, there's no better family for her to marry into." Either a European or a Russian match makes her a target for someone.
- "So she gets to be a bird in a cage?" — "What she needs to learn is that the cage protects the bird from the outside world… If the sight of those buildings collapsing was the embodiment of her failure, history will look back and condemn us adults, wondering what we were doing during all this."
- The boys have sold TIG Systems and bought Furukawa Telecoms shares; the bid would hand them up to ¥6 billion.
- Then the farce turns real: in an American proxy fight over a computer-maker merger, the opposing camp shouts, "If you're going to buy a company, you should buy out Furukawa in Japan instead!"

*4. Okazaki's dare, and the trigger*
- Shinden Bank joins Keika; the other regional banks queue up; Keika Hotels takes on the redevelopment of **Kinugawa Onsen**. Shiyo takes just over 10% of **Hope Memory**, sells its loss-making large appliances to Matsuyuki Electronics and sets itself up as a parts supplier to the IT industry; Keika's stake in Shiyo rises to 50%.
- Okazaki finds her brooding in the Moonlight Fund office. He has worked it out: "You're a turnaround manager… You look at industries instead of just companies. That must mean you know the computer industry is going to see a decline." Runa, to herself: Japanese consumer electronics "really was going to crumble in the next dozen years or so."
- "**Why not just go for it?**… Losing only a few trillion yen isn't the end of the world… Even if you fail, just like Suzuki Shoten, you'll have something to leave behind."
- News: the American computer company's female CEO resigns after the founder's faction wins the proxy fight. Runa: "Let's start the game… If we must bow our heads to anyone, let's do it together." First order: "Start by scouting that female CEO."
- **The war chest.** Working from London so that Angela and Ichijou will not see, Okazaki borrows about **$20 billion** of Gulf oil money at 9% — Runa having told him the US will attack Iraq — in names unconnected to Keika or the fund ("he could jump off a building and the fallout would never reach me"). It passes through shell companies in the Isle of Man, the Caymans and the Virgin Islands into computer-run funds and is levered twenty times: **$400 billion**.
- **The trigger.** The code phrase arrives through an unwitting Erika ("Lady Runa doesn't grasp the taste of black coffee either"). Runa shows Angela a "whistleblower e-mail" — sent by Okazaki from a London internet café — alleging fraud at **WCI Inc.**, America's second-largest long-distance carrier and a client of the same auditor as General Energy Online. She proposes burying it to protect the president, knowing Angela and Eva will report it. "If something was set to blow up, I was going to make sure it happened on my schedule."
- WCI's chief resigns; three days later it files for Chapter 11. Okazaki shorts WCI and NASDAQ technology stocks "as soon as the first reports came out," staying "just barely legal": a 15% return, $60 billion gross, **$35 billion — about ¥4 trillion — net.**
- New York's fall forces the funds home. **Iron Partners abandons the bid** and sells out; Teikoku's syndicate ends with just under 35% of Furukawa Telecoms.

*5. The counter-stroke*
- Okazaki is told to keep ¥100 billion; he banks it "for next time." Eva: "I won't let there be a next time, Mister Okazaki." Angela has punched him. Toudou's scolding: in his place "I'd have just done it without her knowing."
- To put the president in her debt, Runa rescues what the crash endangered: the Moonlight Fund buys **Portercon**, America's number-two PC maker, for $14.5 billion.
- Members of Teikoku's syndicate, short of cash and facing paper losses, sell their Furukawa shares — some to Keika Holdings. Keika shelves its core-banking order in favour of a ¥250 billion, five-year data centre, and uses the work to mend fences with those firms.
- At Café Vesuna she hires the ousted American CEO, **Karin Viola**, to run what follows: on the three o'clock news, **Furukawa Telecoms and Shiyo Electric announce a business integration**, joined to Portercon — "a massive new computer company that spans both the United States and Japan." Keika holds 35% of Furukawa and 50% of Shiyo. "My main goal is cell phones."
- Teikoku Telephone answers by announcing a rescue of WCI in the United States, which the White House welcomes.

*6. The scoldings*
- **Kiyomaro**, to whom she bows as "the only one who could formally scold me": "You've now added a hundred thousand employees and easily over a million connected individuals. Did you understand that before you took action?" He gives her a politician's book and its motto: "'**Know when to quit.**' …In a way, it's how I managed to survive… I only pray that this tendency doesn't lead you to ruin." Looking at a photograph on his desk: "**Don't become like my big brother.**"
- **Nakamaro**: "Will you really be able to control it from the inside, Runa?… You're still a child. It's only natural for people to protect you at this age." She cries. He asks her to smile at his wedding in June.
- What she does not say aloud: "My goal was the revival of Japan's economy, and I never once considered the internal regulations of the Keika Group's companies. I was prepared to sell everything off if it meant making a profit." And, leaving: "I've deserted such good people… this country… this era."
- **Saitou Keiko**, the head maid: "You think a woman will be persuaded with reason? You're a child who's made life difficult for her parent. That alone is a perfectly good reason to scold you!" — until after midnight.

**Revelations**
- Runa's guardians — Tachibana, Ichijou, Nakamaro and Angela — conspired with a hostile corporation and with Washington to engineer her defeat, out of fear for her and of her. She learns of it only when Angela, interrogating Okazaki, lets it out.
- A single phone company can stop a megabank's computer systems with a boycott. That is the vulnerability; the data centre is the answer to it.
- She is being lined up for a dynastic marriage, and the "old man at the racetrack" was part of it.
- She deliberately detonated an American bankruptcy and shorted the market into it. In Volume 3 she had pointedly *not* shorted General Energy Online.
- The Keika Group "had no internal controls." One trading-company adventurer raised and levered $400 billion without its CEO or its CIA-trained secretary noticing.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** veto rights at one-third (blocking mergers, charter changes, share issues); third-party allotments and dilution; buybacks; convertible bonds as a deliberate poison pill; white knights; tax havens and layered laundering; "**Turnaround manager**… Runa buys the failed companies and industries altogether… This is why Wall Street looks at her and thinks, 'What the hell is she doing…?'"; **Suzuki Shoten**, whose failure "warped Japan's history"; the **Showa financial crisis**, in which "the Privy Council played a decisive role" and which led to "zaibatsu strengthening and the rise of the military"; **Kinugawa Onsen** as "a symbol of both the bubble and of the bubble's collapse"; the book is **Takemura Masayoshi's *Chiisakutemo Kirari to Hikaru Kuni Nippon*** ("A Small but Shining Japan"), "the source of the current opposition party's ideology," linked to Ishibashi Tanzan's "Small Japan" idea; "know when to quit" is **Laozi** — "he who knows sufficiency shall prosper."
- **The horse is Stay Gold** *(my identification; the details match exactly)*: fifty starts, a string of seconds and thirds, a win over Fantastic Light — the reigning World Series champion — in the 2001 Dubai Sheema Classic, and a first G1 in his last race, the Hong Kong Vase of 16 December 2001. He went on to sire Orfevre and Gold Ship — the "big things" Runa hints at. His own sire, the fashionable stallion she passes over, was Sunday Silence. The Derby line she quotes is a saying long attributed in Japan to Churchill and, as she says, apparently invented.
- **Lothringe = Habsburg-Lothringen** *(my identification)*: "Lothringen" is German for Lorraine; Karl and Franz are the dynasty's names; and the real head of the house, Otto von Habsburg, sat in the European Parliament for twenty years. **Altered:** in this world Austria restored the family's standing after the war.
- **Furukawa Telecoms = Fujitsu; Furukawa Automatic Machine Manufacturing = Fanuc; Teikoku Telephone = NTT.** Very high confidence (Fujitsu descends from Furukawa Electric, grew up as a supplier to the state telephone monopoly, and long held a large stake in Fanuc, the world leader in machine-tool controls). **Fictional:** the takeover fight itself.
- **Machishita Fund = the Murakami Fund** (village-above → town-below); its real founder, an ex-trade-ministry official, launched Japan's first hostile bid in January 2000 with the same slogan — companies belong to shareholders. **Iron Partners = Steel Partners**, the American activist fund that began hostile bids in Japan in December 2003 *(my identification)*. **Charles & Edward ≈ Merrill Lynch** (founders Charles Merrill and Edmund Lynch).
- **Shinden Bank = Ashikaga Bank.** High confidence: Tochigi's leading bank, ruined by loans to the Kinugawa hot-spring hotels and nationalised in November 2003. The name is a historian's joke — the Nitta (新田, also read "Shinden") were the Ashikaga clan's great rivals. **Altered:** here it is absorbed two years early without public money.
- **The regional banks are the real casualties of 1997–99** *(my identifications)*: Tokuyo City Bank of Sendai (failed 1997), Niigata Chuo (1999), Kofuku ("happiness" = *shiawase*) and Wakayama's Hanwa, Kokumin (1999) and Tokyo Sowa (1999). In the novel all survived the 1990s because of Runa — and are failing now.
- **Hope Memory = Elpida Memory** (the name is Greek for "hope"), the NEC–Hitachi memory venture; it went bankrupt in 2012. **Matsuyuki Electronics = Matsushita/Panasonic**; **Shiyo = Sanyo**. *Altered and telling:* the real Sanyo was dismembered and absorbed by Panasonic in 2009–11. Runa is performing that restructuring a decade early and on her own terms.
- **The American proxy fight is Hewlett-Packard and Compaq.** Real: HP's chief executive, Carly Fiorina, proposed buying Compaq in September 2001; the founders' heirs fought it; she **won** the shareholder vote in March 2002 and was forced out only in 2005. **Altered:** here the founder's side wins and she resigns. **Portercon = Compaq**, then the number-two PC maker; **Karin Viola = Carly Fiorina** (*fiore*, flower → viola).
- **WCI = WorldCom. Very high confidence.** Real: the second-largest US long-distance carrier, audited — like Enron — by Arthur Andersen; its chief resigned on 30 April 2002, an internal auditor exposed $3.8 billion of misbooked expenses on 25 June, and it filed the largest bankruptcy in American history on 21 July 2002. **Altered:** the novel moves the collapse to the end of 2001, has Runa manufacture the leak, and has the Japanese phone company rescue the wreck.
- **A measure of her effect:** the in-world Nikkei ends 2001 at 17,500. The real index closed that year at 10,542.
- **Real background:** "Galapagos syndrome" is the standard term for Japanese handsets that evolved to suit only the home market; Japan's consumer-electronics makers did collapse in roughly the decade Runa gives them.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **This is the first, gentle version of Chapter 9.** The people who love her build a cage and call it protection — and say so: "the cage protects the bird." It fails because the cage was made of money problems, and money is the one thing she can always find. What they taught her instead is that her own circle will act against her wishes "for her good." The state's version in Chapter 9 uses something she cannot buy.
- *Strongly supported:* **Volume 3's lesson, applied.** "I was almost eaten just by refusing to act… I would rather eat before I get eaten" (Vol. 3 Ch. 12). She had kept her hands clean over Enron; here she pulls the trigger on WorldCom herself and bets against the whole market. The series marks the cost at once — Eva's threat, Angela's fist, five scoldings in a row.
- *Strongly supported:* **"Know when to quit" is the chapter's counter-voice, and it is not new.** At Ryoan-ji in Vol. 3 Yuujirou read the same maxim off a stone basin and asked, "But what makes it enough?" Kiyomaro now gives it as his rule for survival and names the man who did not follow it — his brother, Runa's father. The book he hands her argues for a small, modest Japan; she is building the opposite.
- *Strongly supported:* **The control problem is now quantified.** A hundred thousand more employees; "we had the money, just not the manpower"; no internal controls; and Runa's own admission that she never thought about governing what she owns and would "sell everything off." Nakamaro's question — "Will you really be able to control it from the inside?" — goes unanswered.
- *Strongly supported:* **The horse is the thesis in miniature, and it cuts both ways.** She loves him for the losing; Franz says only results count; and the horse then wins. She weeps for "the story." Throughout the chapter she is choosing stories over results — the chestnut over the champion sire, a rescue of Japan's electronics industry over profit.
- *Plausible:* Her private admission — reviving Japan, not running Keika — is the clearest statement yet that she treats the group as an instrument. It is what makes her generous, and it is what makes her dangerous to the hundred thousand people inside it.
- *Plausible:* "I've deserted such good people… this country… this era" and "If I don't, everyone will turn in their graves" suggest that she is answering to the dead of her first life rather than to the living around her.
- *Plausible:* The adults fear a dictator; Okazaki wants a show. He is the only adult who treats her will as the point, and Toudou's remark — he would have acted "without her knowing" — shows that even her risk-takers assume the principal should be kept in the dark.

**Connections:** Furukawa Telecoms was her stated next target in Vol. 3 Chs. 10, 12 and 13. The merged company becomes Keika Electronics Union in Vol. 5 Ch. 2. Karin Viola and Angela are named as the group's future leaders in Ch. 12. The Iraq tip to Okazaki is the first sign of the plan exposed in Ch. 9. The marriage question returns in Ch. 5 (Tachibana) and Ch. 11 (the maids name the Hong Kong meeting for what it was; Eiichi hears of it). Kiyomaro's "know when to quit" answers Vol. 3 Ch. 10's Ryoan-ji scene and anticipates Koizumi's "can't you wait a little longer?" (Ch. 9).

## Chapter 3 — The White House's Nervous Smile

**Narrative position:** Third person, Washington, January 2002 — an underground briefing room, because "the Pentagon and White House [are] still recovering from the aftermath of being struck by planes." The American government reads Chapter 2 from the other side and decides what to do about its author. *(Read in full for the second edition; the first edition had two lines.)*

**What happens**
- The president has the report from Tokyo: "That young lady's underlings must be behind this. Why isn't anyone stopping her?" The vice president: "These are the selfish whims of a little girl."
- **The CIA's account:** several funds colluded in the Furukawa bid, which also threatened Shiyo; the Keika Group bought more Shiyo, "vengefully forced us to back off by secretly reporting on WCI's accounting fraud, crashed the New York stock market, and got the funds in their way to retreat."
- **Why the CIA covers for her.** Congress is asking how ECHELON and the agencies missed 11 September when the warning "end[ed] up in the hands of a trading company in one of the allied countries in the Far East." The director, a holdover appointed under the Democrats, survives because her tips made the agency look competent. "If he abandoned the young lady… it was very possible that the CIA itself would be abandoned by this administration."
- **Why the president smiles.** Congress is investigating his administration's payments to General Energy Online. Prosecuting WCI — also a donor — lets the White House say "we were the ones to dispose of the rotten apple." The Secretary of State: "The information she provided us was true, and it let us avoid a fatal blow."
- **How the leak was really sent** — a method Okazaki "never revealed, even to Angela": intermediaries, a USB drive plugged into a London internet-café computer, and a virus that mailed the message while an unwitting user browsed. The agencies "relied too heavily on ECHELON" and never found the human.
- **The president's question:** "If they could line all this up now, why didn't they expose General Energy Online back when all that was happening?" — and why not profit from 9/11? "They didn't know her motives." She is spending the winnings on rescuing Portercon and on political committees: "It doesn't make any sense."
- The director's answer, which satisfies the room: "The girl is generally our ally, and she only counterattacked when we were hostile toward her."
- **The WCI "rescue."** The attorney general: "we can't sell WCI Inc. to Teikoku Telephone Company." The president knows; the announcement alone steadied the market. (Narration: Teikoku was serious, and "would be completely defeated in an ugly battle with the Department of Justice.")
- **The decoy and the real move.** The director: she "took 10 billion dollars of profit and poured it into Bahrain, calling it infrastructure groundwork." Akamatsu is building a mining railway in Saudi Arabia from **Dammam to Hafar al-Batin**, with a branch to **King Khalid Military City** — a few dozen kilometres from the Iraqi border, with mercenaries budgeted as site guards. Everyone at the table reads the map the same way: "**You're going to invade Iraq, right? Then allow me to help you.**" It is "the earliest declaration of any party that they would participate in the war," and it frightens the president and vice president as "an even clearer display of precognition."
- **Afghanistan by proxy.** The Secretary of Defense: the private military companies cannot be withdrawn. America remembers how each soldier's death fed the anti-war movement in Vietnam; now "it was Japanese and Russian soldiers losing their lives in Afghanistan," with "technically retired" Indian soldiers — seeking revenge for the parliament attack — who "carried out multiple massacres." "**The girl's mercenary company was responsible for assuring their status as a PMC.**"
- The Treasury Secretary jokes that the "necessary expense" is the tens of billions lost in the crash. The Secretary of State: if they economise and "the Statue of Liberty gets taken out next," history will ask how they could be so stupid.
- **Florida, again.** The president: "I'm sitting here today thanks to a few thousand votes in Florida, but that was a gift from her company's extreme support." For the midterms she is building **PACs in swing states** — buying into failing local firms by third-party allotment so that they can sponsor committees — and the daily news of her rescues steadies the market.
- **The Iraq narrative.** "The White House was working on a plan to invade Iraq over 'weapons of mass destruction.' However, **they already knew that Iraq had no such weapons**… so their choice was between pressing forward or creating a fake cover story." Runa's discovery of the missing warheads "supported the story that the terrorists didn't steal the missiles and instead committed the 9/11 attacks with funding from Iraq." The narrative needs her cooperation — and Japan's.
- The president: "If we'll be paying for the lost money with blood, then let's make the best of it." Days later he names an "axis of evil."

**Revelations**
- Washington knows she triggered WCI and crashed its market, and has decided to tolerate it: she is useful to the agency, to the president's scandal management, and to the war.
- She has already — on her own initiative — begun building the logistics of the invasion of Iraq. This is the plan Chapter 9 takes away from her.
- Her private military company is the legal umbrella under which Indian irregulars are committing massacres in Afghanistan.
- The Iraq–9/11 link is, inside the novel too, a story the White House assembles knowing Iraq has no such weapons — out of evidence Runa supplied.
- She helped decide the 2000 election (stated here by its beneficiary) and is investing in the 2002 midterms.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the intelligence community; **ECHELON**; **Deep Throat**; US antitrust law; **swing states** (Ohio, Virginia, Pennsylvania, Florida).
- **Real:** the "axis of evil" State of the Union address (29 January 2002); the CIA director of the time was a holdover from the previous, Democratic administration; the intelligence agencies did face a congressional inquiry into their pre-attack failures; Enron's and WorldCom's leaders were both major political donors; King Khalid Military City, near Hafar al-Batin, was a coalition base in the Gulf War; a causeway links Bahrain to Saudi Arabia near Dammam; no weapons of mass destruction were found in Iraq, and the claimed link between Iraq and the attacks was false.
- **Altered:** the White House was not struck in reality; WorldCom's collapse came in mid-2002 and no Japanese company bid for it; the Saudi railway and its Japanese builder are invented.
- **A correction to the Volume 3 map.** That map said the novel turns the Iraq War's false pretext into "an in-world truth." This chapter shows otherwise: the administration knows Iraq has no such weapons and builds the story anyway. What the novel adds is a real loose-warhead scare for the story to be built from — and a protagonist who supplied it.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **This is the adult world deciding to keep using a child.** Every man in the room has a reason to leave her alone — a job, a scandal, an election, a war. Volume 3's question was whether adults would protect her; here the most powerful adults on earth conclude that she is too convenient to stop. That is the situation Koizumi breaks in Chapter 9, and it is why his refusal reads as integrity.
- *Strongly supported:* **Her foreknowledge has become a commitment.** The railway is not a prediction but an offer, and the Americans understand it as one. The narrator's phrase is exact: "she'd also overcommitted to the US's war and the reasons behind it."
- *Strongly supported:* **The president's question is the reader's.** Why not short Enron, or 9/11? Volume 3 answered it — she would not profit from those — and Chapter 2 has just shown the scruple failing. To the Americans the inconsistency is noise; to the reader it is the record of a line being crossed.
- *Strongly supported:* **Narratives are manufactured in this book by the same method at every scale.** The narration draws the parallel itself: the CIA "making up a narrative to control the definitive outcome" is "similar… to the girl's recent outrageous business endeavors." A fake whistleblower e-mail and a fake casus belli are the same kind of object.
- *Plausible:* The massacres under her company's licence are the "difference in perception" of Vol. 3 Ch. 2 — a top layer that cannot control what its armed bottom layer does — now happening in her name. She is not in the room, and nothing indicates she has been told.

**Connections:** Vol. 2 Ch. 8 (Florida "turns red"); Vol. 3 Ch. 6 (the warheads) and Ch. 8 (the column about private military companies and a massacre in Afghanistan, which this chapter moves closer to fact); Ch. 2 (the WCI leak); Ch. 9 (the Bahrain work is exposed and surrendered) and Ch. 12 (the railway is sold); Vol. 5 Chs. 5–6 (what her plan for the war actually contained). *Back to Vol. 2 (added after its reread):* Ch. 5 (the railway toward Iraq is ordered in February 2000) and Ch. 8 (the Secretary of State's first "thank you — now avoid trouble"; the Gulf War commander's condition for running her military company).

## Chapter 4 — To Admit Defeat Is to Grow as a Person

**Narrative position:** Third person on the three boys at Avanti, early 2002, with Runa arriving at the end. A short companion piece to Vol. 3 Ch. 13: the same table, the same three, one bet later. *(Read in full for the second edition; the first edition had a one-line placeholder.)*

**What happens**
- The boys' Furukawa Telecoms shares have paid: **¥8 billion**. The merged Furukawa–Shiyo–Portercon company, to be called **Keika Electronics Union**, is the talk of the world.
- Eiichi, who "hated to lose," can manage only a dry laugh: "We came out with a win… but Runa's still sprinting ahead of us." Yuujirou: "She has banks, logistics, railroads, and now electronics."
- The Machishita Fund tried to recruit Mitsuya, and only Mitsuya.
- Eiichi's grandfather, the craftsman-founder, scolded him: "**Stop playin' around with money.**" Eiichi concedes the point: "I would have to put the entire Teia Group on the line to bet the same amount of money as Runa. That's not something I'm interested in." The narration: "admitting defeat and giving up on Keikain Runa were two separate things."
- **The next business.** From a newspaper item — Keika Holdings has shelved its core-banking system — Eiichi infers that her whole campaign was a gamble (she postponed "the infrastructure for all of her funds") and that she has not thought about the system since. He proposes an agency of core-banking engineers hired cheaply from post-bubble America, running backup servers for financial institutions.
- Mitsuya refines it: have the Americans build the foundations — libraries, frameworks, databases, security; Java now makes enterprise systems open, and "the patent on public key encryption expired in 2000" — and leave the business logic to Japanese vendors. Yuujirou adds the time zones: staff on the US East Coast can take over maintenance when Tokyo goes home.
- Mitsuya: "We're just assuming that Keikain will cooperate with us." Eiichi: "I'm sure. Win or lose, we'll never catch up to Runa. **She'll find some other boy to cultivate.** I just haven't come up with any ideas for that yet." All three fall silent.
- Runa walks in. "What were you boys talking about?" — "How to use you to make money." Yen signs appear in her eyes: she offers half the capital if the servers are up by spring, admits she "took on a major gamble" and has not looked at the system, and suggests they simply **buy** an East Coast IT firm rather than build one. Eiichi "realized exactly why Runa had been expanding her enterprise so widely: she saw it as buying time with money."
- "How much did you end up betting?" — "Ummm, somewhere in the trillions?"
- **Flash-forward:** TIG Backup Systems buys an East Coast firm with her financing. Its first big customer is not Keika but **Honami Bank**, after "a massive system failure in the spring." First-year revenue ¥42.5 billion, net profit ¥10.5 billion.

**Revelations**
- The boys can now read Runa's position from a press release — and know more about the state of her bank's systems than she does.
- Eiichi has stopped trying to match her and started positioning himself where she will need him.
- Their fear is not losing to her but being replaced beside her.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** Mitsuya is reading ***Hyouka*** by Yonezawa Honobu (Kadokawa Sneaker Bunko, 2001).
- **Real:** the RSA public-key patent expired in September 2000; Java-based enterprise systems were displacing proprietary mainframe software at exactly this time; the dot-com collapse did put American engineers on the market cheaply.
- **Honami Bank's spring failure = Mizuho's.** On 1 April 2002, the first day the three merged banks operated as Mizuho, its systems broke down — cash machines failed nationwide and millions of transfers were delayed or duplicated. It remains Japan's best-known banking IT disaster, and its cause was the one this chapter describes: three banks' systems imperfectly joined.
- **Fictional:** TIG Backup Systems.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The title is addressed to Runa over the boys' heads.** Eiichi "hated to lose," admits a defeat, and grows by it — in the same weeks in which Runa is told by five adults to know when to quit and does not. The volume's defeat (Ch. 9) is the lesson she is made to take; this is what taking it voluntarily looks like.
- *Strongly supported:* **Founder against acquirer.** The narration marks the difference: Eiichi thinks of building a company, Runa of buying one. His grandfather's rule and her method are two capitalisms — craft and patience against capital and speed — and the Teia line is the one the book lets win quietly (¥10.5 billion of profit from other people's failure to do the boring work).
- *Strongly supported:* **Her neglected plumbing is everyone else's opportunity.** Teikoku Telephone attacked it (Ch. 2); the boys monetise it; Honami is wrecked by its own. The core-banking system is the series' recurring image of what conglomerates built by merger forget.
- *Plausible:* ***Hyouka*** is a deliberate choice. Its mystery turns on a student who was made the scapegoat for a school protest and afterwards remembered as its willing hero — a quiet "sacrifice" the crowd rewrote as a legend. Chapter 9 ends with Koizumi asking Runa not to let "the people sacrifice you for this country's prosperity," and the game's Runa loses a school reform vote. *(My reading; the glossary gives only the title.)*
- *Plausible:* "Some other boy to cultivate" shows how the boys read her patronage: as something she bestows, on Okazaki-like protégés, and could bestow elsewhere.

**Connections:** Vol. 3 Ch. 13 (the pact; the first exit); Ch. 2 (the shelved system; Kiyomaro's "know when to quit"); Ch. 9 (Runa's own defeat, and Eiichi's phone call); Vol. 5 (Honami's troubles continue in the news chapters).

## Chapter 5 — The Lady's Daily Life: First Half of 2002

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV (one vignette leans on Amane Mio's view), January to June 2002. The volume's longest chapter: twelve vignettes that look like the usual school-life interlude. Under the cake and board games it holds the rehearsal of the game's ending, the two frankest diagnoses anyone gives her, her bargain with the American president, and the first mention that the game's Keika lost its pharmaceutical core to "a poor merger." *(Read in full for the second edition; the first edition had one line.)*

**What happens**

*1. The student-council election*
- The academy's cliques: the **Courtiers** (nobles — rank, little money), the **Lords** (zaibatsu children), the **Stewards** (politicians' children — fewest, best connected) and the **Heralds** (bureaucrats' children — grades, no connections), roughly 3:2:2:3. The balance shifts in junior high, when "servants" may enrol.
- In the game, Yuujirou began "at the very bottom of this political clique due to his family's scandal" and climbed to the top.
- Eiichi will run for elementary-division president. The son of a major opposition Diet member demands a "primary" against Yuujirou, campaigning on "eliminating school cliques and reforming the student council." The opposition and the press have cameras at the gate, ready to call the Quartet "more of the same Ancien Régime."
- Runa: "We were in the era of TV choosing the prime minister… **The future heroine of the game, Takanashi Mizuho, would be on the receiving end of considerable plot armor thanks to those mass media companies. As someone unchanged from the old times, I would be defeated by Takanashi Mizuho, the embodiment of the average citizen. I now understood that I was the queen with her head on the chopping block.**"
- Her answer is procedural: "Let's ignore them." The president is chosen by the six class-committee members and appoints the other officers himself; the challenger is not on a committee. (Runa sits on the ceremony committee, which runs the election.)
- Then, in the cafeteria and "in a loud voice," she hands Asagiri Kaoru a cheque for **¥100 million** to refurbish the Courtiers' Hall. "Smacking someone with a stack of bills is most effective with a captive audience." She has checked: no school rule forbids buying an election. It is "a premium payment" — break the agreement and "you'll need to raise more money than that."
- Kazuki Shiori shows "a brief glimmer of disgust." Tachibana: "**Just try not to imitate your grandfather in all the worst ways.**"
- Result — president Teia Eiichi; vice president Keikain Runa; secretary Izumikawa Yuujirou; treasurer Gotou Mitsuya.
- Days later the opposition's son stands on a mandarin crate at the gate — "We must make a change!" — with press cameras around him. Runa remembers a game image of **Mizuho giving a speech on this spot**: "(—Don't you find it strange?! We're all humans here! And yet…)" She walks past. "You're not going to bring me down. Takanashi Mizuho is the only one who'll do that." Next day's weekly: "Arrogant Duke's Daughter Ignores Inspired Speech by Opposition's Son."

*2. The photo exhibition*
- The photographer — named here, **Ishikawa Nobumitsu** — mounts a show of every picture he has taken of her: "What can I get for you, Your Little Majesty?"; the sports day; Fuchigami's residence ("The moment when Hokkaido's future was decided"); the Shikoku Shinkansen pilgrim poster; the maids at Café Vesuna ("Her Little Majesty's military parade"); Mount Takao; Erika's garden.
- Last, the picture that "swept all the awards": the towers on a screen, Koizumi holding her. "It was the only one I couldn't add to."
- Ishikawa, serious for once: "You're going to become a great lady, I guarantee it. So be happy — **despair doesn't suit great ladies.**" Then the nude joke, and a shin kick. He settles for a wedding dress. "You know I can't promise you that!"

*3. The idol scout, and Tachibana on television*
- After a free mini-concert at Teisei (Micaëla's aria; a song from a late-night anime "about an eternal farewell"), a rough, middle-aged scout pushes through her guards: she could be "a diva of the Heisei era." "I'm sorry. I make more money than an idol." He had no idea who she was.
- Tachibana: the networks want her on screen "twenty-four hours a day." And power inside the media lies with "**the middle class**" — the working layer, which an agreement among executives cannot bind. He cites the Hokkaido food company ruined by a poisoning and a beef-labelling scandal, now under Teisei's wing.
- Runa: between "the nail that sticks out gets hammered down" and "the demon of 'reading the room,'" Japan removes what is foreign to it. "**Looking at it that way, the 'villainess' known as Keikain Runa was also a sacrifice, taken down by Japanese culture and its systems.**" The media know she is the Keika Group's fixer, "that makes them want to see your downfall all the more," and the public "took joy and delight in watching the downfalls of others."
- The way out, says Tachibana: "**You just have to get married, my lady.**" Downfalls and marriages both end the coverage. "If you want to step down, you can tell me any time. Toudou-san, Ichijou, Angela, your father, your brother, and I all want you to be happy."
- That night she realises she has been singing the farewell song "to my future self."

*4. A Sunday*
- Breakfast with two maids so she does not eat alone (Eva stands apart); a run around the palace moat with Anisha and guards; a voice lesson. Her coach, grieving that "one of the leading coloratura sopranos in Japan" will not make music her life, offers letters for Vienna, Paris or Rome. Window-shopping in Ginza; dinner; a book.

*5. Two board games*
- At a party the "three sisters" and Mio play a settlement game; Hotaru wins. Then, with the boys, a negotiation game about pre-1914 Europe that **Angela** supplied and referees. Runa draws the **Russian Empire**, Eiichi Germany, Yuujirou Austria-Hungary, Mitsuya France, Asuka Britain, Mio Italy, Hotaru the Ottomans.
- Runa agrees with Hotaru to keep the Black Sea neutral. Hotaru sends her fleet in on the first move — and wins that game too. "Hotaru-chan was the last person we should ever become enemies with."

*6. Professor Kanbe*
- After the gamble, Kiyomaro and Nakamaro have asked the professor — at Erika's suggestion — to assess her. She sits in on his "sociology of genius" lecture: a genius is "the first person who created the opportunity for an advancement"; Japan produces few because a disaster-prone country rewards preparing as a group, and "the nail that sticks up gets hammered down"; what Japan values is the able, hard-working ordinary person who develops someone else's breakthrough.
- In his office he drops the commission: "personally, I don't want to pick out those traits." His finding: "you are a 'gifted' child… a normal genius." His advice to the family: "**have you leave the country. This place is too small for your talents.**"
- "Do you know what your biggest problem is?" — "Is it that I'm gifted?" — "**No. It's that you're a child. You can avoid taking responsibility because you're still a child… The adults around you will cover for it.** You have infinite time and possibilities. I don't know why you're in such a rush… At this rate, you may become the kind of person who can achieve anything, only to leave nothing behind."
- Runa: "I think that's so arrogant… I'm not so conceited as to think of myself as a genius. Nor would I say that I have infinite time." — "You're saying that right now is the only time that you can be a genius?" — "Correct." He smiles.

*7. Salt Lake City*
- February: the Winter Olympics, "thoroughly" used by politicians after the attacks. Having "completely covered for the president" over GEO and WCI, Runa lays flowers at Ground Zero and works the private charity events as "the daughter of a dukedom, princess of House Romanov, and owner of the major fund" — "all of these were things Americans loved."
- Meanwhile Okazaki sells: a logistics complex in **Bahrain** beside the US base — storage, runways, housing, a power plant, the desalination plant once meant for California, guarded by private military companies — built for $10 billion and to be bought on completion, at 1.25 times cost, by a Bermuda shell that will run it for the American military; plus the Saudi mining railway from Dammam to Hafar al-Batin with a branch to King Khalid Military City.
- **The president walks into her waiting room** ("it looks like I got the wrong room"). "Are you interested in setting the world in motion?" — "No. I want to change the world, not set it in motion… I want to change the history that will follow me, Mr. President. I'm sure you don't want to bring about a second Vietnam."
- He pats her head: "I know that you tried everything you could." Then: "**When do you see us starting the war?**" — "The spring of next year." Do not spend a victory on the midterms; take the loss, build a Gulf War-sized army and a coalition, get Congress's approval.
- She will keep the midterm loss "within acceptable limits": $2.5 billion in political donations and $10 billion of rescues for American firms. "**If I lost in Japan, I would make it up in the United States.** If the mass media and Prime Minister Koizumi's policies were going to get in my way, I would simply have the US put pressure on them."
- She notes that wartime will concentrate power in Koizumi's hands. Her last word to the president: "The world simply won't allow a child to set it in motion." Then she is on American television "singing the tune of the next war over and over and over again."

*8. Portercon*
- In Texas, Karin Viola interrogates her new managers. Her rule: "Be sure you know what you don't know. Then find someone who does know and ask them."
- The structure: Furukawa Telecoms and Shiyo merge first; Portercon then buys the result and ends with just over 30%. If the other shareholders and Furukawa's managers combine — probably with Teikoku Telephone behind them — "I had no way of protecting her." Karin asks for only three board seats: start small, attack after results.
- Karin on the handset plan: it "can fight on a global level. However, it's still on too small of a scale to take over the world" — that would mean owning carriers. Runa: "Yes, I know that."
- Portercon is reorganised at speed. "No one was laid off."

*9. The girls' tea party*
- At the tower's rooftop garden: Asuka, Hotaru, Asagiri Kaoru, Machiyoi Sanae, Kurimori Shizuka, Takahashi Akiko — and, unexpectedly, **Kazuki Shiori** (here "Katsuki," a viscount house and a Keikain branch family). Runa hopes to close the distance; Shiori answers the garden's beauty in a voice "directed… at someone else."
- **Tachibana Yuka** hosts; she "would attend school with me as my servant starting next year." "This was something right out of a normal life. I realized just how much I'd lost sight of myself."

*10. Sixth grade*
- April opens "in a state of chaos thanks to a certain bank's system failure." The new council, "the most powerful one in recent years," turns the elementary council "from a subcontractor for the upper grades into an independent entity." Runa is drafted as president of the girls' kendo club, which has swollen since she won the city tournament.
- **The exams.** At Kanbe's suggestion she is preparing for the junior-high equivalency and university-entrance qualification tests; his university will introduce grade-skipping to have her. His reason: "when you become a normal person, you'll need time to think." Hers: "If the unthinkable happened and my life did end up going the same way it did in the otome game, it wasn't like I would be going to college at all."
- Mitsuya assumes she will go abroad. "I truly do love this country… I'd been a victim of unfair treatment, yet I still loved this country all the same" — for "the food, miso soup, the four seasons, and light novels." He himself cannot skip: the bureaucracy's path runs through Tokyo University's law faculty.

*11. A "fine young lady"*
- Saitou Keiko's definition, from Coco Chanel: "One's shortcomings may be forgiven, but never her negligence." Simplicity; machines and hired help for the chores, so that time goes to the next task; and perfume, with a handwritten card, for every maid's birthday — "A woman who doesn't wear perfume has no future." Runa waits for the day she receives hers.

*12. The wedding*
- June, a small church in Karuizawa: Nakamaro marries Asagiri Sakurako.
- President Iwazaki takes Runa aside: **Hatabe Pharmaceuticals** has approached Keika-Iwazaki Pharma for a merger. "**In the original game, Keika Pharma ended up losing its power due to a poor merger, but we could avoid that now.**" They could buy up the stock and delist instead. "I owe you a lot more than this."
- The decision will be Nakamaro's first as heir: "I have every intention of bowing my head to you when the time comes."
- Sakurako gives half her bouquet each to Kaoru and Runa. "I'd spent my past life just desperately trying to survive, and I knew a future of ruin awaited me in this world. I looked at the bridal garments from afar, feeling certain that I would never wear them myself." On the train: "Will I really be able to wear that kind of dress someday too…?" Tachibana: "I'm certain you will, my lady." "But who will be the one at my side on that day?"

**Revelations**
- The game's council line-up already exists in the sixth grade: Eiichi president, Runa vice president, Yuujirou secretary, Mitsuya treasurer.
- Runa understands how she will lose: to "the average citizen," carried by television — and she has just watched a dry run of the method and beaten it with money and procedure.
- She reads her own villainy sociologically, as a society's sacrifice of what does not fit.
- She personally told the American president when to invade Iraq, and pledged $12.5 billion to carry him through the midterms.
- She is quietly building an exit — qualifications and early university — for the case that the game's ending comes anyway.
- In the game, Keika's pharmaceutical core was lost through a bad merger; a version of that merger is now on the table.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** primaries; the song is "**Canta Per Me**" from the anime ***Noir*** (2001); her book is ***Kino no Tabi*** (Sigsawa Keiichi); the games are ***Settlers of Catan*** and ***Diplomacy*** — "a game of negotiation that shows you just how scary the creatures known as humans can be"; the 1993 US Department of Education definition of "gifted"; the two Japanese equivalency examinations; the light novel is ***Maria-sama ga Miteru: Rainy Blue***; the church is Kyu-Karuizawa Chapel; and a note that Japan's well-known route to the elite through Tokyo University's law faculty "has the benefit of a stabilizing effect on society."
- **Real:** the Salt Lake City Winter Olympics (8–24 February 2002), held under heavy security and opened with the flag recovered from the World Trade Center; the US Navy's Fifth Fleet base in Bahrain; a sitting president's party usually losing seats at the midterms; the Iraq War beginning in spring 2003, as she advises; the quotations attributed to Coco Chanel.
- **For comparison:** Runa advises the president to "accept a loss in the election." In reality his party *gained* seats in November 2002 — and it does in this world too, with her money behind it (Vol. 5 Ch. 2). The real war was fought with a far smaller force than the Gulf War's: the "limited number of troops" plan she argues against here.
- **The Hokkaido food company = Snow Brand** *(my identification)*: its milk poisoned more than ten thousand people in 2000, and in January 2002 its meat subsidiary was caught relabelling imported beef as domestic. In reality the subsidiary was dissolved; here Teisei takes it in.
- **The bank system failure = Mizuho, 1 April 2002** (see Ch. 4).
- **Hatabe Pharmaceuticals = Tanabe Seiyaku** *(my identification; 田 "paddy" → 畑 "field")*. Mitsubishi's pharmaceutical arm did absorb Tokyo Tanabe in 1999 and merged with Tanabe itself in 2007 to form Mitsubishi Tanabe Pharma. The novel brings that merger forward five years.
- **Portercon's Texas headquarters** fits Compaq, of Houston.
- **Plausible (my identification):** Ishikawa Nobumitsu nods to **Shinoyama Kishin** (篠山紀信 → 石川信光, mountain to river), the star photographer of idols and celebrity nudes. The "actress who retires at the peak of her career and marries a prince" is Grace Kelly.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The election is the game's ending in miniature, and Runa wins it the old way.** A reformer's son, cameras, a speech at the gate about equality — the same staging, she notes, as Mizuho's scene. She answers with the rulebook and a cheque. It works because the electorate is six children. It is also exactly the conduct — money, back rooms, privilege — that the reform movement of the game exists to end, and Tachibana names its model: her grandfather.
- *Strongly supported:* **She now explains her downfall as a social mechanism, not a plot.** "The embodiment of the average citizen" defeats the one "unchanged from the old times"; the villainess is "a sacrifice, taken down by Japanese culture and its systems." This is the vocabulary of Chapter 9's dream and of Koizumi's last plea ("don't… let the people sacrifice you").
- *Strongly supported:* **Kanbe says it before Koizumi does.** "Your biggest problem… is that you're a child. You can avoid taking responsibility." The prime minister's "you simply aren't qualified" is the same finding from a man with the power to act on it. Runa hears the professor and hears only the word "infinite."
- *Strongly supported:* **Her reply is the truest thing she says about herself.** She has no infinite time: the first life ended early, and this one has an appointment in 2008. "Right now is the only time" is foreknowledge speaking — and it is why "know when to quit" cannot reach her.
- *Strongly supported:* **"If I lost in Japan, I would make it up in the United States."** She decides to use Washington as leverage over her own prime minister. Chapter 9 turns on the fact that Washington and Tokyo agree instead.
- *Strongly supported:* **Everyone offers her an exit.** Marriage (Tachibana), emigration (Kanbe), a conservatoire (her coach), stepping down "any time." She takes none, and prepares the one the others do not know about — examinations for a life after ruin.
- *Plausible:* The second board game is a seating plan. The Romanov heiress plays Russia; the girl from a house of exorcists breaks a Black Sea pact and wins; the referee is the CIA. Angela chose a game in which promises are unenforceable — weeks after breaking one of her own.
- *Plausible:* The reading list fits the mood: a traveller who never stays (*Kino*), a song of permanent farewell, a novel remembered for its unbearable cliffhanger. She is consuming stories about leaving.
- *Plausible:* Keiko's Chanel is the household's version of Runa's method — buy time with money, delegate the chores — given by the woman who scolded her for it until midnight. Perfume is the house's rite of adulthood; Runa has one bottle from Tachibana and Angela (Vol. 3 Ch. 2) and is still waiting for Keiko's.

**Connections:** The council line-up and Mizuho's speech return in Ch. 9's dream. Kanbe's diagnosis → Koizumi's (Ch. 9). The Bahrain complex and the railway → exposed in Ch. 9, sold in Ch. 12. The president's question → Ch. 10. The Hatabe merger → Vol. 5 Ch. 4 (Keika-Iwazaki-Hatabe Pharma). Tachibana Yuka's enrolment → Vol. 5 Ch. 9. ## Chapter 6 — The Keika Group's Reorganization

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV after a narrated political summary; spring to early summer 2002. The corporate centre of the volume: the "one-woman company" is turned into institutions, every lieutenant names the date he will leave, and Runa keeps buying anyway. *(Read in full for the second edition; the first edition had two lines.)*

**What happens**

*The political weather*
- "**Prime Minister Koizumi has good luck.**" A spring of scandals takes out his enemies and critics rather than him: the party secretary-general is ousted in a reshuffle; members suspected of embezzling secret funds leave the party; the opposition's investigators resign under suspicion of their own; an independent wins the Yokohama mayoralty; and Katou, his "sworn friend and rival," resigns over a scandal.
- In the reshuffle **Izumikawa becomes Foreign Minister**, charged with "normalizing" the ministry; **Yanagitani**, the Izumikawa faction's financial-affairs minister, is removed and **Takenaga** takes the portfolio as well, promising to finish the bad debts and to fight "the resistance forces" in his own party.
- The megabanks open; **Teisan Bank** in Fukuoka fails under a strict Financial Services Agency inspection and, with other regional banks, runs to Keika Holdings. Attention turns to the dissolving supermarket chain Taiei and to the general contractors — which Keika wants to revive with money and Koizumi wants as "a keystone in their reforms."
- The economy sags after the American IT crash, and holds up mainly because of "the massive railway work being done with the Keika Group at its center."

*Buying a Buddha, and three railways*
- **Fukui.** Come to buy a failing local railway, Runa sees a giant Buddha built by "the taxi king of the Showa era," now surrounded by a ghost town and bubble debts, and buys the temple too: "If they forgot to add a soul to the Buddha statue, we need to be the ones to put it in."
- **Osaka.** With the Shikoku Shinkansen finished, western Japan's contractors have nothing to build. Keika starts the **Naniwasuji line** (about ¥400 billion) — "the amount needed to pressure the Osaka Diet members."
- Tachibana: if they oppose the prime minister, should they not force him out? Runa, vehemently: "**Definitely not! Then the opposition party will get all the power!!**" She remembers what the opposition coalition did when it last governed; with single-seat districts and television it could now take far more. Koizumi's unspoken threat to his own party — "If you drag me down, I'll break you up" — binds her too.
- **Nagoya.** Keika Railway takes on three freight lines (about ¥200 billion) to serve the Teia Group's home ground and the airport under construction, with the Kyoto Protocol and the Aichi Expo as public reasons. Eiichi, at the ceremony: "You know, I don't hate this side of you."

*The first loss*
- **AIRHO** lost ¥7.4 billion in 2001 as travel collapsed after the attacks and will lose about ¥8.8 billion in 2002, against ¥7.2 billion of hotel profit — pushing "the first company that I directly controlled" into the red. The hotels want it cut loose; Runa decides to move it under Keika Railway.

*From one woman to a structure*
- "The Keika Group was currently nothing more than a one-woman company with me at the top. I also generally operated under the motto '**reign, but don't rule**.'" Every decision still needs a report to her; the presidents' council is "a social gathering"; and "the government is considering dissolving and reorganizing the Keika Group."
- Tachibana on why no company asks for independence: "you're the most convenient person to have on top… You reign without ruling, yet you still protect the workers' employment… and even guide them onto the right paths… **However, for our sake — and for yours, my lady — it's about time for that course to come to an end.**"
- **Listing Keika Holdings.** To stop it being the group's institutional bank, half its shares must leave the fund. The compromise Ichijou recites to a room of seven (Runa, Kiyomaro, Nakamaro, Tachibana, Toudou, Angela): 10% to a foundation the Keikain family controls; 10% each to Akamatsu and Keika Railway; the Moonlight Fund keeps 19.9%; the rest is sold to the public. The proceeds — "a few trillion yen" — are already earmarked for railways.
- **Ichijou** asks Angela to become an independent director and build the securities and insurance side into "Japan's first investment bank." Of his own post: "In the end, I haven't been able to find a successor." He will become president and give the chief executive's job to whichever faction wins.
- **Nakamaro resigns** from the bank's board: the former city-bank men have used the listing "to eliminate the Keikain family's influence." He returns to Keika-Iwazaki Pharma.
- Runa offers the fund's money for the coming pharmaceutical merger. Kiyomaro says one word — "**Runa.**" — and then: "That job belongs to Nakamaro and me. If you involve yourself in every little thing, you'll have no time for yourself. Isn't that why all of this started in the first place?" She blushes: "Was I always butting into things without the slightest thought?"
- **A second pillar.** An unlisted holding company under Keika Railway takes the hotels (with AIRHO), the Teisei department-store group, the ferries, the gated communities and the Kinugawa resort. Railways stay private: "Railroad projects are long-term endeavors. They can't be visualized from quarterly reports."
- **Tachibana's term:** "We'll organize the system and pass the baton again in three years." He will choose a successor for loyalty. "You'll still need at least thirty more years before you can lead… the Keika Group itself." Runa: "Live a long life so that you can keep on supporting me." — "Please don't forget that I'm farther along than you."
- He hands her a blank sheet for a corporate vision. She writes: "**Connecting your present to the future.**"
- Her three realisations: "Everyone would leave someday"; they are still short of people; and "I had even fewer people with me in the game. No, I was all alone in the first place."

*Three more companies*
- The government wants the trading houses consolidated. Akamatsu is to absorb two weak ones, **Teisen Ishii** and **Teimen Corporation** (¥2.75 trillion of receivables and loans, much of it Southeast Asian). Runa: "Fine, then. Let's eat them up" — it also delays Akamatsu's own listing.
- The character for "cotton" in Teimen's name reminds her of **Kanegana Textiles**, about to fail over accounting fraud: its cosmetics arm propped up heavy losses until market-value accounting made the cover-up worse; debts ¥350 billion. She adds it to the list, telling Okazaki, "I'm a girl, and I'm old enough to be interested in makeup now" — and keeping to herself that "we were on the verge of coming face-to-face with hell."
- The four companies will share a holding company, sort several hundred subsidiaries, merge in three years and then list. The cosmetics business — the real prize — goes to Akamatsu; housing to Keika Developments; pharmaceuticals to Keika-Iwazaki; food and retail to Teisei.
- **Toudou** asks to rename the merged company "**Keika Corp**": employees are uneasy that the group's flagship trader does not carry the name. His own term ends with the merger; a successor will come from inside, and Okazaki must wait a turn. Okazaki: once his "adventurous spirit" fades, "I'll probably become a loyal subject." Runa: "If I hadn't met my downfall by that point, I would make sure to rely on him."

*The boys, a data centre and a teacher*
- TIG Backup Systems is thriving on Honami Bank's breakdown. Karin Viola wants to buy Runa's half — it would lift her first results as chief executive of Keika Electronics Union and give her a foothold in a company already at war with itself. Her offer values TIG at ten times what was invested, on the assumption of ¥10 billion of profit a year. (Runa had put in her half from her personal account "so as not to bother anyone.")
- Runa declares herself neutral. Eiichi brings a different question: a Teia joint venture — "the first large-scale LAN service in Japan," about ¥70 billion in debt — has "come crying" to him. He would like to save it and thinks Runa could use its data centre for her unified cards.
- **Eiichi's creed.** He will not think about running Teia "until I get my license and can drive." "Watching Runa has made me realize she has the foresight of the gods, but I can't say the same about myself. If I just kept rolling the dice over and over again like that, I'd go bankrupt in the end… **I would be betting the lives of the Teia Group workers and their families.**" "We've successfully created a company twice now. That's why I think we need to show that we can maintain our success." And: "Someday, I want to see the same heights as you."
- Runa phones Karin, who arrives within the hour: "**You need to buy this company. If you don't, then I will.**" Data centres "sort it, analyze it, and sell it. They're gold mines." Some 40 million of Japan's 140 million people will carry Keika's unified card; their purchase records alone repay the investment. The venture failed because "they built a birdhouse, but never got the birds."
- Eiichi will sell half of TIG and borrow the rest, but not give up control — "That's an order from my grandfather." He says they founded the company to see what Runa sees. Karin: "I don't mean to be rude, but **my lady is the investor, not the president.**" Eiichi: "…Ah."
- Runa's settlement: Karin takes half and may "boss around the four of us." Karin: "My tuition fee is quite high, children."

*Runa in the machine room*
- With a lunchbox and a guest pass, Runa walks into Honami Bank's headquarters as a subcontractor's niece; nobody recognises her. "When infiltrating a Japanese organization, generally the outer layer was impenetrable, while security inside was laughably weak."
- She is TIG's "vice president of operations," come because Honami's managers and Furukawa Telecoms' project manager are driving the subcontractors into the ground. Honami's three predecessor banks never agreed on one system and joined three with relay computers; Furukawa's part in that started the trouble, and its managers are now shifting blame downward.
- In the meeting room the project manager shouts, "What idiot brought this brat in here?!" She hands him her card, then her phone — with Karin on the line. He drops it.
- TIG withdraws from Honami's main system; Furukawa Telecoms follows, at a cost of nearly ¥10 billion, and is put to work on Keika Holdings' own system instead. "Let's get rid of this system and build a real one."

**Revelations**
- The government is weighing the break-up of the Keika Group; the reorganisation is partly a pre-emptive defence.
- Runa's four oldest lieutenants now have exit dates: Ichijou (at the listing, no successor), Nakamaro (already leaving the bank), Tachibana and Toudou (three years).
- She prefers Koizumi in office to any opposition government, and will not help bring him down.
- She put ¥6 billion of pocket money into her classmates' company without telling anyone. *(The text prints "sixty billion"; the "ten times" return on a ¥60 billion valuation shows it should be six.)*
- In this world Japan's population is 140 million, and 40 million of them are expected to carry a Keika identity-and-payment card.
- In the game she was "all alone."

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary — "the spring theater," with real names:** Ōhashi Kyosen (opposition upper-house member, resigned), **Tanaka Makiko** (dismissed as foreign minister), **Suzuki Muneo** (resigned his Diet post and left the party), Tsujimoto Kiyomi (resigned her seat), Nakada Hiroshi (elected mayor of Yokohama as an independent), **Katō Kōichi** (resigned his seat). All between January and April 2002. Also glossed: the Kyoto Protocol; quarterly results ("you can only see what's right in front of you"); "shareholders' eyes"; business cards.
- **Real, and moved:** Koizumi's financial-services minister, Yanagisawa Hakuo, was replaced by Takenaka Heizō — who kept his economics post — in the reshuffle of 30 September 2002. **Yanagitani = Yanagisawa** (swamp → valley). The novel brings the change forward to spring.
- **Altered:** the real foreign minister after Tanaka was a non-politician; here the deputy prime minister takes the job. The real Financial Services Agency did carry out "special inspections" of big borrowers in 2001–02.
- **The Fukui temple** *(my identification)* is the Echizen Daibutsu at Katsuyama, built in 1987 by an Osaka taxi magnate and left with a deserted shopping street. The failing railway matches the Keifuku line there, shut after two collisions in 2000–01 and reborn as a local-government-backed company in 2003; the novel's version is "Kuzuryuugawa Railway."
- **Real projects, built early:** the Naniwasuji line through central Osaka was approved only in 2019; Nagoya's Aonami line opened in 2004 on a converted freight line, and the Jōhoku line and the southern freight line exist; Chubu Centrair airport and the Aichi Expo both opened in 2005.
- **AIRHO = Air Do** (Hokkaido International Airlines). In reality it filed for court protection in June 2002 — the month this chapter reaches — and was rebuilt under ANA's wing.
- **Teisen Ishii and Teimen = Nissho Iwai and Nichimen** *(my identification: 岩井 "rock-well" → 石井 "stone-well"; "Japan Cotton" → "Imperial Cotton")*. In reality the two merged with each other in 2003–04 as Sojitz, after heavy losses in Asia.
- **Kanegana Textiles = Kanebo** (Kanegafuchi Spinning). Real: its cosmetics business carried a loss-making textile parent; years of falsified accounts were exposed in 2004–05; the cosmetics arm was sold to Kao and the company dissolved. Runa buys it two years before the fraud surfaced — her "hell."
- **The LAN venture = Crosswave Communications** *(my identification)*: Japan's first wide-area Ethernet carrier, a joint venture of an internet company with Toyota and Sony, which built large data centres, never found the traffic, and filed for reorganisation in August 2003 with debts of the order the chapter gives.
- **Honami's system:** the real Mizuho joined three banks' systems — built by Fujitsu, IBM and Hitachi — through relay computers, and it was the relay that failed on 1 April 2002. Fujitsu was the old Dai-Ichi Kangyo's supplier, as Furukawa Telecoms is "the former DK Bank's" here.
- **Teisei's contents** — department stores, supermarkets, convenience stores, fashion malls, a beef-bowl chain — match the old Saison group.
- **Uncertain:** Teisan Bank.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The adults are answering Volume 3's question with structure.** "Reign, but don't rule" is the constitutional monarch's formula, and Runa applies it to herself; the lieutenants conclude that a sovereign who owns everything and governs nothing is a risk to the companies and to her. Their cure is the ordinary corporate one: listings, holding companies, "shareholders' eyes." It is Koizumi's programme, adopted voluntarily and a size too small.
- *Strongly supported:* **The price is her household.** One meeting sends Angela to the bank and Nakamaro out of it and puts Tachibana on a three-year clock. "Everyone would leave someday" — and in the game there was no one to leave.
- *Strongly supported:* **Kiyomaro's single word is this chapter's "job for adults."** He will not let her money into the family's own business. It is said with a smile and it works: for once she hears it.
- *Strongly supported:* **She cannot stop.** In the middle of a reorganisation meant to shrink her role she adds three companies and up to ¥3 trillion of debt, on a pun and a joke about makeup. Okazaki's line — "most company presidents aren't brave enough" — is admiration; everyone else in the room looks at him as the culprit.
- *Strongly supported:* **Eiichi is being written as her opposite number.** He counts what a bet would cost other people, wants to learn the product before the balance sheet, and thinks proof lies in *keeping* a success. Karin then names the gap in his plan to "see what Runa sees": Runa has never run anything. She is an investor.
- *Strongly supported:* **The railways are fiscal policy.** The narration says outright that Keika's construction is what keeps the economy from collapsing under "reform." A private fortune is doing the state's counter-cyclical spending — and using it to "pressure the Osaka Diet members."
- *Plausible:* **The card is the quiet menace.** Karin's "gold mine" is forty million people's purchases, movements and identity in one company's data centre — next to Volume 3's colour-ranked ID cards and employment-matching computer. Nobody at the table hears anything but revenue.
- *Plausible:* The Honami scene is the comic form of a serious pattern: the people who suffer in these mergers are subcontractors at the bottom, the people who decide never meet them, and the only thing that reaches the middle manager is a phone call from above. Runa enjoys being that call. It is also one more exercise of power by someone with no office that would let anyone hold her to account for it.
- *Plausible:* "If they forgot to add a soul to the Buddha statue" is a self-description. The bubble built shells; she buys shells and means to animate them — "Connecting your present to the future."

**Connections:** Kiyomaro's "know when to quit" (Ch. 2); Kanbe on a child's unaccountability (Ch. 5); the listing and the lieutenants' exits return in Chs. 9 and 12; Keika Electronics Union launches in Vol. 5 Ch. 2; the Hatabe merger (Ch. 5; Vol. 5 Ch. 4); the unified card continues Vol. 3 Ch. 7; "all alone" in the game ↔ the Kansai Airport image (Vol. 3 Ch. 10) and the sparse tea party of Vol. 5 Ch. 9.

## Chapter 7 — Mission Impossible

**Narrative position:** A comic interlude told almost entirely in overheard dialogue and sound effects, set at Kudanshita Station and Keika Tower in spring 2002. It is followed by the volume's first interstitial document, a railway fans' message-board thread. *(Read in full for the second edition; the first edition gave it one line.)*

**What happens**
- **The game.** Kaihouin Hotaru walks into the tower "invisibly." If she reaches Runa and Asuka on the top floor unseen, she wins the decorations off their cake.
- **The defence.** Angela — not Runa — has mobilised everything after Hotaru "crushed Lady Runa in a game of hide and seek": station guards, the maid corps, plainclothes men "from the company," and armoured exoskeletons wearing "Guard Training" sashes. Commuters complain. The suits "were a symbol of the terror in Toyohara's underground shopping center."
- At Café Vesuna a police detective on stakeout finds his apple pie gone and a hundred-yen coin in its place.
- The guards know that "she doesn't show up on surveillance cameras," and the suits' thermal sights do not register her either. They watch the express lift arrive at the top floor empty.
- **Okazaki** shares a lift with nobody and says aloud: "I bet some money on you, so do your best." His verdict on the talent: "If someone evil had that power, they would probably use it to become a terrorist."
- On the stairs something answers Hotaru: "**Who goes there? Who called me here…? Ah, it's you. Trying to scare the girl?… I'm a supernatural tanuki too. I love to startle people, and I won't complain, since you're the one who found me when I was collapsed in the city street.**" A guard screams — "Something just touched my neck" — and the cordon breaks.
- A knock at the door. Asuka: "See, Angela-san? You can't beat Hotaru-chan when it comes to hide and seek!" Angela: "Yes, I've come to understand that all too well."
- Days later Runa is found hiding in a cardboard box: "I wanna have hide and seek powers like Hotaru-chan too!"

**Revelations**
- Hotaru's invisibility is literal: cameras and heat sensors do not see her. She can also call on a spirit that owes her a favour.
- Angela has been treating Hotaru as a security problem since the Fushimi Inari vanishing (Vol. 3 Ch. 10); Chapter 2 mentions her frantic call to the CIA's "science division." This is her test, and she loses it.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the "supernatural tanuki" is *tanuki-bayashi*, the folk tale of phantom festival drums — "By the way, *Pom Poko* came out in 1994"; the box is ***Metal Gear Solid***.
- **Fictional:** the exoskeletons and their history in Toyohara (the capital of the former Northern Japan) continue Vol. 3 Ch. 6's borrowing from the *Kerberos* series.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The joke is the fortress.** A tower with a police tunnel, a private army, powered armour and two intelligence services cannot keep out one eleven-year-old who wants cake. Volume 3 built that apparatus in earnest; this chapter shows its limit from the friendliest possible angle.
- *Strongly supported:* The supernatural layer is real in this world and sits outside every system the book otherwise takes seriously — money, law, surveillance. Hotaru keeps winning the games (Ch. 5) for the same reason.
- *Plausible:* It is also the one episode in the volume where adults pour resources into controlling a child and fail without consequences — a light counterweight to Chapters 2 and 9.

## Chapter 8 — One Day on a Talk Show

**Narrative position:** A transcript of a daytime television "wide show," with stage directions for what the studio monitor displays; late spring 2002, after the cabinet reshuffle. No narrator. *(Read in full for the second edition; the first edition gave it one line.)* It is the public case against Runa, assembled out of footage from the previous four volumes.

**What happens**
- The host opens on the reshuffle and the Foreign Ministry scandal: officials dismissed, the prime minister's approval down — "No one is walking away from this unscathed. However… there are a few people who've managed to avoid drawing the short straw." A guest: "How unfair. Who are these people?" — "Ambassadors and envoys from noble families."
- A primer on the peerage (five ranks; daimyo descendants, Meiji statesmen, court nobles, zaibatsu heads) leads to "the daily lives of nobles that few people get to see" — and the monitor shows **Runa running the Akasaka marathon**.
- She is introduced as the girl who "leads the zaibatsu Keika Group," total assets "somewhere close to **twenty trillion yen**." The host is even-handed: critics cite its closeness to politicians, but it "has constantly worked for the good of this country, with their handling of bad debts being one example."
- The monitor shows her with Fuchigami at his residence, then with her arms raised in Izumikawa's election office. She is "the source of funding for the Izumikawa faction."
- "Why is such a little girl involved in the investigations of politicians?" — "That's related to the special noble privilege of diplomatic immunity."
- **The explanation the public is given:** the privilege "allows the heads of noble families to decide what happens to their own households." Nobles in the Foreign Ministry scandal "avoided arrest for this very reason." The check is supposed to be the **Privy Council**, "watchmen of the constitution" — but since the war it has left such matters to the families and has never punished anyone.
- "**Now, if this girl were to do something wrong, it wouldn't be the police, prosecutors, or judges who would punish her, but only her father, Duke Keikain Kiyomaro.**" A guest: "The parent is in charge of punishing the child? But there's no way of knowing if they're going easy on them." Host: "Indeed, and that's probably on purpose. **The Keika Group has placed all of its assets in the hands of young Keikain Runa.**"
- A diagram shows the group "united by the Moonlight Fund, headquartered on the west coast of the United States. In fact, the fund is owned by the girl herself." Guests: "No one would believe an elementary schooler owns a company worth almost twenty trillion yen!" — "How can we believe that punishment will be fair and just?!"
- "Can't anything be done about this? We're standing by and watching injustice in action." The monitor shows **the photograph of Runa walking past the opposition member's son** as he gives "a righteous speech."
- The host's close: Koizumi can be expected to seek reform here — but Privy Council reform "is being led by Deputy Prime Minister Izumikawa, their chairman," former prime minister Fuchigami now sits on it as a count, and it is "a stronghold for these opposition forces the Koizumi administration speaks of."

**Revelations**
- Runa's ownership of the Moonlight Fund, and through it of a ¥20 trillion group, is now daytime-television knowledge.
- The public has been handed a simple, accurate formula for the problem: a child who owns everything, answerable only to her father.
- The Privy Council — her side's chosen check on Koizumi since Vol. 2 — is being framed as the old order's bunker.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** Britain's upper house as a court of final appeal until the UK Supreme Court took over (in reality in 2009, under a 2005 act — the glossary's link to EU membership is loose); France's *parlements*, abolished in the Revolution.
- **Real:** the format is the Japanese "wide show," the daytime mix of news and gossip that shaped opinion in the Koizumi years; the 2002 Foreign Ministry turmoil (the minister dismissed in January, the top bureaucrat removed with her, officials disciplined over misused funds).
- **Fictional, and central:** noble "diplomatic immunity," the surviving Privy Council and noble ambassadors. In our history the peerage and the Privy Council were abolished in 1947.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **This is the accountability argument in its popular form.** Kanbe put it clinically (Ch. 5: a child escapes responsibility; the adults cover for her). Koizumi will put it politically (Ch. 9: "you simply aren't qualified"). Here a studio guest puts it as fairness: who punishes her? Chapter 2 gave the answer in Runa's own words — Kiyomaro "was the only one who could formally scold me" — and what he did was hand her a book.
- *Strongly supported:* **The series has been filming her for this.** Every image on the monitor is a scene the reader watched from inside as charm or triumph — the marathon (Vol. 3 Ch. 4), the Hokkaido produce visit (Vol. 2 Ch. 2), election night (Vol. 1 Ch. 9), the walk past the speech (Ch. 5). Re-cut, they are an indictment. Her procedural victory in the school election supplies its closing picture.
- *Strongly supported:* **The programme is fair, which is why it is dangerous.** It credits Keika with the bad-debt clean-up and never calls her a villain. It only lays out the structure — and the structure is as it says. Tachibana warned her in Chapter 5 that the media "want to see your downfall"; what is shown here is subtler: they need only describe her.
- *Plausible:* This is the "middle class" of the media at work (Ch. 5): not proprietors, whom Keika can lobby, but a host, a booker and an editor of clips.
- *Plausible:* The talk show does to the nobility what the game's Mizuho will do to the school: it asks why some people stand outside the rules, in front of an audience of "average citizens."

**Connections:** Vol. 3 Prologue (noble immunity as Hikomaro's invention) and Ch. 2 (the secret-funds scandal; the act making the deputy prime minister chairman of the Privy Council); Ch. 5 (the speech at the gate; Tachibana on television); Ch. 9 (the bill to end noble privilege in criminal cases; the dream of the school vote); Vol. 5 Ch. 9 (the game's scheme relied on exactly this privilege).

## Chapter 9 — The Lady's Defensive War

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV with one third-person scene; June–July 2002. The volume's centre and, by the author's own account, its purpose: Runa is defeated, by a prime minister, in person. *(Rewritten in the second edition from a full read. Added: the Kanbe–Takenaga meeting, the financial programme, the fact that Runa had already chosen to yield, the mechanism of the attack, Angela's answer about fleeing, and the second and third phone calls.)*

The chapter has seven movements: a lecture; a warning; a squeeze; a raid; a surrender; a dream; a scolding and three phone calls.

**What happens**

*1. Kanbe on money, happiness and nobles*
- "**Do you know how much money you'd need to save this country?**" ¥4 million a year for eighty years is ¥320 million a head; times 140 million people, ¥44.8 quadrillion — against national assets of just over one quadrillion. The "trick": lever those assets fifty times, as the markets do. "Can you actually do that?" — "Probably not. But isn't it a fascinating thought experiment?"
- Why not simply make everyone happy? Define happiness first. Runa picks "the family life of a long-running TV anime" — a house with a garden near Tokyo: "a life that felt like a distant dream in my past existence."
- Not everyone can have it: the cities are a game of musical chairs, and those already seated win by not moving. "**Neoliberalism. This country has an image of personal responsibility, but its true essence is in the way it gives handicaps**… 'I'll give you a chance, but it comes with a handicap.'" Equality of results "is what's called communism or socialism. Around the time you were born, the Eastern Bloc countries failed the pilot study."
- "But isn't that unfair?" — "Sure. It's based on the premise that people aren't equal."
- Why do humans protect the weak? "Because humans are special?" — "**No, it's because we want to believe we're special.**" He writes "privileged class," then, in red, *noblesse oblige*. "You only have two hands, and that's not enough to save all of Japan. Come to understand that weak person thoroughly. Decide if you want to save them or force their sacrifice, then select as many of the weak as your hands can hold."
- Runa's private gloss: "One such weak person was going to bring about my downfall. If I was going to go down in history as the loser no matter how much I struggled, I needed to be a good loser at the very least."

*2. The members' club*
- Third person: Takenaga has invited Kanbe to a hotel club. "I want you to know this — because she might turn against us." The next Diet session will bring "the complete elimination of bad debts," market-value accounting, and listings for financial institutions — each aimed at something she owns.
- "The world is currently at the mercy of the young lady's whims… the Prime Minister isn't going to allow that." Washington has said it can no longer overlook the Foreign Ministry's paralysis and has given the "axis of evil" speech. "**Having the two of them at the very top during a war would be a total nightmare.** Unless we build a top-down system, we might end up losing the next war."
- And: "The prime minister says he doesn't want the girl dealing with any more matters of blood. She's still young enough that she needs to be protected."
- Kanbe: "I told her to leave this country because it's going to kill her talents." Takenaga: "Just killing them would be better. She's going to be unfairly judged." Kanbe adds that she runs gated communities across the United States, one in Florida: "If she felt like it, she could escape. Don't forget that." (Narration: he "didn't fully comprehend what it meant to give that information to Prime Minister Koizumi.")

*3. The programme*
- Takenaga's seven-point financial restoration plan: mark assets to market more strictly; align banks' treatment of big debtors; publish the gap between banks' own assessments and the regulator's; set rules for injecting public funds; tighten deferred tax assets; issue improvement orders; and, on top, introduce market-value accounting. Target: halve the big banks' bad loans by March 2005, with nationalisation for laggards.
- With stocks near 19,000, Keika, Imperial Iwazaki and Futaki Yodoyabashi have largely finished; Honami and Gowa Osan have not. The "real bomb" is the accounting change, which could send even the clean banks back to the start.
- A committee to privatise the public highways cuts into anti-Koizumi strongholds; seven general contractors have been wound up (¥1.23 trillion); approval holds at 40%.
- Takenaga names Keika Holdings on television: list "as a symbol of zaibatsu dissolution." Ichijou answers at a press conference: "we're a private company now and nothing more."
- **Runa chooses not to fight.** The anti-Koizumi factions want Keika with them; Izumikawa offers to sabotage the Diet extension. She declines: "Taking out the minister will cause a backlash that reaches the prime minister too. I'll hand over the achievements and step back myself… The prime minister is a wounded beast right now."

*4. Half past five in the morning*
- Tokitou Aki wakes her: the Tokyo prosecutors' special investigation unit will search **Tachibana and Keika Railway**.
- **The thread they are pulling.** Former secretary-general Katou resigned over tax evasion in his office. His office's representatives had bought land at Sakata at the top of the bubble; Keika Development — the former Far Eastern Developments, her father's company, now part of Keika Railway — later bought it from them at the bubble price, in effect a payment to Katou, and the gain went undeclared. Insider land deals around the Sakata complex were "what my father had done."
- **Kiyomaro invokes noble immunity** for the family's companies. Runa wails: "It's like they're saying there's a reason to suspect us!" Tachibana explains: the prosecutors are not after Katou but "digging up the old issue of Far Eastern Development's collusion with the East." The family has no choice — and she has no say.
- **How the privilege works.** A noble house may enrol a number of people fixed by its rank; the house judges them under its own rules and reports to the Privy Council. Houses routinely fill their quotas with outsiders — "criminals such as tax evaders in exchange for a kickback." Lately the Council has sometimes been pressed by the government into overturning a house's verdict.
- By seven every channel carries it, reporters at the foot of the tower. Runa sees it whole: "**This was an attack from Prime Minister Koizumi himself, and his ultimate target was me.**"

*5. The war room*
- The opposition Democratic League sends a team to talk. "Send them away." Any link would only arm the press; Koizumi already has "the will of the people."
- "If I fled to America, how many days do you think I could survive?" Angela: "**If you ran away now, Eva would kill you before you even crossed the international date line.**" Runa knows why: America has not forgotten the crash she caused.
- Anisha: as an attempt to destroy her it is "half-hearted." Tachibana and Ichijou are under fire; Toudou and the family's pharmaceutical company are not. "They're only trying to cut off your own functioning limbs." Kitagumo: the Izumikawa faction is pinned inside the cabinet.
- Okazaki: the Cabinet Intelligence and Research Office has found the Bahrain project. Runa drops her head on the table: this is the one thing "the prime minister had every right to be furious with me over."
- Tachibana: "The prime minister only has one goal in mind: '**Make the girl behave.**'… why don't you take a break for a while?… **My lady, please withdraw from Iraq.**" Angela and Eva stay silent, "which meant that both the United States and Japan had decided to remove me together."
- "Is that what the whole world wants?" — "I don't believe that's something for an elementary school child to feel."
- She is not jailed, stripped or exiled — "compared to the usual condemnations and banishments of villainesses, this was a rather lukewarm outcome" — but it is "my conclusive loss."
- Koizumi's announcements follow: public funds for all the megabanks; a state scheme to buy the banks' shareholdings; "a bill… to outlaw the recent use of nobles' special privileges in criminal cases"; and talks with Russia on a peace treaty over Northern Karafuto.

*6. The dream*
- She dreams the game's climax. High school; the class committees are voting on abolishing the privileges of noble and zaibatsu children. The majority are "**scholarship students who'd reached their boiling point over the discrimination they'd faced**," and **Takanashi Mizuho** is "the face of this reform." Students ring the building.
- The council is Eiichi (president), Yuujirou, Mitsuya — and Runa, vice president, on her feet: "I promised we would improve!… You were all lying when you said you'd join me!"
- "As a class committee member, Keikain Runa formed her own faction, split the opposition, and was frantically trying to find points of compromise… **Keikain Runa was a reformist despite being a noble. Still, the game made her the loser in the end.**"
- The vote ties. The president — her fiancé — breaks it: "As president, I have decided to adopt this proposal." Afterwards: "**Keikain, both of us are behind the times.**"
- Awake: "I don't mind losing. Not to Takanashi Mizuho and not to Teia Eiichi. Not to Koizumi Souichirou. They were all first-rate actors — stars, even… **The one thing I didn't want to defeat me was posterity.** Eras of time were like gigantic waves." In the game's history this happened "right in the middle of the global financial crisis," which "stole all the power from the zaibatsu," the nobles having already been broken — by "the gray-haired politician, Prime Minister Koizumi."
- "…A dream? Or is this whole life a dream?"
- She admits what she felt in her first life: "when he took power, I thought I might be able to cut corners a bit… Like many others, I believed that he could destroy the sense of hopelessness we felt."

*7. The prime minister's residence*
- Officially she is Hokkaido's goodwill ambassador again. When the cameras leave: "Have I done something to upset you?"
- "It's an adult's job to stop children when they're heading in the wrong direction… **A child is trying to help kill people. Don't you think any adult would put a stop to that?**"
- "When you fainted during the terrorist attacks and I caught you, I was shocked at how light you were. I had to chastise myself for letting such a small child take on the fate of this country, and even the whole world."
- She argues that a politician should bear it if it is the best course. He answers with democracy: "the person who sits in my seat represents the will of the majority of our people. Keikain-kun, do you intend to leave both me and the people who chose me in the history books as those who helped a child commit murder for the good of their country?" She thinks: "I knew that I'd been like a sort of dictator." Aloud: "That's a cowardly way of putting it."
- "If I had to criticize you, it wouldn't be because you're a child… **You simply aren't qualified.** You aren't a Diet member. You may belong to a dukedom, but you aren't a duke. You aren't a soldier, and while you probably control the Keika Group, you don't represent them. **You leave everything to proxies.** Think back to the fixers of long ago. Even they came to face the public when the time was right. Right now, you can't even choose to do that. **That's why you'll end up betrayed in times like these.**"
- "You can never appear at the very end and ask to be sacrificed for the good of the country. The people around you wouldn't let you."
- "What if I don't become qualified until it's too late?! What if I've met with my downfall?!" — "You'll just have to live with it. Accepting the past from one's predecessors and giving the future to people like you is the job of adults."
- She cries: "I just couldn't convey what a terrible past we'd been given. He wouldn't believe me." He offers a handkerchief. "I believe in this country's people. I believe in their will to change. So, Keikain-kun, **can't you wait a little longer? Don't take on the dishonor of letting the people sacrifice you for this country's prosperity.**"

*Three phone calls — "If you were going to betray me, when would you do it?"*
- **Eiichi** calls first: "You reached out to Yuujirou when everything happened last time… I figured it was our turn now." His answer: "Right now, I'd go find the version of me that betrays you and beat him up." Then, honestly: "**If I had to go up against you, I'd never win unless I took you down with me.**" Runa understands the dream's line at last: "He'd gone down with me so that he could win."
- **Yuujirou:** "It definitely wouldn't be my own choice. Either Eiichi-kun talked me into it or something had me blinded." He admires Koizumi — "he never goes back on a public commitment." And he reads her: "**You compromise, Keikain-san.** You can't just keep running through everything like the prime minister, so when you can't get a major victory, you aim to avoid a major loss instead." His father "is making moves."
- **Mitsuya** separates betrayal from opposition, then gives two causes. "One reason… might be disappointment." "**The other reason is good intentions**… there's no way to defend oneself against a betrayal born of pure good will. After all, I would only betray you out of concern for you, Keikain." She sees her reflection gasp: that is what Tachibana, Ichijou, Angela, Kiyomaro and Nakamaro tried, and she brushed them off and brought Koizumi down on herself. "Are you still going to stay on the same path?" Silence. "If we ever betrayed you, that would probably be why. I'm warning you as your friend."
- Last line: "Soon, I would learn the truth — the truth of what lay ahead when Prime Minister Koizumi stopped me."

**Revelations**
- The game's central conflict is a school reform against noble and zaibatsu privilege, led by scholarship students; its Runa was a reformist who lost a tied vote to her own fiancé.
- In the game's history, too, Koizumi broke the nobility, and the zaibatsu fell in the 2008 crisis.
- Runa had liked Koizumi in her first life.
- Koizumi's objection is neither her age nor her policy: she holds power without any office in which she could be held to account.
- The attack was built from her father's crime and the hush money that followed it; her family, not she, chose the response; and the American officers in her own household would kill her if she ran.
- Each boy has now told her how the game's betrayal could come about.

**Historical references**
- **The programme is the real "Takenaka Plan," moved up four months.** Real: Takenaka Heizō's Programme for Financial Revival (30 October 2002) demanded stricter asset valuation, consistent classification of large borrowers, disclosure of gaps between banks' and inspectors' assessments, a framework for public funds, tighter treatment of deferred tax assets, and improvement orders — and set the target of halving the big banks' bad-loan ratio by March 2005. The novel's list is nearly word for word. **Altered:** market-value accounting is bolted on as a seventh point.
- **Real:** the state-backed purchase of banks' shareholdings (from 2002); a public recapitalisation of a major bank (2003); the committee on privatising the highway corporations (June 2002); the string of general-contractor failures; the Koizumi slogan the translation renders "With no reforms, no growth"; the 2002 World Cup; the Churchill line on democracy.
- **Katou's fall = Katō Kōichi's.** Real: his office chief was arrested for tax evasion in March 2002; Katō left the party and in April resigned his seat. **Altered:** the novel makes him Koizumi's party secretary-general, and ties the money to Sakata land — which is in the real Katō's own constituency in Yamagata.
- **Fictional:** the search of Keika Railway; noble immunity and its abolition; the "Democratic League Party" (the opposition's stand-in); the Northern Karafuto peace talks. *Context:* the real Koizumi's surprise diplomatic stroke of that autumn was his visit to Pyongyang; the novel's equivalent is Moscow.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The defeat is the author's thesis.** "Would you really make a child still in elementary school sign an order for murder?… There's no such thing as a child who hasn't been scolded by an adult" (afterword). Koizumi is written as the adult who refuses the bargain every other adult in the volume accepts.
- *Strongly supported:* **"You aren't qualified" reframes four volumes.** Acting through proxies was introduced in Volume 1 as the clever workaround for a child with no legal capacity. Koizumi names it as the defect: no mandate, no title, no face she can show. And he states the consequence exactly — "that's why you'll end up betrayed" — after the reader has watched it happen twice (Ch. 2's farce; Tachibana's "please withdraw").
- *Strongly supported:* **The attack is aimed at the instrument, and it makes her side display it.** A prosecutor's raid cannot touch a duke's household; so the household must invoke its privilege on live television, weeks after a talk show explained that privilege to the country (Ch. 8) and weeks before a bill to end it. Her father's crime, Katou's hush money and Hikomaro's legal invention are all cashed in one morning.
- *Strongly supported:* **She had already yielded on everything but the war.** She told Izumikawa she would "step back" on the banking bills. The raid came anyway, because the issue was Bahrain. This is not a policy dispute won by the stronger side; it is an adult taking something out of a child's hands.
- *Strongly supported:* **Mitsuya supplies the volume's key term: betrayal from good will.** It names what her guardians did in Chapter 2, what Tachibana does here, and what Koizumi does from outside — and it reinterprets the game. The boys who "betray" the villainess may be doing it for her.
- *Strongly supported:* **The three answers are the game's three routes.** Eiichi: mutual ruin, the only way he can win. Yuujirou: persuasion and the pull of a leader who never compromises — the appeal, Runa reflects, that Mizuho saw in him. Mitsuya: concern. She collects them the way she once collected balance sheets.
- *Strongly supported:* **"Posterity" is the real antagonist.** She can accept losing to people — "first-rate actors." What she cannot accept is losing to the era. It explains her answer to Kanbe ("right now is the only time") and her tears with Koizumi: she knows the future he is asking her to trust, and cannot say so.
- *Strongly supported:* **Kanbe and Koizumi make the same argument from two sides.** The professor: you have two hands; choose whom to save, and know that you save them to feel special. The prime minister: the choice of who is sacrificed belongs to the people's representatives, not to you — and you may not volunteer yourself either.
- *Plausible:* **The leash.** "Eva would kill you" turns Volume 3's ring of protectors inside out. The alliance that shields her is also the one that would stop her leaving; Kanbe's well-meant remark about Florida has just told the government where the exit is.
- *Plausible:* Yuujirou's portrait — she compromises, avoids the great loss, takes her risks in finance and not in politics — is the most accurate description of her in the book, and it comes from the boy the game uses to corner her.
- *Plausible:* "Or is this whole life a dream?" The game shows her as a reformist who loses; her life has just shown her as a fixer who loses. For a moment she cannot tell which is the original.
- *Plausible:* The reformist game-Runa and the cheque-writing Runa of Chapter 5 are the same person on opposite sides of the privilege question. The game's girl tried to mend the system from inside and was outvoted; this one has been using it.

**Connections:** "A job for adults" (Vol. 3 Chs. 2 and 6) becomes "an adult's job." Fuchigami's warning about her grandfather's path (Vol. 2 Ch. 5) → "the fixers of long ago." ## Chapter 10 — Conference Call: Japanese-American Leaders

**Narrative position:** Two pages of unattributed dialogue between Prime Minister Koizumi and the American president, shortly after Chapter 9. No narration, no Runa. It is the adults' own account of what they have just done and of the price they agree to pay for it. *(Read in full for the second edition; the first edition had one line.)*

**What happens**
- President: "That was quite a show. You did a good job shutting up the young lady." Koizumi: "I couldn't do it on my own. I was able to silence her thanks to the people around her."
- The president presses: her strategy was built around the United States and "linked to profits" — does silencing her mean Japan will take the profits itself? Koizumi: "That's only natural, don't you think?… let's promise to get through the crisis together as partners."
- The president admits guilt at "having silenced the girl who said that in the first place." Koizumi gives him the argument he gave her: "Can we really allow the history books of those people to one day say, '**A young girl successfully brought prosperity to her country by becoming a murderer**'?"
- "And that's why the people in her life sided with you?" — "Your country also fears that she might turn into a monster, do they not? Russia feels the same."
- President: "It sounds like a funny joke if you don't know any better. 'An elementary school girl started a war against the American, Japanese, and Russian governments.' But she sees everything clearly and she moves with wisdom. Some people here call her the '**Moon Mistress**.'" — "Is that from the Heinlein novel?"
- The president *(the call has no speaker tags; by the strict alternation of speakers this line is his, not Koizumi's as the first printing of this map said — corrected 15 October 2026)*: "A cruel mistress of the night doesn't suit our world today. **We can't allow Karafuto's independence, even as a federation.** Come to think of it, the same goes for your country and Russia being governed by one leader. We don't know how the world order would change with that young lady in control of a nation."
- Shared history: "Only half a century ago, we made a play for the mainland and suffered for it." — "And it wasn't too long ago that we learned the same lesson in Vietnam." They laugh.
- **The promise.** Koizumi: "Mr. President, **let's promise to stain our hands with blood.** Can I have your word on this? You don't want a second Vietnam." — "I'll do anything to avoid a second Vietnam. We'll take out Iraq's dictator and leave the rest to the Iraqi people."
- **The story to be told.** Japan has "the full story about those terrorist attacks from suspects we arrested here" and will publish it jointly: "The dictator had a plan to get nukes, but when that failed, we ended up with the terrorist attacks. Even if that country doesn't have weapons of mass destruction now, it's clear that they could still use them… in the future."
- **The timetable** to "next fall": publication, a Security Council vote, a multinational force. Izumikawa will be Japan's point of contact; the Secretary of State will come as "witness" for the Northern Karafuto talks.
- President: "Right now, it's the people who set the world in motion. Their will means everything. Even if it's justified, it wasn't a divine message coming from a young maiden."
- Koizumi: "Indeed. **That's why we need her to become Cassandra**, although it's hard to say how long she'll need to stay in that role."
- The close (by alternation: president, Koizumi, president): she will grow up, "fall in love someday and use her powers for her partner." — "And then the spell on the princess will break, and she'll become a normal girl? That would make us the clock that strikes midnight." — "At the very least, why don't we have her lose her glass slipper?"

**Revelations**
- The removal was coordinated between the two heads of government, and "the people around her" were its instrument.
- The leaders' fear has a geopolitical edge beyond the moral one, and it is the president who states it: a Runa who ruled Karafuto, or a Japan and Russia "governed by one leader," would rearrange the world order. *(Corrected 15 October 2026: previously attributed to Koizumi.)*
- The two men take on the war she was preparing — deliberately, as their own responsibility — and take over the case for it that her discoveries made possible.
- They intend to make her a prophet who is not acted on, for as long as it takes her to grow up.

**Historical references**
- **Real:** Robert Heinlein's *The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress* (1966), in which a lunar colony fights for independence — hence Koizumi's turn to Karafuto; the real case for war ran through the UN Security Council in autumn 2002 (Resolution 1441, 8 November); "take out the dictator and leave the rest to the Iraqi people" is the light-footprint plan actually followed.
- **Altered:** in this world the public case rests on Iraq's *attempt* to buy a Soviet warhead and on confessions obtained in Tokyo, not on stockpiles (compare Ch. 3 and Vol. 5 Ch. 2).
- **In-world history:** "we made a play for the mainland" is Japan's war in China; America's "same lesson" is Vietnam.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The title of two volumes is spoken aloud as policy.** "Cassandra's Struggle" (Vol. 2) and "Cassandra's Lament" (Vol. 3) described a prophet who was believed and still could not prevent the event. Here the curse is imposed on purpose: the men who believed her decide that she must stop being listened to.
- *Strongly supported:* **"Let's promise to stain our hands with blood" is the other half of "a job for adults."** Koizumi does not claim the war is clean. He claims the dirt for people who hold office. The president's last principle — the people's will, not "a divine message coming from a young maiden" — is Chapter 9's argument in one line.
- *Strongly supported:* **They are not her enemies and they are not innocent.** The president feels guilty; the prime minister helps himself to the returns on her strategy. The chapter lets both be true.
- *Plausible:* The fairy tale is chosen with care. Cinderella's magic has a deadline and ends with her becoming ordinary; the "glass slipper" they mean to take is whatever would let someone find the princess again. Read with Chapter 2's marriage plans and Tachibana's "you just have to get married," the adults' shared solution for Runa is that she fall in love and stop.
- *Plausible:* "Moon Mistress" fits more than the pun on the Moonlight Fund: Heinlein's revolution is run from behind a figurehead by a mind nobody elected.

**Connections:** Ch. 3 (the White House's earlier calculation) and Ch. 5 (the president's question at Salt Lake City); Ch. 9; Ch. 11 (what the war plan might otherwise have been); Vol. 3 Ch. 9 (Moscow's "don't make your mistress the ruler"); Vol. 5 Chs. 2 and 6 (the congressional report; the war; the Geneva talks on Karafuto) and Ch. 9 (the game's Runa and a Karafuto zone).

## Chapter 11 — Time to Eat

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV, July–August 2002: the days after the defeat, then two weeks in Karuizawa. This is the chapter that shows her what she was stopped from — and the volume's emotional resolution, which pays off a scene planted in Volume 1. *(Read in full for the second edition; the first edition called it a "domestic chapter.")*

**Content note:** the chapter contains an invented American plan for the mass killing of Iraqi civilians. See "Historical references" for what is and is not real.

**What happens**

*The report*
- The "Desert Hero," head of her private military company, forces his way past Angela with a document: Washington is split — "the Department of Defense is fighting with the State Department about Iraq."
- Runa reads it and vomits. He exhales: "**Good. I was planning on quitting this job if you finished reading the whole thing and kept your cool.**" Angela, furious: "Everyone worked so hard to keep this a secret…!"
- The paper, from a Washington think tank, calculates "the most efficient returns the US could receive in the Iraq war": use nuclear, biological and chemical weapons "on all fronts," at a cost of **twenty-three million Iraqi civilian dead**, sparing the oil installations.
- Angela explains the reasoning. Iraq is a **rentier state**: its revenue comes from oil, few of its people work in producing it, and outsiders can run the fields. "They want Iraq's oil, not their people." Runa: "**A 'bad debt' of twenty-three million people**… To them, the Iraqis weren't humans. They were debt."
- Eva adds the second logic: an exchange rate. After Vietnam and Somalia (where American deaths forced withdrawal) and after 11 September, some want to "show the world that the murder of a single American civilian would be repaid a hundred or a thousand times over." Bombing cities, wrecking railways, then nuclear strikes "to set an example": "Ah, it's like the Empire of Japan in 1945. They were apparently going to reenact the history that never happened in this world here in Iraq."
- The text calls the authors "a Jewish think tank… also supported by the government of Israel," with the Gulf War's Scud attacks as motive. Eva, Runa later learns, is Jewish.
- "**Now I finally understood why the adults had forced me to distance myself.** I was involved in the outbreak, logistics, and intelligence of this war. Before the adults chased me away, I was so close to signing off on the execution of twenty-three million people." She cries "from learning the true kindness behind the adults' rejection of me," and because some of them would approve this.
- **Why he showed her.** Her preparations assumed a Gulf War-scale force — logistics for 300,000. With this paper circulating, "some in Washington are starting to say we need even less." The State Department and the field generals oppose it; the Pentagon's leadership does not. "**I need your help to hold them back, my lady.**" Runa: "The battle in Washington continued to drag me across the Rubicon."

*The fast*
- She cannot eat and asks for an intravenous drip. The tower has its own medical floor; Angela already has a specialist in acute stress disorder waiting.
- **The neoconservative doctrine**, as she understands it: Vietnam taught that each American death moves the country toward defeat, so wars should be won with technology and as few troops as possible — under fifty thousand — destroying the regime and leaving the rest to the UN. "That was why they were so overjoyed by my participation in the war. Foreign mercenaries… could die without any effect." The generals think it folly to make mercenaries the main force.
- Iraq is "a mosaic" — a Sunni-led dictatorship over a Shia population tied to Iran and over the Kurds, whose independence Turkey, Iran and Syria all fear. The neighbours, too, would find the Iraqis' disappearance convenient.
- **A dream.** Okazaki tells her Iraq is tied to the Russo-Japanese treaty: Russia will not give up Northern Karafuto's oil without compensation, and the compensation is Iraq. "Personally, I think this genocide is going to start with Russia." One of Russia's conditions is that "the young lady never be made the head of Russia or any neighboring state." "My lady, if you wished, you could even create your own kingdom in Iraq after the genocide." She wakes retching: "**I couldn't stand the ugly side of me who'd just sensed that a genocide was, from my perspective, the optimal solution.**"
- **The first life, told plainly.** "I had to love loneliness. I loved no one and received no love." A modest family whose house was finished as the bubble burst; loan repayments; university on student debt; the financial crisis; a company "that would later become known for its exploitative conditions"; friends lost to overwork; health ruined; "in the end, I was abandoned. The only things I enjoyed in my short life were video games."
- "I'd lived my second life in the world of this game. That was why it took me far too long to realize the truth. This world wasn't just made of beautiful things. There had to be losers to go along with each victor. **This wasn't just playing a game — other people here had wills of their own.**"

*The visit*
- Three days on, still on a drip, she receives her friends: the boys; Asuka with "Great Master Tanuki" mandarin jam bearing "Hotaru's seal of approval"; Kaoru, Mio, Hotaru, Sanae, Shizuka, Akiko — and Kazuki Shiori. Yuujirou brings class notes "and some information." Mio wails that Runa will die if she cannot eat.
- Erika brings a tape "from the co-head maid": **the video of Runa's school-entrance day.** The small girl on screen addresses "the adult me": "You haven't gone bankrupt, have you? You haven't joined a company that forces you to do useless tasks and drives you to bad health, right? Your future isn't pitch-black, is it?… Right now, I'm happy. Are you happy in the future? Even if you're not, please remember that you had this happy past once… I'm sending you this smile so that you'll never forget it."
- "I'd only ever known misfortune. I'd only ever known loneliness. But now that I'd experienced happiness and understood the necessary price of that happiness, I felt like I couldn't allow myself to feel joy." In tears, to the screen: "**Yes, I'm happy. So please don't worry.**"
- Tea with the jam is put in front of her. "It's okay. **Everything we eat involves taking a life. That life, that blessing, is something we must digest with the utmost respect.**" The words are her first mother's. She drinks — the first thing she has kept down in days.

*Karuizawa*
- Angela's recovery plan: two weeks at the villa with "time to herself where she can relax without thinking about anything else." Tachibana stays in Tokyo at her request.
- Baking with the maids. Erika: "You are still a child, my lady. You should be treasuring these times more than you do." Erika's own trouble is a flood of marriage offers from presidents' and politicians' sons — the price of being Ichijou's daughter and Runa's maid.
- **Tokitou Aki's prospects:** whoever marries her will be adopted into the Tokitou name, which will stand as a Keikain branch family — it "would sort out grandfather's bloodline, but also make the Tokitous the family that protected me."
- "Do you have feelings for one of those three boys?" Runa sprays grape juice. Aki: "Didn't you meet with that family of European nobles in Hong Kong?… don't they always say there are no coincidences in high society?"
- Erika's creed: "I don't understand complicated things, but I'm smart enough to ask people who do understand them… Trying to figure out everything myself would be wrong if it ended up making me fall apart."
- Runa's first cake tastes worse than everyone else's. "I wanted whoever ate my cakes to say they were 'delicious.'"
- **Eiichi turns up in Karuizawa.** In a hired café: "I knew something about the position you've been put in. I was even thinking about telling you to stop, but I didn't… I guess I really do want to beat you." Runa: "Becoming an adult isn't a very good thing." — "**Then why are you rushing to become one, Runa?**" She has no answer.
- She tells him she may be introduced to a prospective fiancé — "Europe, even though I'm Japanese." Her hair moves as she says it: she can "never fully be a 'Japanese person,'" and in Europe "people would treat me differently" too. Eiichi: "I see. **But I don't want to give you up, Runa.**" They walk to the station hand in hand: "memories that were never in the game."
- Erika slips him a bag of "cookies made by Lady Runa" — actually hers and Aki's. Runa adds baking to her schedule.

**Revelations**
- What Runa was preparing logistics for could, in other hands, become the extermination of a country — and she recognises the reasoning as a version of her own.
- Her soldier would have resigned had she read it calmly. He is testing whether the "Boss" is human.
- The first life in full: a bubble-era mortgage, student debt, an exploitative employer, isolation, early death.
- The household contains a succession plan for her own protection (the Tokitou branch).
- The Hong Kong meeting was a marriage viewing, and now she knows.
- Eiichi has said, in so many words, that he does not want to lose her.
- **This world has never seen a city destroyed by a nuclear weapon.** "Ah, it's like the Empire of Japan in 1945. They were apparently going to reenact the history that never happened in this world here in Iraq." Japan's negotiated surrender (Vol. 1) came without Hiroshima or Nagasaki. *(Added 9 October 2026.)*

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** rentier states; NBC weapons; the CIA's long involvement in South Vietnam; the "brilliant armchair theory" is the **Rumsfeld Doctrine** — "lightweight, fast, and new"; the Iranian Revolution and the Gulf monarchies' fear of it.
- **Real:** the argument inside the American government in 2002 between a defence leadership that wanted a small, fast invasion force and uniformed and diplomatic officials who wanted a large one; the Gulf War's coalition of several hundred thousand; Iraq's Scud attacks on Israel in 1991; the Sunni–Shia–Kurdish make-up of Iraq and its neighbours' fears; "neoconservatives" as the label for the war's most committed advocates.
- **Fictional — and to be read with care:** no plan to kill Iraq's population existed. The report is the novel's invention, an extreme form of the "optimisation" logic the series has followed through finance. The text's attribution of it to "a Jewish think tank… supported by the government of Israel" leans on a real conspiracy-theory motif about the Iraq War; readers should know that it is one. The book partly complicates it — the explanation is given by a Jewish officer who is nursing Runa, and America's diplomats and generals are shown opposing the paper — but the label is the text's own.
- **Real background to the first life:** the mid-1990s to mid-2000s "employment ice age," student-loan debt, and the "black companies" that the term was later coined for.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **This is the proof that Chapter 9 was mercy.** Until now the reader could side with Runa against a prime minister who would not listen. The report turns Koizumi's "a child is trying to help kill people" from rhetoric into arithmetic, and she says so: "the true kindness behind the adults' rejection of me."
- *Strongly supported:* **"Bad debt" is the series' own vocabulary turned on its heroine.** For four volumes she has classified, written down and disposed of non-performing assets. The report applies the method to people — and a part of her recognises "the optimal solution." What makes her sick is not the authors; it is the kinship.
- *Strongly supported:* **"Other people here had wills of their own" is the end of the game frame.** She states the mechanism herself: she lived this life "in the world of this game," so she was slow to see that winning has losers. It is the clearest statement in five volumes of how the frame worked — as a licence — and of its breaking.
- *Strongly supported:* **The tape is the book's longest-held note.** Recorded in Vol. 1 Ch. 3, it is a message from a woman who had already died once, speaking as a child, to the adult she feared she would become again. The answer — "Yes, I'm happy" — is given by an eleven-year-old who has just learned what her happiness can cost others, and who chooses to accept it rather than refuse to eat.
- *Strongly supported:* **The title answers Volume 3's last line.** There it was "I would rather eat before I get eaten." Here eating is neither conquest nor guilt but the ordinary condition of living — a life taken, to be received "with the utmost respect." The grace before a Japanese meal says the same thing. The rule comes from her first mother, the only inheritance from that life the book has shown as a gift.
- *Strongly supported:* **The men around her divide on what a child should know.** Angela hid the report to protect her. The soldier showed it to find out whether she deserved protecting — and then asked her for help. Koizumi's solution (take her out of it) and his (bring her back as a restraint) are both still on the table when the chapter ends.
- *Strongly supported (second-edition note, from Vol. 5 Ch. 5):* **The kinship was closer than this chapter admits.** Here the plan is someone else's paper and her complicity is a dream and a flash of recognition. In Volume 5 she says outright that mass-casualty deterrence was the plan she had been pressing on Washington's hard-liners — "You people haven't done anything to me, but I want millions of you to die so that I can build a better future with my country" — and that she fell apart because she saw her own thinking carried out. The soldier's test ("I was planning on quitting… if you finished reading the whole thing and kept your cool") was better aimed than he knew.
- *Strongly supported (added 9 October 2026, from a reader's observation):* **The paper wants to supply the demonstration this world never had.** Without a Hiroshima there is no shared picture of what one bomb does to a city, and no taboo born of it. So the planners talk about nuclear strikes "to set an example" and an "exchange rate" for American lives, as if fear still had to be founded. That explains why the series keeps returning to weapons of mass destruction: Vol. 3's nuclear plot, Afghanistan's chemical and biological "tool of fear," Congress's nuclear debate in Vol. 5. Runa's own Fallujah plan (Vol. 5 Ch. 5) would have made her a co-author of the first demonstration. See the Vol. 5 map, Part II §3a.
- *Plausible:* Eiichi's question — "Then why are you rushing to become one?" — is the one Kanbe and Mitsuya have each asked. She can answer the professor ("right now is the only time") but not the boy.
- *Plausible:* Erika's "ask people who do understand" echoes Karin Viola's management rule (Ch. 5) from the other end of the hierarchy. The volume's two sanest voices, a chief executive and a maid, both define competence as knowing what you do not know — the opposite of a child who "can do everything."
- *Plausible:* Her badly baked cake is the first thing in the series she makes rather than buys, and she is bad at it. The chapter sets a small, earned skill against trillion-yen acquisitions.

**Connections:** Vol. 1 Ch. 3 (the video is recorded); Vol. 3 Ch. 12 ("eat before I get eaten") and Ch. 8 (a column imagining mass killing in Afghanistan under her company's name); Ch. 3 (massacres already occurring under her PMC's licence); Ch. 9 (Koizumi; Mitsuya's "good intentions"); Ch. 2 and Ch. 5 (the Lothringes; marriage as an exit); Vol. 5 Ch. 5 (Okazaki names what broke her; the fear of weapons of mass destruction in Iraq returns) and Ch. 4 (her refusal to "pull the trigger" on subprime).

## Chapter 12 — An Unappealing Banquet

**Narrative position:** Third person on Okazaki Yuuichi and then on a lunch between him and Ichijou Susumu; late summer 2002. Runa is offstage. The volume closes with the adults clearing away what she built for the war, scheduling their own departures, and naming — in the last sentence — the next crisis.

**What happens**

*The railway is sold*
- The Saudi mining railway from Dammam towards Hafar al-Batin, with its branch to King Khalid Military City, is 51% owned by the Saudi government and 49% by Akamatsu, which runs it. The Bahrain base went to an American-controlled private military company; for the railway "the US military didn't even try to hide their identity."
- Okazaki's private thought: "That young lady is made of different stuff than I am." He is "proud to work for someone who could predict the future so cleverly while also retaining human empathy," and has not noticed how much more he is smoking.
- The "Gulf Hero," chief of her private military company, arrives with "an unofficial offer from the US military": the Defense Department wants the internal files. Okazaki hands them over. Progress is 80%; the project was signed in **February 2000** — "we've been compelled to have it finished by the start of the war."
- **Her full design:** extend the line to **Amman** and on to **Aqaba** on the Red Sea — her answer to what Washington's hard-liners whisper as "Baghdad departure, Jerusalem arrival." It would let American forces move "with frightening ease," press Syria and cover western Iraq. "Lady Runa has predicted the future of Iraq. Be sure to reflect that in the final sales price."
- The soldier: "Is that boss of ours really all right?" — "She's not well. She nearly broke down." — "**We need to be grateful that this country's prime minister brought the boss back down to earth and scolded her. But at the same time, we need to shoulder the responsibility for the future, so that she doesn't fall apart.**" Okazaki: "You should say that to my lady in person. I'm too embarrassed."
- Next day Akamatsu sells its stake to the United States, price undisclosed.

*The lunch*
- A private room, no alcohol. "I thought you hated me, Ichijou-san." — "I do. You led Lady Runa down a dangerous road. But in the business world, you can't just refuse to meet someone because you hate them."
- **The exits.** Tachibana will resign as president of Keika Railway "before the Diet removes diplomatic immunity for nobles." Ichijou will use the listing of Keika Holdings "to go out on top" and become a chairman without the right to represent the company. Toudou stays for the mergers.
- **The succession.** Angela Sullivan will head Keika Securities and then Keika Holdings; with Karin Viola she is the "second generation." The Moonlight Fund is "under heavy surveillance by the United States." Okazaki might lead "two generations after that" — three to five years.
- **What Angela taught Ichijou:** "when Lady Runa plans to do something, she doesn't think of herself at all." She turned "hundreds of billions of yen… into nothing" to clear the banks' debts; the electronics company "was just a bonus" on reviving an industry. "That was probably why she was so weakened when she had to argue not just over money, but over life itself." Ichijou, who watched borrowers "offer up their lives as repayment" after the bubble: "Like you, Lady Runa is a gambler deep down. However, she's willing to put her life on the line to get a good rate."
- **The assignment.** "Look for replacements for Miss Angela and Miss Karin. Japanese people would be preferable." The three elders mean to move Okazaki out of the Moonlight Fund — partly so no one can say he was bought by foreign capital, and because of "the Iwazaki zaibatsu."
- **The danger Ichijou sees.** The group is still "a one-woman shop" that stops if she stops. Angela and Karin will recruit abroad, and those recruits will be loyal to them; staff sent through the family's Iwazaki marriage will be loyal to Iwazaki or the Keikains. "**At that point, the Keika Group will become a hodgepodge colony for the Iwazaki zaibatsu, with no one remaining who was there from the start**… and Lady Runa will still be a minor." Katsura Naoyuki, who keeps the fund's private accounts, will also have to move on. "Miss Angela wants to get you away from Lady Runa."
- **Ultranationalists.** Some now call Keika a "sellout company" and Runa an enemy "because of her birth and blonde hair": the bad debts were "an American conspiracy"; Japan should be "fully independent." Why would such people favour Iwazaki? "Iwazaki is the only zaibatsu that started in the Meiji Restoration and has walked the same path as this country… the Keika Group needs to merge with the Iwazaki zaibatsu and form the foundation of a **Heisei Restoration**." Ichijou: "I don't understand the logic myself."
- **The puppet master.** "Who?" — "**The 'Ministries.'**" The war is coming regardless; someone must finish the preparations she began. Japan's foreign, military and trade ministries, and America's Defense and State Departments, are now "starting a terrible mudslinging contest using Lady Runa's plan." Koizumi did not take the plan to run it himself; "he wanted to leave it in the hands of others as much as he could."
- The alignment of ultranationalists with America is "a loyalty test… 'Are you with us or against us?'"
- **Why Ichijou asked him.** "The Keika Group has grown far too big, but Lady Runa still has a few years until she can truly make her debut in this world. When that happens, I don't want it to be in a future where her house of cards has collapsed and nothing remains for her."
- Okazaki's realisation: "Lady Runa built the entire Keika Group while taking it for granted that she was going to lose everything…"
- Ichijou, passing on what Tachibana told him: she holds qualifications in bookkeeping, secretarial work and English and is "aiming for an early college graduation. **She clearly believes the Keika Group may collapse and is preparing for it to happen. However, Tachibana-san and I refuse to let her grow up only to have nothing left!**"
- Okazaki orders the table's only beer and pours for him. "You really love Lady Runa too, don't you?" — "Of course I do. **I'm the second guy she scouted.**"
- The afterthought: "I heard the Wall Street folks are up to something. Angela is supposedly involved in it too… I don't know anything about financial engineering." Ichijou, "only a third-rate banker," will look into it. "What are they supposedly plotting?" — "**Apparently, there are these things called subprime loans…**"

**Revelations**
- The railway was commissioned in February 2000 — nineteen months before the attacks. Its timing is the hardest single piece of evidence, inside the story, that Runa knew. **The reader was told at the time:** Vol. 2 Ch. 5 shows the order being given, as the price of renewing a Gulf oil concession, with her reason — "Its cargo route was to run along the southern border of Iraq… the line would soon make profit, before the eventual Iraq war would wipe it out. I hoped it wouldn't happen, and yet I found myself preparing for the worst — something that threatened to push me into a pit of self-loathing."
- Her design ran from the Gulf to the Red Sea and into the Israeli–Syrian balance: a plan for the region, not for one campaign.
- Tachibana and Ichijou both leave within about two years; the group's next leaders are two Americans, one of whom wants Okazaki removed.
- The elders are organising a loyalist faction against the day Runa comes of age.
- No single enemy took her plan over. The bureaucracies of two governments are now fighting for it.
- Her lieutenants know she expects to lose everything, and why she has been collecting certificates.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** "senior assistant" as a courtesy title in Japanese firms.
- **Real:** King Khalid Military City near Hafar al-Batin; Aqaba as Jordan's Red Sea port; the split between the Pentagon and the State Department over Iraq in 2002; "with us or against us" as the president's formula after the attacks; the widespread Japanese suspicion that the bad-debt crisis had served American buyers — the "anti-American sentiment" the author names as Volume 5's subject; **subprime loans**, which expanded rapidly from 2002–03 in the low-interest-rate years that followed the dot-com crash.
- **Altered:** the Saudi railway. Its origin (Vol. 2 Ch. 5) is a real negotiation — Saudi Arabia's demand that Japan fund a mining railway as the price of renewing the Arabian Oil Company's Khafji concession, which Tokyo would not fully fund, so that the concession expired on 28 February 2000 *(my identification; facts verified — [Petroleum News](https://www.petroleumnews.com/pnarchpop/786986120.shtml): the talks were "bogged down by Saudi Arabia's demand that Japan build and pay for a new railway line," a project of about $2 billion)*. In the novel Runa pays, the line is built, and it is routed toward Iraq. **Fictional:** its sale to the United States; the Keika succession; the "Heisei Restoration" movement (the slogan already appears in a newspaper in Vol. 2 Ch. 8). *Echo:* "Restoration" slogans have a long history on the Japanese right — the "Shōwa Restoration" of the 1930s officers, and this series' own second February 26 Incident.
- **Real, lightly rephrased:** "Baghdad departure, Jerusalem arrival" matches a saying current among the war's advocates in 2002 — that "the road to Jerusalem runs through Baghdad," meaning that remaking Iraq would unlock an Arab–Israeli settlement.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The last line closes the loop.** Volume 1 opened on 15 September 2008. Volume 4 ends in 2002 with two men who "don't know anything about financial engineering" naming, over one shared beer, the instrument that will produce that day. The series' sequence of accounting failures — *tobashi*, cross-shareholdings, Enron, WorldCom — now has its last term.
- *Strongly supported:* **"The Ministries" is the antagonist the series has been building toward.** Not a rival or a schemer but institutional self-interest in two capitals, which cannot be bought, shorted or charmed. It is also the answer to Vol. 3's sentence about her downfall: structures whose top layer does not control what is done in its name.
- *Strongly supported:* **Ichijou's outburst is four volumes of payoff.** The banker she cornered at five is now organising a faction to make sure something is left for her. His phrase for the group is hers: "a one-woman shop."
- *Strongly supported:* **The qualifications are the truest thing about her.** She has built ¥20 trillion of companies "while taking it for granted that she was going to lose everything." The certificates are for the life after.
- *Strongly supported:* **The soldier holds both positions at once** — grateful that Koizumi scolded her, and determined that the adults now carry the weight. It is the volume's ethic stated by a man who kills for a living.
- *Plausible:* Angela is the open question the chapter leaves. She is the designated heir, the channel through which Washington watches the fund, the one who wants the gambler gone — and she is "involved" in whatever Wall Street is building.
- *Plausible:* The ultranationalists and the Americans both end up backing Iwazaki's absorption of Keika, for opposite reasons. Runa's refusal to be either anti-American or a nationalist totem leaves her with no camp.

**Connections:** Ch. 1 (Angela as front-runner); Ch. 2 (Suzuki Shoten; the adults' pact); Ch. 6 (the exits, first announced); Ch. 9 (Koizumi) and Ch. 11 (the report); Vol. 3 Ch. 12 (Kiyomaro on trusted managers; the three loyalty answers) and Ch. 2 (the downfall sentence); Vol. 5 Ch. 1 (the family tries to strip her rights), Ch. 4 (subprime) and Ch. 7 (the American view of Tachibana's structure).

# Part II — Economic, Political and Thematic Analysis

## §1. The shape of the volume — one lesson, taught three times

Volume 4 is built as a sequence of attempts to stop Runa, each by someone more powerful and with a harder instrument.

| Attempt | Who | Instrument | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ch. 2, first half | Her own circle — Tachibana, Ichijou, Nakamaro, Angela — with Teikoku Telephone and Washington | A staged takeover and a manufactured shortage of cash | **Fails.** She finds ¥4 trillion and reverses the outcome |
| Ch. 2, end | Kiyomaro, Nakamaro, Saitou Keiko | Words: "know when to quit," "you're still a child," a midnight scolding | She cries, and continues |
| Ch. 5 | Kanbe; Tachibana | Diagnosis ("your problem is that you're a child"); exits (leave the country; marry) | She answers that "right now is the only time" |
| Ch. 6 | The lieutenants | Structure: listings, holding companies, successors | Accepted — and she buys three more companies |
| Ch. 9 | The prime minister, with the United States | Prosecutors, her family's privilege turned into a trap, the loss of her two "functioning limbs" | **Holds.** "My conclusive loss" |
| Ch. 11 | Her soldier | The truth about what she was preparing | She stops eating — and then accepts |

The order matters. The gentle version comes first and fails *because* it is gentle: it is made of money problems, and she can always find money. What she takes from it is not restraint but the knowledge that her guardians will act behind her back. The version that works uses things she cannot buy — a criminal investigation, public opinion, the alliance — and is delivered by the one adult who then explains himself to her face.

Chapter 11 is what makes the volume more than a punishment. Only after the defeat is she shown what the war she was provisioning could become, and only then does she agree with the people who stopped her. The author states the intention in the afterword: "There's no such thing as a child who hasn't been scolded by an adult. In short, that's what this volume is about."

## §2. Betrayal from good will

Mitsuya gives the volume its key term (Ch. 9): there are two reasons to betray someone — disappointment, and "good intentions… there's no way to defend oneself against a betrayal born of pure good will."

Every act against Runa in this volume is of the second kind:

| Where | Who acts against her | Stated motive |
|---|---|---|
| Ch. 2 | Tachibana, Ichijou, Nakamaro, Angela | "May Runa be guaranteed a good, happy life" |
| Ch. 2 | Angela, to Washington | "She might even become a dictator at this rate" |
| Ch. 6 | Kiyomaro | "That job belongs to Nakamaro and me" |
| Ch. 9 | Kiyomaro (invokes immunity without her consent) | To keep her father's history buried |
| Ch. 9 | Tachibana | "Please withdraw from Iraq" |
| Ch. 9 | Koizumi | "It's an adult's job to stop children" |
| Ch. 10 | Koizumi and the president | "It was wrong for an elementary school student to be involved" |
| Ch. 11 | Angela (hides the report) | To spare her |
| Ch. 12 | Ichijou, Tachibana, Toudou (plan to move Okazaki; build a faction) | "I don't want… a future where her house of cards has collapsed" |

*Strongly supported:* this is deliberate patterning, and it is offered as the likeliest shape of the game's ending. Eiichi "went down with me so that he could win"; Yuujirou would act only if "something had me blinded"; Mitsuya would act "out of concern for you." The villainess is not abandoned by enemies. She is overruled by people who love her.

*My reading:* it is also the series' answer to Volume 3's sentence about her downfall — a top layer that does not control what is done in its name. In Volume 3 that looked like a risk from below (soldiers, guards). Volume 4 shows it at the top: her own treasurer, butler, brother and secretary acting on their own judgment of her good.

## §3. The accountability argument, in three registers

The same finding is stated three times, each by a speaker with more power to act on it.

- **Clinical (Kanbe, Ch. 5):** "Your biggest problem… is that you're a child. You can avoid taking responsibility because you're still a child… The adults around you will cover for it."
- **Popular (the talk show, Ch. 8):** "If this girl were to do something wrong, it wouldn't be the police, prosecutors, or judges who would punish her, but only her father… How can we believe that punishment will be fair and just?"
- **Political (Koizumi, Ch. 9):** "You simply aren't qualified. You aren't a Diet member. You may belong to a dukedom, but you aren't a duke… You leave everything to proxies."

**Why this bites.** Since Volume 1, Runa has acted through Tachibana and Ichijou because a child has no legal capacity; Volume 3 spelled out the benefit — "the buck stopped with those three." The series presented this as the necessary workaround. Volume 4 presents it as the constitutional defect of her power: nobody can vote her out, sue her, question her in the Diet or even name the office she holds. The one formal check is a duke who gives her a book.

**The instrument that made it possible.** Chapter 9 finally explains noble immunity in working detail: each house may enrol a number of dependants fixed by rank, judges them by its own rules, and merely reports to the Privy Council; houses sell places to tax evaders. Volume 3's glossary called this Hikomaro's invention — "a real demon." Koizumi does not argue against it. He arranges matters so that the Keikain house must use it on live television, and then tables the bill.

**What the book does not say.** It never claims Runa has abused the privilege for herself. The cheque in the cafeteria (Ch. 5) is the nearest she comes — and Tachibana's comment there is "try not to imitate your grandfather in all the worst ways."

## §4. The finance

### (a) How the rescues actually balanced — the "matryoshka"

Chapter 1 supplies the accounting that Volume 1 skipped. Selling a failed bank's bad loans to the RCC at market price crystallises a loss; a merger lets the surviving entity revalue the other side's assets and book the gain on holdings that had sat at low historical cost. Run three times with the legal survivor changing each time, the losses of each acquired bank were absorbed by the unrealised gains of the previous shell. The Bank of Japan's ¥8 trillion was liquidity insurance, mostly undrawn — "something to display" — and the whole structure was finally repaid from one trade, the Russian bonds.

Two things follow. First, the rescue depended on asset prices being *above* book — the hidden reserve that Volume 2's mark-to-market fight was about. Second, it is unrepeatable: the narrator says so, and Takenaga's programme (below) is designed to make the hidden reserve visible and therefore unusable.

### (b) Why she "would be defeated" — liquidity, not solvency

Ichijou's verdict in Chapter 2 is the most concrete constraint the series puts on her. The Moonlight Fund's cycle is yen → dollars → crude oil → Japan → yen; every leg takes weeks. She had also paid ¥2 trillion at once for the Shinjuku Shinkansen to win a discount. A takeover fight is settled in cash by a date. She was enormously solvent and briefly illiquid — and the discount had cost her the option to respond.

That the shortage was partly staged does not make the mechanism false; the conspirators chose it because it was plausible. The same constraint appears twice more in non-financial form: "We had the money, just not the manpower" (regional banks), and Kiyomaro's "a hundred thousand employees."

### (c) The WCI operation, as arithmetic

- Borrowed: about $20 billion of Gulf money at 9%.
- Levered twenty times through offshore funds: $400 billion of positions.
- Return in the crash: 15%, or $60 billion.
- After interest, fees and repayment: $35 billion, about ¥4 trillion.

A 5% move against a twenty-times position erases the equity; the bet could only be made by someone certain of the direction. That certainty was manufactured: she chose the day of WCI's exposure by forging the whistleblower. The text says Okazaki stayed "just barely legal" by selling only after the first public reports. *My assessment:* the trading may have been timed lawfully, but the scheme as a whole — fabricating a document to move a market one is positioned against — is market manipulation by any ordinary standard, and the book knows it: the people who love her react as if to a crime, and Washington's tolerance is explicitly political.

It is also a break in her conduct. In Volume 3 she refused to short General Energy Online; "I didn't want to go along and end up in the same place as them." Here she ends up exactly there. Volume 5's refusal to "pull the trigger" on subprime is the third term in that sequence.

### (d) Turning around an industry

The glossary's joke is the analysis: a turnaround manager fixes a company; "Runa buys the failed companies and industries altogether… This is why Wall Street looks at her and thinks, 'What the hell is she doing…?'"

Her targets are the future casualties of Japanese electronics: Sanyo (dismembered 2009–11), Fujitsu's hardware lines, Elpida (bankrupt 2012), with Compaq as the route to American distribution. Her diagnosis — that Japan's handsets would become a "Galapagos" species unless it set world standards early — is the standard retrospective one. Her remedy is consolidation ahead of the crisis rather than after it, and "no one was laid off."

Karin Viola supplies the limit: handsets alone are "too small of a scale to take over the world"; the money is in carriers and, as she tells the boys, in data. Chapter 6's data-centre scene is the series' first look at the business model that actually won the next decade — and at who would hold forty million people's records.

### (e) From one woman to institutions

Chapter 6 is a corporate-governance case study:

- **Listing as discipline.** "Shareholders' eyes" replace the owner's. The glossary is cool about it: quarterly results show "only what's right in front of you."
- **What stays private.** Railways, because "railroad projects are long-term endeavors." The series' consistent view is that infrastructure and quarterly capitalism do not mix.
- **Layering control.** A foundation (10%), two operating companies (10% each) and the fund (19.9%) hold just under half of the bank between them without any single holder crossing a regulatory threshold — a cross-shareholding structure rebuilt in miniature, by the person whose bank is supposed to symbolise their dissolution.
- **The conglomerate discount.** lunakeikain's complaint that "the stock market doesn't appreciate them" is the textbook objection to exactly the sprawl Chapter 6 lists.
- **Succession.** None of the three elders has an heir of his own making. Ichijou: "I haven't been able to find a successor." The group's next leaders are hired Americans.

### (f) The Takenaka Plan, four months early

The seven points of Chapter 9 are the real Programme for Financial Revival of October 2002: value assets strictly, stop banks classifying the same borrower differently, publish the gap between self-assessment and inspection, limit deferred tax assets, and stand ready with public money — with the target of halving bad-loan ratios by the end of fiscal 2004. In reality it forced the recapitalisation of one major bank in 2003 and is generally credited with ending the bad-loan era, as the author's afterword says.

The novel's twist is that the country Takenaga applies it to is *healthier* than ours: the Nikkei stands at 17,500–19,000 rather than near 10,000, and the largest groups have already cleaned up. That is why the in-world question is "whether it was necessary to go this far" — and why Keika, the clean bank, can be told to list "as a symbol."

### (g) The accounting sequence, one term from complete

The first edition's table still holds, and Volume 5 now supplies its last row.

| Volume | What is hidden, and how | Real case |
|---|---|---|
| Vol. 1 | Losses parked off the books and passed around (*tobashi*) | Yamaichi Securities, 1997 |
| Vol. 2 | Assets and cross-held shares carried at old book values | Japan's move to mark-to-market accounting |
| Vol. 3 | Debt and trading losses hidden in special-purpose entities; revenue round-tripped | Enron, 2001 |
| Vol. 4 | Ordinary running costs booked as investment, so profits look larger | WorldCom, 2002 — audited, like Enron, by Arthur Andersen (the novel's WCI uses "the same accounting firm as General Energy Online") |
| Vol. 5 | Credit risk pooled, re-rated AAA and sold on | Subprime securitisation, 2003–08 |

What is new in Volume 4 is her role. In Volumes 1–3 she *knew* a fraud would surface and positioned herself to survive it. Here she **chooses the day**, by fabricating the whistleblower. It is the first time she uses foreknowledge to cause a collapse, against a company whose fall she is positioned to profit from.

## §5. Neoliberalism, argued fairly

Kanbe's second lecture (Ch. 9) is the volume's intellectual centre and it is not a caricature.

- **The arithmetic.** A decent life for everyone costs forty times the country's assets. The only way to "have" that much is leverage — which is what the financial markets already do, and what the volume's last sentence points to.
- **The definition.** Neoliberalism "has an image of personal responsibility, but its true essence is in the way it gives handicaps": a chance for everyone, starting from unequal places. Equality of results was tried, and "the Eastern Bloc countries failed the pilot study" — a line with extra weight in a world where a socialist Japanese state existed until about 1990.
- **The sting.** We protect the weak "because we want to believe we're special." Charity is the privileged class's proof of itself — and "those people are called nobles in this country."
- **The instruction.** "You only have two hands… select as many of the weak as your hands can hold."

Set this beside Volume 3. There Runa answered Kanbe's "one genius outweighs a million" with a defence of the million. Here he tells her that her kind of rescue is a form of self-regard and is necessarily selective — which is an accurate description of *naniwabushi* capitalism (Vol. 3 map, Part II §11): she saves those she can reach, on her terms.

The author's own position is in the afterword: people "accepted it with wild enthusiasm," he "couldn't write about the theater politics negatively in the end," and "all of us must carry the past on our backs, merits and wrongdoings alike."

## §6. The war she built

**What she did, in order:**
- February 2000 — commissions a Saudi mining railway toward the Iraqi border (Ch. 12; the order itself is shown in Vol. 2 Ch. 5).
- Winter 2001–02 — tells Okazaki the US will attack Iraq; puts $10 billion of the WCI profits into a logistics city in Bahrain (Chs. 2, 3, 5).
- February 2002 — advises the president in person: invade in spring 2003, with a Gulf War-sized force and a coalition; pledges $12.5 billion to see him through the midterms (Ch. 5).
- Her PMC meanwhile provides legal cover for irregulars in Afghanistan (Ch. 3).
- Her design extends to Amman and Aqaba (Ch. 12).

**Why.** She says it to the president: "I want to change the history that will follow me… a second Vietnam." She remembers a war fought with too few troops and no plan for the aftermath, and is trying to buy America a better one.

**What it looked like from outside.** To Washington, "the earliest declaration of any party that they would participate." To the war's hard-liners, a supply of soldiers who "could die without any effect." To Koizumi, "a child is trying to help kill people."

**What stopped her, and what she learned.** She is removed in Chapter 9 and shown in Chapter 11 a paper that takes her own premise — fix the aftermath in advance — to its end: no aftermath, because no population. "I couldn't stand the ugly side of me who'd just sensed that a genocide was, from my perspective, the optimal solution."

**What Volume 5 adds — and it is not small.** In Vol. 5 Ch. 5 Runa says that the logic of the Chapter 11 paper was her own: deterrence by mass death was "what I'd tried to do by displaying my political strength to the neocons," with a nuclear strike on Fallujah as the instrument, and she broke down because "my thoughts were actually being implemented in real life." Read back into this volume, her advice at Salt Lake City and her "predicted future of Iraq" are darker than they appear here, and Koizumi's "a child is trying to help kill people" is closer to literal. The same chapter states that chemical and biological weapons *were* used in Afghanistan in this world.

**What the book claims about history.** That the war was coming regardless (the "axis of evil" speech is given before she has done more than build); that its justification was assembled (Ch. 3: "they already knew that Iraq had no such weapons"); and that the choice Japan faced was to help or not. The afterword of Volume 5 says the same in the author's voice.

*A note on the invented report:* see the caution in Chapter 11's entry. It has no historical basis, and its attribution in the text draws on a conspiracy motif.

## §7. Theatre politics and "posterity"

The afterword's question is "What, exactly, were the Koizumi theater politics?" The volume's answer is spread through its documents.

- **Television chooses.** "We were in the era of TV choosing the prime minister" (Ch. 5). A reformer's son stages a speech for cameras; the weeklies write the headline; the wide show re-cuts five years of Runa's life into a case (Ch. 8).
- **Who holds the power.** Not owners or executives but "the middle class" of the media (Ch. 5) — the same theory Runa offered in Volume 3 ("the ones on the ground hold the real power").
- **The performer.** Koizumi is "an actor" in Volume 3 and here one of the "first-rate actors — stars, even." Runa, a performer herself, grants him the stage.
- **The audience.** Koizumi "was good at reading the desires of the independents, who were practically a majority now." Yuujirou — a politician's son — "admires" him because "he never goes back on a public commitment." The heroine of the game "reflected the values of the world at that time."

Against the actors stands the thing Runa actually fears: "**posterity**. Eras of time were like gigantic waves." She can lose to people. What she cannot bear is that the outcome was never about people at all. It is the first time she states her opponent correctly — not Mizuho, not Koizumi, but the direction of the age: against privilege, against opacity, against patrons. She is all three.

## §8. Exits offered, and the game laid bare

**The exits.** Marry (Tachibana; the Lothringe "viewing"; the prime ministers' Cinderella). Leave Japan (Kanbe). Study music in Vienna (her coach). Step down "any time." Be a child (everyone). She declines each and keeps one of her own that the others do not know about until Chapter 12: examinations, for a life after ruin.

**What the game was.** Chapter 9's dream replaces the romance plot Runa assumed in Volume 1 with a political one:

- The issue is the school's privileges for noble and zaibatsu children.
- The reformers are scholarship students; Mizuho is their face.
- Runa is vice president of the council and a *reformist*: she "formed her own faction, split the opposition," sought compromise, promised improvement.
- The vote ties; her fiancé breaks it against her: "both of us are behind the times."
- It happens during the 2008 crisis, in a Japan where Koizumi had already broken the nobility.

**What this changes.** "Villainess" was a genre label on a moderate who lost. Chapter 5 adds how she now understands it: "a sacrifice, taken down by Japanese culture and its systems," defeated by "the embodiment of the average citizen" with television's "plot armor." And the chapter in which she says this is the one in which she buys a student election for ¥100 million. The living Runa is, for the moment, less of a reformist than the game's.

## §9. The reference shelf

The author's habit of telling the story through other works continues; in this volume the works cluster around three ideas.

**Sufficiency and its refusal.**
- *Stay Gold* — the horse of "nearly," who wins at the last. Runa backs the story over the result.
- Laozi's "he who knows sufficiency" and Takemura Masayoshi's *A Small but Shining Japan* — Kiyomaro's counter-text, the programme of a modest country, handed to a girl building a colossus. The same maxim was carved on the basin at Ryoan-ji in Volume 3.
- Coco Chanel — simplicity, and never negligence: the household's ethic of delegated labour.

**Farewell and departure.**
- "Canta Per Me" (*Noir*) — "a song about an eternal farewell," sung "to my future self."
- *Kino no Tabi* — a traveller who never stays more than three days anywhere.
- *Maria-sama ga Miteru: Rainy Blue* — remembered for an unbearable parting.
- The Tora-san film of Volume 3 belongs here too.

**Power, promises and scapegoats.**
- *Diplomacy* — a game whose promises bind nobody, refereed by a CIA officer.
- *Hyouka* — a schoolboy scapegoat remembered as a hero.
- Suzuki Shoten — the trading house whose 1927 failure set off the Shōwa financial panic and, the glossary notes, strengthened the zaibatsu and the military: Okazaki's oddly chosen consolation.
- Heinlein's *The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress* and Cinderella — the two stories the heads of government tell about her: revolution, and a spell with a deadline.
- *Pom Poko* and *Metal Gear Solid* — the comic register of the same theme: things that cannot be surveilled.

*Strongly supported:* these are chosen, not decorative; the glossary identifies each. *My reading:* the first group is what the adults say to her, the second is what she says to herself, and the third is what the world says about her.

## §10. Where the volume strains

- **Scale.** $400 billion of leveraged positions run from London by one man, unnoticed, in a market the size of 2001's NASDAQ, is beyond plausibility; so is a boycott by every systems vendor on earth.
- **A protagonist everywhere at once.** In six months an eleven-year-old runs a student council, a kendo club, a merger, a US midterm strategy and a field visit to a bank's machine room. The book half-acknowledges it ("this wasn't a job for a fifth grader").
- **Continuity.** The railway thread is dated October 2001 and discusses mid-2002; the boys' profit is ¥6 billion in one chapter and ¥8 billion in the next; Runa's stake in their company is "sixty billion" where the arithmetic requires six; Kazuki Shiori is "Katsuki" again. The author warned of this in Volume 3's afterword.
- **Translation noise.** Koizumi's slogan appears as "no reform and no growth"; noble privilege is called "diplomatic immunity" throughout; several sentences in the political summaries are garbled enough that the sense has to be inferred from the glossary.
- **The invented report.** Chapter 11's document does necessary work — it converts the defeat into mercy — but it attributes an imaginary plan for genocide to a real country's leadership and labels its authors by religion. It is the volume's least defensible choice.
- **Thin chapters.** Chapters 7, 8 and 10 are sketches. Each earns its place thematically, but the mosaic method is visible.

# Part III — Synthesis

## Alternate-history ledger

**Preserved:** the aftermath of Enron and the collapse of the telecom giant audited by the same firm; the fight over Compaq; the Salt Lake City Olympics under heavy security; the "axis of evil" turn and the road to Iraq; the split between America's defence and diplomatic establishments over the size of the invasion force; Koizumi's popularity, the dismissal of his foreign minister, and the run of political scandals and resignations of early 2002 ("spring theater"); the megabank computer failure of April 2002; the mislabelled-beef scandal; a struggling Hokkaido airline; the football World Cup; the Financial Revival Programme (seven points; halve bad loans by fiscal 2004); the first signs of the subprime boom.

**Altered:**
- **The telecom collapse comes half a year early** (end of 2001, not mid-2002) — because Runa forges the tip that exposes it.
- **Compaq is bought by a Japanese fund**; the American executive who in reality won that fight comes to work for it. The result is **Keika Electronics Union** (this world's Sanyo + Fujitsu + Compaq).
- **A Japanese trading company builds a Saudi railway** toward the Iraqi border from February 2000 (ordered in Vol. 2 Ch. 5, on the back of a real oil-concession negotiation that in reality failed), and a logistics city in Bahrain; both pass to American hands before the war.
- **A Japanese-owned military company** holds the UN licence under which Indian irregulars operate in Afghanistan.
- **The White House knows Iraq has no weapons** and builds its case on an attempted purchase and on confessions obtained in Tokyo.
- **No city has ever been destroyed by a nuclear weapon** — there was no Hiroshima or Nagasaki (Ch. 11). This is why a plan for nuclear strikes "to set an example" can circulate in Washington.
- **The president is advised on the timing and scale of the invasion by an eleven-year-old**, who also bankrolls his midterms.
- **The Pentagon and the White House are "still recovering from… being struck by planes"** (carried over from Vol. 3).
- **Japan is richer:** the Nikkei ends 2001 near 17,500 (really about 10,500); its population is 140 million; bad loans are smaller.
- **The reform programme arrives about four months early** and is applied to a healthier banking system — hence the in-world doubt "whether it was necessary to go this far."
- **The fall of a ruling-party grandee** (really a secretary's tax case in 2002) runs through a Sakata land deal tied to Runa's father.
- **China's capital is Nanjing**; Karafuto is Japanese; a peerage with legal immunity and a Privy Council exist — and the cabinet moves to abolish the immunity.
- **A regional bank that really failed in 2003** is absorbed by Keika two years early and without public money (Ch. 2); a Sapporo department store and a Hokkaido builder that really went under are shown, in the banker's memoir, as having been kept alive (Ch. 1).

**Wholly fictional:** the Moonlight Fund and its $400 billion operation; the guardians' conspiracy; the Lothringe marriage viewing; the student-council election; the think-tank report (see the content note); the leaders' telephone call; the dream of the game; Hotaru's raid on the tower.

## Interpretive possibilities

**Strongly supported**
- The volume's subject is accountability, not ability: Runa is not out-thought; she is ruled out.
- Each of the three blows (guardians, television, prime minister) is delivered by people acting in good faith, and the book treats good faith as no defence.
- The WCI operation is the point at which she becomes what Volume 3 feared — and she is rewarded for it, which is why the later scolding has to come from outside her circle.
- The defeat is written as mercy, and the afterword says so.
- The game's "villainess" was a moderate destroyed by a change of era.

**Plausible**
- The adults' common solution for her is marriage — Tachibana, the Lothringe viewing, the leaders' Cinderella — and the series is setting that up to be refused or redefined.
- Ichijou's loyalist faction and Angela's succession are the two poles of a later struggle over the group.
- Koizumi's private motives are mixed; the telephone call shows him taking the profits of her strategy.

**Speculative**
- The staged takeover in Chapter 2 and the dawn raid in Chapter 9 share an author among her guardians.
- The video message was kept for five years because someone in the household — the "co-head maid" who sends it — expected it to be needed.

## On the "What is Runa?" question

Volume 4 moves the question from *what* she is to *what she is allowed to be*.

- **Gifted.** Kanbe gives her the clinical word and its consequence: a child who "can avoid taking responsibility."
- **A monster in waiting.** "Your country also fears that she might turn into a monster… Russia feels the same."
- **A sacrifice.** Her own reading of the game's Runa: "taken down by Japanese culture and its systems."
- **Cassandra, by decree.** In Volumes 2 and 3 the name described her; here two heads of government assign it.
- **The Moon Mistress.** Washington's nickname, from a novel about a revolution run by a mind nobody elected.
- **Someone who died once.** Chapter 11 tells the first life straight for the first time — and explains the series' central blindness with it: "I'd lived my second life in the world of this game… other people here had wills of their own."
- **A child.** The word every adult uses, and the one thing about her that is simply true. The tape proves it: the same person, at six, was afraid of the future she had already lived.

The volume's answer is the reply she gives the tape. Asked by her younger self whether she is happy, an eleven-year-old who has just learned the cost of her happiness says yes — and eats.
