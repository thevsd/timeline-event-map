# Modern Villainess Vol. 4 — Complete Event Map
### Chapter-by-chapter, with economic analysis and the defeat arc

**Scope:** All 12 chapters plus two interstitial documents, all glossaries, afterword, synthesis.

**Coverage:** Winter 2001 to spring 2002, confirmed by the afterword.

**The volume's thesis, stated by the author.** The afterword is unusually direct: this is the volume in which **Runa is defeated**, and he took criticism for it. "As an author from Narou, I belong to a heretical group of people who love stories where the protagonist is defeated… the readers refuse to accept stories where the main character gets defeated, and I still remember the many criticisms I received over the main part of this volume." His stated reason:

> "Even if the child in question wanted it, would you really make a child still in elementary school sign an order for murder?… It's the kindness of the adults who don't allow that child to make such a choice, and also their responsibility as the adults who hold the current era on their shoulders. There's no such thing as a child who hasn't been scolded by an adult. In short, that's what this volume is about."

He also names the volume's subject outright: **neoliberalism**, and "Koizumi theater politics." And he refuses to write Koizumi as a villain, crediting his administration with two achievements that "remain even today" — ending bad-debt disposal, and building the Japan–US relationship.

# Part I — Chapter-by-Chapter Map

## Chapter 1 — The Banker Reminisces

**Narrative position:** A retrospective memoir by an anonymous ex-Far Eastern Bank employee. Re-tells Volume 1's bank rescue from inside, with mechanics Volume 1 never supplied.

**What happens**
- The narrator worked in Far Eastern Bank's loan screening department and saw the real books.
- He supplies the **call market** explanation that Vol. 1 omitted: institutions fund daily operations by lending to each other, and being cut off means the market has judged you unable to repay. Sankai Securities, Ichiyama Securities and Hokkaido Kaitaku Bank had all been cut off — "the fuses of their destruction had been lit." Short sellers then moved in to deliver the killing blow and profit from it.
- **This is why all three had to be saved simultaneously.** Rescuing only the two securities firms would have concentrated every short seller on Hokkaido Kaitaku Bank, guaranteeing its collapse.
- The Keika Rules are restated as a four-point protocol: force out all executives; punish wrongdoing; send all bad debts including *tobashi* to the RCC; obtain BOJ special loans for stability.
- MOF's **vertically segmented bureaucracy** meant the banking and securities sections never compared notes, so nobody realized all three were one problem. Far Eastern Bank's approach made it "common sense."
- Ichiyama's concealed losses, sokaiya payments and off-book debts made it ineligible for a BOJ special loan — hence the reverse takeover, rescuing it "under the pretense of only saving Sankai Securities."
- **Amazaki Construction**, a major general contractor with ¥100bn in interest-bearing debt and two thousand Hokkaido subcontractors, was saved specifically because the Keika Group's railway expansion gave it work, starting with the Shikoku Shinkansen. This is retroactive justification for the railway strategy.
- **Kyomei Bank** emerges from the former LPCB and Nihon Credit Bank as a superregional bank spanning Tokyo, Osaka and Hokkaido.
- Keika Holdings' internal politics are a mess: a Hokkaido faction pushing former Kaitaku executives against CEO Ichijou. Ichijou responds by importing foreign-capital talent and creating a succession race — current frontrunner, **Angela Sullivan**.
- The narrator ends up an executive at a Tokyo IT venture, and closes: "now that the Koizumi administration is toughening up on the processing of bad debts, it's possible that everything under Hokkaido Kaitaku Bank would crumble if the same thing happened today."

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* Opening with an outsider's retrospective is now the series' habit, but this one does specific work — it establishes Runa's achievement as **historically settled** before the volume takes it away from her.
- *Strongly supported:* The Angela-as-successor detail is planted here and pays off in Ch. 12 with a very different colour.

## Chapter 2 — The Lady's Pursuits: Horses, Furukawa Telecoms, and Conflict

**Narrative position:** The volume's longest chapter, and the opening move of the defeat arc — though the reader only learns that partway through.

**What happens**
- **Hong Kong.** Runa flies in for the Hong Kong Vase at Sha Tin Racecourse to buy a racehorse — one about to retire after a career of near-misses, who finally beat the World Series Racing Championship holder at Dubai. She identifies with him explicitly: "as a villainess doomed to defeat, I couldn't help but sympathize with the horse."
- She meets **Karl and Franz Lothringe**, European aristocrats from the game's unused backstory. Their family was revived by postwar Austria's need to avoid absorption into a Cold War-strengthened Germany; European blue bloods consequently dominate EU bureaucracy and diplomacy, which is part of why Japanese nobles operate freely. Franz was scrapped as a study-abroad love interest, likely for length.
- Franz's verdict on the horse: "Results are all that matter. That's how it is in the world of competitions."
- **The Furukawa Telecoms crisis.** A hostile takeover bid is launched by the **Machishita Fund**, backed by **Iron Partners**, controlled by the American securities firm **Charles & Edward**. They seek only 33.4% rather than the usual 51% — enough for veto rights.
- Their real target is Furukawa's **just under 40% stake in Furukawa Automatic Machine Manufacturing**, the market leader in machine tools and robotics.
- The true instigator is revealed: **Teikoku Telephone Corporation**, for whom Furukawa Telecoms subcontracts. Keika's banks ousted an ineffective Furukawa president who had been placed there through that connection — and whose father was a director at the parent before privatization. The takeover is revenge dressed as rescue, designed to cast Keika as the villain.
- **Ichijou's assessment of an all-out fight: "We'll be defeated."** The reason is concrete. The Moonlight Fund earns in dollars, converts through crude oil sales into yen, and that lag can't be compressed. Worse, the **¥2 trillion Shinjuku Shinkansen** was paid in a lump sum for a discount. "We would have had the cash necessary to oppose the takeover bid if we hadn't made that investment."
- Meanwhile **Shinden Bank** and a list of regional banks (Sendai City, Echigo Central, Wakayama Shiawase, Shinmin, Imperial Aiwa) need rescuing — all survivors of 1997 *because of* Runa's intervention, now failing under Koizumi's accelerated bad-debt disposal. Tachibana points out that regional bank rescues require local engagement, not RCC dumping: "We had the money, just not the manpower for it."
- Runa stands down and pivots to defending **Shiyo Electric Co.** via a share buyback. Her strategic reasoning is genuine: Shiyo's small LCD screens and batteries plus Furukawa's telecoms position would let them set global standards for mobile phones rather than falling into **Galapagos Syndrome**. "This is how we bring the world under our control."
- **Then the POV shifts, and the reader learns the whole thing was staged.** Ichijou and Nakamaro, in the office next door: "It's not like we can tell her that Tachibana-san and Secretary Angela know this was all a calculated move by our own people."
- The real sequence: after Keika forced out the Teikoku president, Teikoku retaliated by getting **every systems developer — Japanese and foreign — to cancel Keika Holdings' core banking orders simultaneously.** Faced with that, Ichijou, Tachibana, Nakamaro and Angela struck a deal. Teikoku's single condition: **"Get that young lady under control."**
- So they manufactured the takeover bid, invented the regional-bank cash squeeze as cover, and steered her toward Shiyo — all to make her stand down without knowing she'd been managed.
- **The WorldCom operation.** Separately, Okazaki has raised roughly **$20 billion** from Gulf oil money at 9% — deliberately not under Keika or Moonlight names, so "he could jump off a building and the fallout would never reach me." Routed through paper companies in the **Isle of Man → Cayman Islands → Virgin Islands**, then leveraged twenty times to **$400 billion**.
- Runa manufactures a whistleblower report — sent from a disposable address at a London internet café — revealing accounting fraud at **WCI Inc.**, the second-largest US long-distance company, which uses the same auditor as General Energy Online. She stages the reveal for Angela, proposing they suppress it to protect the president, knowing Angela will report it and the CIA will route it to the White House. Since WCI is also a major presidential donor, protecting them would be fatal.
- The takeover bid resolves theatrically: Machishita and Iron Partners split, Iron Partners raises to ¥2,600 and files an injunction, and Machishita leads a **white knight syndicate** with Teikoku Telephone at ¥2,800. The media frames it as all-Japanese companies protecting one of their own from foreign money.

**Revelations**
- Runa's inner circle now actively manipulates her — with the CIA's assistance.
- Teikoku Telephone can paralyze Keika Holdings' entire IT infrastructure with a phone call.
- Runa is running an independent, leveraged, offshore war chest through Okazaki.

**Historical references**
- **Furukawa Telecoms = Fujitsu. Very high confidence.** Real: Fujitsu descends from Furukawa Electric, was a core NTT-family supplier, and holds a major stake in **Fanuc** — whose name comes from **Fu**ji **A**utomatic **NU**merical **C**ontrol, matching "Furukawa Automatic Machine Manufacturing," the real world leader in machine tools and robotics.
- **Teikoku Telephone Corporation = NTT. Very high confidence.** "Teikoku" means "Imperial"; NTT's supplier network was literally called the *denden* family, and the privatization reference matches NTT's 1985 privatization.
- **Charles & Edward = Merrill Lynch. High confidence** — founders Charles Merrill and Edmund Lynch.
- **Machishita Fund ≈ the Murakami Fund. Moderate-high.** Japan's famous activist fund of 1999–2006; "Murakami" (village-above) inverted to "Machishita" (town-below).
- **WCI = WorldCom. Very high confidence.** Real: second-largest US long-distance carrier, audited by Arthur Andersen (Enron's auditor), collapsed in the largest US bankruptcy to that date in July 2002 after an internal whistleblower exposed improperly capitalized expenses.
- **The American computer manufacturer that "ousted their female CEO after a proxy fight"** references HP and Carly Fiorina and the 2002 Compaq merger fight — **altered**, since Fiorina won that proxy contest and was removed in 2005.
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** veto rights at 33.4%; third-party allotment and dilution; stock buybacks as takeover defense; convertible bonds used deliberately for dilution as a poison pill; **white knight**; tax havens and layered money laundering; the definitions of millionaire and billionaire; **Suzuki Shoten**, the real pre-war trading house whose failure "warped Japan's history"; the **Showa financial crisis** and the Privy Council's decisive role in it, leading to "zaibatsu strengthening and the rise of the military"; Kai Tak Airport; Japan–US trade friction.
- **Confirmed by the glossary, and pointed:** "**Turnaround manager**… Runa buys the failed companies and industries altogether, which means she also revives the entire industry herself, fortunately. This is why Wall Street looks at her and thinks, 'What the hell is she doing…?'"

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* The horse is the volume's thesis in miniature. She buys an animal whose entire meaning is a career of dignified losing, immediately before losing.
- *Strongly supported:* The staged takeover is the series' most morally complicated act so far. Her guardians conspire with a hostile corporation and a foreign intelligence service to engineer her defeat — out of concern for her.
- *Plausible:* Franz's "results are all that matter" is the position the whole volume argues against.

## Chapter 9 — The Lady's Defensive War

**Narrative position:** The volume's centre. Runa's defeat, delivered personally by the Prime Minister.

**What happens**
- **Professor Kanbe's neoliberalism lecture.** He opens with arithmetic: ¥4 million a year × 80 years × 140 million people = ¥44.8 quadrillion, against national assets of just over ¥1 quadrillion. Then the trick — apply fifty times leverage and you have enough. "Probably not. But isn't it a fascinating thought experiment?"
- Asked why they don't simply do what makes everyone happy, he redirects to the definition of happiness, then to **handicaps**: not everyone can live in the city, and those already there have to give up their chairs or automatically win. "Neoliberalism. This country has an image of personal responsibility, but its true essence is in the way it gives handicaps… it's about equality of opportunities and results."
- Equality of results, he notes, "is what's called communism or socialism. Around the time you were born, the Eastern Bloc countries failed the pilot study for these systems in a major way."
- Asked whether it's unfair: "Sure. It's based on the premise that people aren't equal."
- Asked why humans protect the weak: "**No, it's because we want to believe we're special.**" He writes "privileged class," then **noblesse oblige**, and gives her the lecture's real instruction: "you only have two hands… Decide if you want to save them or force their sacrifice, then select as many of the weak as your hands can hold."
- **The squeeze.** Tachibana is being targeted over Keika Railway; Ichijou is under fire over Keika Holdings' listing. Toudou and Keika-Iwazaki Pharma are untouched. Anisha's read: "They're only trying to cut off your own functioning limbs, my lady." Koizumi is after people, not money. The Izumikawa faction is immobilized inside the cabinet; Koizumi's approval is still 40% after falling.
- **Okazaki's report:** the Cabinet Information Research Office is onto the Bahrain work. Runa's involvement in preparing for the **Iraq War** is exposed.
- **Tachibana defects.** "My lady, please withdraw from Iraq." Angela and Eva say nothing — meaning the US and Japan have agreed to remove her together. Runa: "I realized that the prime minister wasn't my only enemy in this case."
- Koizumi's simultaneous announcements: public funds injected into all major megabanks to finish bad-debt disposal; a government system to purchase problem stock holdings; a bill to **outlaw nobles' special privileges in criminal cases**; and talks with Russia toward a peace treaty on the Northern Karafuto Crisis.
- **The dream.** Runa sees the game's climax for the first time in detail. In high school, the class committees split over abolishing noble and zaibatsu privileges at the school — a reform driven by **scholarship students** at their boiling point over discrimination, fronted by **Takanashi Mizuho**. Eiichi is student council president, Yuujirou secretary, Mitsuya treasurer, Runa vice president.
- Crucially: "**Keikain Runa was a reformist despite being a noble.**" She had formed her own faction, split the opposition, and was seeking compromise. The game made her lose anyway.
- The vote ties. The tiebreaker falls to the president — her own fiancé. "As president, I have decided to adopt this proposal." And afterward: "**Keikain, both of us are behind the times.**"
- Waking, Runa concludes she doesn't mind losing to Mizuho, Eiichi or Koizumi — "They were all first-rate actors—stars, even." What she cannot accept is being defeated by **posterity**: "Eras of time were like gigantic waves that destroyed everything in their path."
- **The confrontation.** At the PM's residence, under cover of a Hokkaido produce event, she asks what she did wrong.
  - "It's an adult's job to stop children when they're heading in the wrong direction."
  - "**A child is trying to help kill people. Don't you think any adult would put a stop to that?**"
  - "When you fainted during the terrorist attacks and I caught you, I was shocked at how light you were. I had to chastise myself for letting such a small child take on the fate of this country, and even the whole world."
  - She fires back that it's a politician's job to bear that if it's the best option. He answers with democracy: "the person who sits in my seat represents the will of the majority of our people. Keikain-kun, do you intend to leave both me and the people who chose me in the history books as those who helped a child commit murder for the good of their country?"
  - Then the real charge: "**You simply aren't qualified.**" She isn't a Diet member; she belongs to a dukedom but isn't a duke; she isn't a soldier; she may control the Keika Group but doesn't represent it. "You leave everything to proxies… Think back to the fixers of long ago. Even they came to face the public when the time was right. Right now, you can't even choose to do that. **That's why you'll end up betrayed in times like these.**"
  - She cries out: what if she isn't qualified until after her downfall? "You'll just have to live with it. Accepting the past from one's predecessors and giving the future to people like you is the job of adults."
  - He hands her a handkerchief. "Keikain-kun, at the very least, I believe in this country's people. I believe in their will to change… can't you wait a little longer? Don't take on the dishonor of letting the people sacrifice you for this country's prosperity."
- **Eiichi phones.** "You reached out to Yuujirou when everything happened last time, right? I figured it was our turn now."
- She asks him when he would betray her. "Right now, I'd go find the version of me that betrays you and beat him up… I mean, you'd be a wounded beast at that point, right, Runa? There's no way your risk management would be any good." Then, honestly: "**Still, if I had to go up against you, I'd never win unless I took you down with me.**"
- She realizes the dream-Eiichi's line was addressed to her, not Mizuho: "He'd gone down with me so that he could win."

**Revelations**
- The game's central conflict is finally specified: **scholarship-student discrimination and the abolition of school privileges**, not romance.
- The in-game Runa was a **reformist** who lost — which reframes "villainess" entirely.
- Eiichi's canonical betrayal was a deliberate mutual destruction, not abandonment.
- Koizumi's objection is not that she's a child, or wrong, but that she is **unaccountable**.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* Koizumi's "you aren't qualified" is the single most important speech in the series to date. Every volume has shown Runa operating through proxies specifically so that liability stops elsewhere — a structure introduced in Vol. 1 as cleverness. Koizumi reframes it as the defect that makes her power illegitimate, and the text does not give her a rebuttal.
- *Strongly supported:* The author's afterword confirms this was the intended shape. The defeat is administered by an adult exercising responsibility, not by an enemy.
- *Strongly supported:* Kanbe's lecture and Koizumi's speech are the same argument from two directions. Kanbe: you have two hands, choose whom to save. Koizumi: you have no mandate, so the choice isn't yours.
- *Plausible:* "I don't mind losing to first-rate actors; I mind losing to posterity" is Runa articulating, for the first time, what she has actually been fighting — not Mizuho, not Koizumi, but historical inevitability.

## Chapter 12 — An Unappealing Banquet

**Narrative position:** POV of Okazaki and Ichijou. Closes the volume by showing the adults dividing up the aftermath — and naming what comes next.

**What happens**
- **The Saudi railway is sold.** Akamatsu held 49% of a Saudi mining railway from Dammam Port to **King Khalid Military City**, with the Iraqi border a few dozen kilometres north. Finalized February 2000, rushed to 80% completion — "compelled to have it finished by the start of the war."
- Runa's fuller plan, relayed to the PMC CEO: extend the line to **Amman**, then to **Aqaba** on the Red Sea — the answer to what Washington hardliners call "**Baghdad departure, Jerusalem arrival**." It would let US troops move with frightening ease, pressure Syria, and cover western Iraq. "Indeed, Lady Runa has predicted the future of Iraq. Be sure to reflect that in the final sales price."
- The CEO's response is concern, not admiration: "is that boss of ours really all right?" Okazaki: "She's not well. She nearly broke down." The CEO: "**We need to be grateful that this country's prime minister brought the boss back down to earth and scolded her.** But at the same time, we need to shoulder the responsibility for the future, so that she doesn't fall apart."
- **The succession lunch.** Ichijou meets Okazaki, opening with "I thought you hated me." — "I do. You led Lady Runa down a dangerous road. But in the business world, you can't just refuse to meet someone because you hate them."
- Ichijou lays out the exit plan: **Tachibana will step down** from Keika Railway before the Diet removes noble diplomatic immunity; **Ichijou will use Keika Holdings' public listing to step back** to a non-representative chairmanship. Three to five years out, **Angela and Karin will lead the Keika Group**.
- The danger: Angela and Karin will recruit from abroad, and those hires' loyalty will be to them. Iwazaki secondees will be loyal to Iwazaki or the Keikain family. "At that point, the Keika Group will become a hodgepodge colony for the Iwazaki zaibatsu, with no one remaining who was there from the start… and Lady Runa will still be a minor."
- Hence Okazaki: "You're the only one. You're a manager with the right skills and you've sworn allegiance to Lady Runa." The lunch's real purpose is to build a loyalty faction holding the key Japanese posts. "Miss Angela wants to get you away from Lady Runa."
- **The ultranationalist thread.** Elements criticizing the Keika Group as a "sellout company" and targeting Runa for her birth and blonde hair have aligned, bizarrely, with America. Ichijou explains it as a loyalty test — "Are you with us or against us?" — and notes the ultranationalist logic on Iwazaki: "Iwazaki exists alongside this nation. That's why the Keika Group needs to merge with the Iwazaki zaibatsu and form the foundation of a **Heisei Restoration**." Ichijou admits he doesn't understand it.
- **The puppet master** behind the year's manoeuvres is named: "**The 'Ministries.'**" Not one actor but the bureaucracies of both countries — Japan's Foreign, Military, and Economy/Trade ministries; America's Defense and State Departments — now fighting a "terrible mudslinging contest using Lady Runa's plan." Koizumi didn't seize her Iraq preparations to run them; he deliberately scattered them, and the departments erupted.
- **Ichijou's confession.** Tachibana told him why Runa behaves as she does: she holds bookkeeping, secretarial and English qualifications and is aiming for early college graduation. "She clearly believes the Keika Group may collapse and is preparing for it to happen. However, Tachibana-san and I refuse to let her grow up only to have nothing left!" Okazaki: "You really love Lady Runa too, don't you?" Ichijou: "Of course I do. I'm the second guy she scouted."
- **The last line of the volume.** Okazaki mentions Wall Street is up to something, with Angela involved. Ichijou asks what. Okazaki: "**Apparently, there are these things called subprime loans…**"

**Revelations**
- Runa's entire empire has an expiry date her own executives have already scheduled.
- She has been quietly qualifying herself for a life after total collapse.
- The antagonist was never a person — it's institutional self-interest in two governments.
- The next volume's subject is named in the final sentence.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* Ending on "subprime loans" closes the loop the series opened with. Volume 1 Chapter 1 was 15 September 2008; Volume 4 ends in 2002 with two middle-aged men over cold beer naming the mechanism that will produce it. The series' countdown is now explicit.
- *Strongly supported:* "The Ministries" is the most sophisticated antagonist the series has produced — diffuse, non-malicious, and unbeatable by acquisition.
- *Plausible:* Ichijou's outburst is the emotional payoff of four volumes. The banker Runa blackmailed in Vol. 1 is now the man organizing a secret faction to preserve something for her after everyone else has gone.

# Part II — Economic and Historical Analysis

## §1. The accounting-fraud trilogy completes

Volume 3 gave us Enron. Volume 4 gives us **WorldCom**, and the connection is the one that mattered historically: a shared auditor. The novel's WCI uses "the same accounting firm as General Energy Online" — in reality Arthur Andersen, which audited both and was destroyed by the pair of them.

The series' running argument now has four terms:

| Volume | Mechanism | Real case |
|---|---|---|
| Vol. 1 | *Tobashi* loss concealment | Yamaichi Securities |
| Vol. 2 | Book value vs. mark-to-market | Japan's accounting big bang |
| Vol. 3 | Round-tripping, off-balance-sheet SPEs | Enron |
| Vol. 4 | Improper capitalization, auditor capture | WorldCom |
| **Next** | **Subprime** | **2008** |

What's new here is that Runa doesn't merely predict the fraud — she **triggers it on her own schedule**, manufacturing an anonymous whistleblower report and routing it through her own secretary to the CIA to the White House. This is the first time in the series she uses foreknowledge to *cause* a collapse rather than survive one, and the volume notes without comment that she does it to a company whose destruction she is positioned to profit from.

## §2. Why she actually lost: the currency-lag problem

Ichijou's "We'll be defeated" is the most concrete financial constraint the series has ever put on her, and it's correct.

The Moonlight Fund's structure: yen → dollars → crude oil → shipped to Japan → sold → yen. Every leg takes time. She has enormous **assets** and almost no **liquidity**, because converting takes roughly a month and she just spent ¥2 trillion in a lump sum on the Shinjuku Shinkansen to capture a bulk discount.

This is a real and well-understood failure mode: a balance sheet can be enormously solvent and still lose a takeover fight, because takeover fights are settled in cash on a deadline. It also quietly indicts the lump-sum discount decision — an optimization that destroyed her optionality.

The regional-bank problem is the same lesson in a different register. Tachibana's objection is not financial but operational: rescuing regional banks means engaging with local economies and politics, and dumping their debts on the RCC makes things worse. "We had the money, just not the manpower for it." This is the constraint Kiyomaro identified in Vol. 3 — managerial depth, not capital — now binding in practice.

## §3. Neoliberalism, presented fairly

Professor Kanbe's lecture is the most intellectually careful passage in the series so far, and it's notable that the book lets the argument stand rather than caricaturing it.

His framing — neoliberalism as a system of **handicaps** rather than of personal responsibility, distinguishing equality of opportunity from equality of results — is a defensible academic summary. His observation that communism and socialism are equality of results, and that "the Eastern Bloc countries failed the pilot study," is delivered flatly rather than triumphantly.

And his answer to why humans protect the weak — "it's because we want to believe we're special" — is genuinely uncomfortable, and is immediately attached to *noblesse oblige* and the privileged class. He is telling a duke's daughter that her charity is self-definition. The book does not soften this.

The afterword confirms the seriousness of intent: "people are beginning to rethink neoliberalism, but back then, the people accepted it with wild enthusiasm when it was presented by the government… what I want to emphasize is how the people of that time chose those things. All of us must carry the past on our backs, merits and wrongdoings alike."

## §4. The accountability argument

Koizumi's "you simply aren't qualified" deserves separate treatment because it retroactively reframes three volumes.

Since Volume 1, Runa's operating method has been proxy control: Tachibana and Ichijou sign, she decides, and — as Vol. 3 stated explicitly — "if anything bad happened, the buck stopped with those three and I would experience no backlash." The series presented this as the necessary workaround for a minor with no legal capacity.

Koizumi identifies it as the defect. Not a child, not wrong, not even dangerous — **unaccountable**. No mandate, no office, no title she actually holds, no public face she can present. And he draws the consequence precisely: "That's why you'll end up betrayed in times like these." An operator with no standing has no protection when the people acting for her decide otherwise.

The volume then demonstrates it. Chapter 2's staged takeover and Chapter 9's Tachibana defection are exactly the betrayal Koizumi describes, and both are committed by people who love her.

## §5. Where the volume strains

- **The staged takeover requires implausible coordination.** Every Japanese *and* foreign systems developer cancels simultaneously, then Teikoku, Merrill-analogue, an activist fund, the CIA, and four of Runa's closest people all execute a months-long deception flawlessly.
- **The $400 billion figure is enormous.** $20bn at twenty times leverage is arithmetically fine but represents a scale that would be historically conspicuous.
- **The Saudi railway is a large invention** placed into real Iraq War logistics, and the "Baghdad departure, Jerusalem arrival" framing attributes to Washington hardliners an ambition the historical record supports only partially.
- **Chapters 3, 4, 7, 8, 10 and 11 are brief and structurally light** — this volume's mosaic approach sometimes produces chapters that are mainly connective tissue.

## On the "what is Runa?" question

Volume 4 supplies the clearest answer the series has given, and it substantially revises the whole frame.

**The "villainess" was never the right description.** The dream in Chapter 9 shows the game's Keikain Runa as a **reformist noble** who formed a faction, split the opposition, and sought compromise with the scholarship students — and lost anyway. She wasn't defeated because she was wicked. She was defeated because she was, in Eiichi's words, "behind the times." The label she has organized her identity around for four volumes turns out to be a genre convention laid over someone whose actual crime was being on the losing side of a reform she partly agreed with.

**And what she's actually afraid of is finally named.** "I don't mind losing… Not to Takanashi Mizuho and not to Teia Eiichi. Not to Koizumi Souichirou… However, the one thing I didn't want to defeat me was posterity. Eras of time were like gigantic waves." That is the fear driving everything since Volume 1 — not the game's script, but the sense that history is a force no amount of effort survives.

**The private preparation is the tell.** Ichijou reveals she holds bookkeeping, secretarial and English qualifications and is aiming for early college graduation. She has been quietly equipping herself to survive as an ordinary competent adult after losing everything. That is not a villainess hedging her downfall; it is someone who has never actually believed any of this is hers to keep.

*Strongly supported:* the villainess frame is now demonstrably a misreading — the text has shown us the original and she was a reformist.
*Strongly supported:* her driving motive is dread of historical inevitability, stated in her own words.
*Plausible:* the qualifications are the most honest thing about her — evidence she has always expected to end up with nothing, which is exactly what Ichijou and Tachibana have now organized themselves to prevent.
*Speculative:* if the school-reform vote is the downfall, then Koizumi's abolition of noble privileges in this volume may be its direct cause — the legislative change that makes the school reform inevitable years later.
