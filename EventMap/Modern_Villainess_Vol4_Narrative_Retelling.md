## Before we begin: A book about being told no

Most web novels of this kind follow the same template, and the author of this one says so himself in the afterword. The hero starts as a child, and the story stacks up advantage after advantage for them. He describes himself as one of "a heretical group of people who love stories where the protagonist is defeated," and he admits he still remembers the angry comments he got over this book. Volume 4 is the volume in which Keikain Runa loses.

What makes it worth reading is who she loses to. It isn't the game's heroine, a rival company or a scheming relative. She loses to the prime minister of Japan, in person, and before that to the people who love her most. The author gives his reason in one plain question: "Even if the child in question wanted it, would you really make a child still in elementary school sign an order for murder?" Then he sums up the whole book in two sentences I think about a lot: "There's no such thing as a child who hasn't been scolded by an adult. In short, that's what this volume is about."

The story runs from December 2001 to the late summer of 2002, the first full year of Prime Minister Koizumi's government. If you weren't following Japanese politics then, two words will help. The first is **neoliberalism**, the belief that markets and personal responsibility should do more and the state should do less. In Japan in those years it meant finally forcing the banks to write off their bad loans, selling off state corporations, and letting failing companies fail. The second is **"theater politics,"** the nickname for Koizumi's style: politics staged for the television cameras, with a slogan, a set of villains and a hero who never backs down. The author lived through it, and he is honest about his mixed feelings. He says that "as someone who lived through that era, I couldn't write about the theater politics negatively in the end," and he credits the government with two lasting achievements: it ended the bad-debt crisis, and it rebuilt Japan's foreign relations around the alliance with America. He also lists its faults, and then adds the line that sets the tone for everything that follows: "what I want to emphasize is how the people of that time chose those things."

The book is built like a mosaic. Around four long chapters in Runa's own voice sit a banker's memoir, a briefing in Washington, a chapter about the boys alone, a comic interlude, a television transcript, a phone call between two heads of government and a business lunch. Running through all of it is one lesson, delivered three times and less gently each time. First her own guardians try to rein her in, quietly and with money. Then a daytime television show explains her to the whole country. Finally the prime minister does it to her face. It's a bit like being told to slow down first by your family, then by the neighbours, and finally by a police officer.

I came to this volume braced for a punishment. What I found was more interesting: a book about who is allowed to hold power, and about adults who, for once, do what adults are for.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Web novel template**: the common pattern in stories first serialised online, where a hero (often reborn in the past or in a game world) steadily builds an unbeatable position.
- **Neoliberalism**: a belief in markets, competition and personal responsibility over state protection; in Japan it is tied to Koizumi's reforms.
- **Theater politics**: the nickname for Koizumi's media-driven political style.
- **Bad debt**: a loan the borrower probably can't repay in full.
- **Elementary school**: Runa is in fifth grade when the volume opens and in sixth grade from April 2002, so she is about ten or eleven.

**Precision pointers**
- *"The volume in which she loses"* → she loses control of her Iraq war plan, two of her chief lieutenants come under attack, and a bill to end noble immunity is announced. Her fortune (a group worth about ¥20 trillion) is untouched. (Vol. 4 map, Part III, "Where the series stands")
- *"One lesson, delivered three times"* → the map's table counts six attempts to stop her (Ch. 2 twice, Ch. 5, Ch. 6, Ch. 9, Ch. 11). The three described here are the ones about accountability. (Vol. 4 map, Part II §1 and §3)

## Chapter 1: The banker who never saw her face

The volume doesn't open with Runa. It opens with a stranger: a man who used to check loan applications at Far Eastern Bank and now manages the money at a small IT company in Tokyo. Writing in early 2002, he wants to set down how the great bank rescues of the late 1990s really worked, seen from the back office. What strikes me, and I think the author means it to, is that in the whole memoir he never once mentions a little girl. To him the miracle belonged to two men, Ichijou and Tachibana, and to "the Moonlight Fund's easy access to quick profits."

He starts with the books he saw. Far Eastern Bank was the Keika Group's house bank, the bank a business family uses as its own wallet, and it had been dragged down by the family's property and hotel companies. Once it had been cleaned up and given a management team of former officials from the Ministry of Finance (the MoF, the powerful ministry that then supervised Japan's banks), it became, in his words, "the perfect vessel" for rescuing other banks. He even suspects "the whole thing was rigged by the Ministry of Finance."

The rescues all ran on the same four conditions, which he calls the Keika Rules. Every executive of the failed bank goes. Anyone involved in wrongdoing is punished. All the bad loans are sent to the Resolution and Collection Corporation, the RCC, a state company that buys up bad loans and collects what it can. That includes the loans hidden by *tobashi*, the trick of passing losses from company to company like a hot potato so that no inspector catches them. And the Bank of Japan, the BoJ, Japan's central bank, provides emergency loans to keep everything steady.

The next part is, I think, the clearest explanation in the whole series of how a financial panic actually kills. Banks and brokerages live on what's called the call market, where they lend each other spare cash for a day or two, rather like neighbours borrowing a cup of sugar in the evening and returning it in the morning. As long as everyone trusts everyone, nobody thinks twice. But, as the banker puts it, "If you find yourself unable to get a loan, it means the other institutions have determined that you won't be able to repay any money they decide to lend you." Two brokerages and one bank in Hokkaido had all been refused their sugar. "The fuses of their destruction had been lit," he says, and short sellers, investors who profit when a share price falls, were already circling. Rescue only two of the three, and every short seller would pile onto the third. They had to be saved together.

Nobody at the ministry had seen this, because banks and securities firms were supervised by separate departments that never compared notes. It was like two doctors treating the same patient's heart and lungs without ever speaking to each other. One of the brokerages, Ichiyama, was also too dirty to rescue openly: it had been hiding losses and paying off *sōkaiya*, racketeers who extort companies by threatening to disrupt their shareholder meetings. So it was quietly folded into the cleaner brokerage first and rescued "under the pretense of only saving Sankai Securities."

Then comes the clever part, and it helps to remember the pudding. In Volume 1 Ichijou taught a very young Runa what a bad debt is by buying her a pudding for ten thousand yen that nobody would buy back for more than a hundred. Bad debts are puddings bought at bubble prices. But there is also the opposite kind of pudding: something a bank bought long ago and still records at the old, cheap price, which would now fetch far more. The banker explains it simply: "Five billion yen in assets could rise in price and become worth 10 billion at market price." Those hidden profits are called unrealised gains.

The rescue machine used both kinds. First, sell the failed bank's bad loans to the RCC at their real, low price, which leaves a big loss. Then merge, because a merger is the moment when everything on the other side gets counted at today's prices. The hidden gains appear, and they cover the loss. Then do the whole thing again with the next failed bank, and again with the one after that, each time wrapping the last result inside a new, larger outer shell. The staff, he says, "laughed and called it a matryoshka doll."

And the famous eight trillion yen of central-bank loans, the figure that once seemed impossibly large? "We generally didn't use the 8-trillion-yen loan from the Bank of Japan," he says. It was "something to display," proof to the markets that the money was there. He calls it the anaesthetic, and I think that's exactly right. An anaesthetic doesn't heal anybody, but it lets the surgeon cut. They had to cut away the bad debts "while the anesthesia—the Bank of Japan's massive special loan—was still taking effect," and the Hokkaido police arrested the old bank's executives in the same window. What finally paid everything back was profit: Keika Securities' share gains, a Moonlight Fund that "would make us a hundred billion yen in a year like it was nothing," and "the finishing blow," Russian government bonds bought cheaply after Russia's 1998 collapse.

There was a human side to the rescue too, and the banker is honest about both halves of it. Buying up the failing Hokkaido bank's shares kept local companies' holdings in it from turning into waste paper: "That probably saved the entire Hokkaido economy." But the merged bank was a house of rival tribes, from former ministry men to former city bankers, and the ex-Ichiyama staff were "a band of demons, waiting vigilantly to strike." Ichijou held it together by bringing in outsiders and staging a race for his job, and the current favourite is a woman called Angela Sullivan. With so many branches duplicated, surplus staff were sent out to client companies, and "they knew they'd probably received one-way tickets." He wasn't on that list himself, but he didn't fancy his chances in the race to the top, so when a younger friend from his university days invited him to a new IT company, he went.

He signs off grateful, "whatever their intentions were," with a warning attached. Under Koizumi's tougher rules on bad debt, he writes, the whole Hokkaido structure "would crumble if the same thing happened today."

I love this chapter as an opening move. It sets down Runa's greatest achievement as settled history, explains the trick the first volume skipped, and quietly tells you that the trick can't be done again. Everything that follows in Volume 4 is about the world changing the rules she won under. And the girl who made it all happen doesn't appear in a single line of the memoir of a man who lived through it.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Moonlight Fund**: Runa's investment fund, based in Silicon Valley, the source of the Keika Group's money.
- **House bank (institutional bank)**: a bank that mainly serves one business group and acts as its wallet.
- **MoF**: the Ministry of Finance, which supervised Japan's banks until 1998.
- **Keika Rules**: Runa's four rescue conditions (executives out, wrongdoers punished, bad loans to the RCC, central-bank loans for stability).
- **RCC**: the Resolution and Collection Corporation, a state company that buys bad loans and collects on them.
- **Tobashi**: hiding losses by passing bad assets from company to company.
- **BoJ**: the Bank of Japan, Japan's central bank.
- **Call market**: where banks and brokerages lend each other money for very short periods, usually overnight.
- **Short sellers**: investors who make money when a share price falls.
- **Sōkaiya**: racketeers who extort companies by threatening trouble at shareholder meetings.
- **Unrealised gains**: the profit hidden in an asset still recorded at its old purchase price.
- **Merger**: two companies becoming one; here, the moment when assets are counted at today's prices.
- **Matryoshka doll**: a Russian nesting doll, the staff's joke name for the repeated merger trick.
- **One-way ticket**: being sent from the bank to work at a client company, with no expectation of coming back.

**Precision pointers**
- *"The hidden gains appear and cover the loss"* → strictly, the legal survivor changed each time ("reverse mergers"), so each acquired bank's losses were absorbed by the unrealised gains of the previous shell. It worked only because asset prices were still above book value. (Vol. 4 map, Part II §4a; Ch. 1)
- *"Something to display"* → the ¥8 trillion was liquidity insurance, mostly undrawn; government deposits replaced fleeing deposits. This answers the Vol. 1 map's doubt about the figure. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 1, Revelations)
- *"Two brokerages and one bank"* → Sankai Securities, Ichiyama Securities and Hokkaido Kaitaku Bank. In reality Sanyo Securities, Yamaichi Securities and Hokkaido Takushoku Bank, which were not rescued this way; Yamaichi closed. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 1, Historical references)
- *"Sugar between neighbours"* → the call market. In reality Sanyo Securities' default there in November 1997 began the chain of failures. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 1)
- *Left out:* Maruima Department Stores and Amazaki Construction (the department store and builder kept alive in Hokkaido), and the body text's slip of "9.4 trillion" for Maruima's debts, which the glossary gives as ¥94 billion. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 1)

## Chapter 2, part one: A cage made of money

Runa's first scene in the volume is at a racecourse. It's December 2001, at Sha Tin in Hong Kong, and she has talked her father and brother into letting her fly out for a single race, the Hong Kong Vase, to watch one horse run for the last time. He is the kind of horse she can't help loving: forever second or third, never the winner of a top-grade race, but once, gloriously, the conqueror of a world champion in Dubai. In the seats beside her are an old European aristocrat, Karl Lothringe, and his grandson Franz, and Runa half-recognises the boy. He was written as a love interest for the game and then cut.

"Don't you think the journey means more than the destination?" she asks him. "Results are all that matter," Franz replies, and she privately admits he's right. That is exactly why, "as a villainess doomed to defeat, I couldn't help but sympathize with the horse." Then, on the final straight, the horse everyone has written off breaks through to the front and wins. Runa cries, and when Franz asks why, she says, "I'm not crying about the race. I'm crying about that horse's story." Back home she becomes the owner of his foals, and she adds, with a wink at the reader who knows racing, "I never imagined what big things that horse would accomplish…" Offered the most fashionable stallion in Japan as a sire, she picks a chestnut instead, because "He's just so lovely to look at."

I find this opening lovely, and it also tells you how to read the chapter. All the way through, Runa chooses the story over the result.

The trip wasn't only about a horse. With Japan's Foreign Ministry in disarray, the government has been using the Keikain family as a back channel, so Tachibana and Angela spend the race weekend trading information in the Hong Kong salons. Runa suspects, rightly, that she hadn't met the old man at the track "by coincidence." What she doesn't know yet is the rest of it.

On the flight home the trouble starts. A Japanese activist fund, backed by an American one, launches a takeover bid for Furukawa Telecoms, the electronics company Runa has had her eye on. A takeover bid is a public offer to buy a company's shares at a premium. This one aims at 33.4 percent, just over a third, which is a blocking stake: in a Japanese company a third of the votes can veto a merger or a change to the company's rules, much as a third of the members of a club can block anything that needs a two-thirds majority. Behind the funds stands the real opponent, Teikoku Telephone, one of the country's ten largest conglomerates, which is furious that banks led by Keika forced its man out as Furukawa's president.

In the meeting room Runa asks what would happen if she fought on every front at once. Ichijou doesn't hesitate: "We'll be defeated."

That sounds absurd for the richest girl in Japan, so it's worth understanding why. Runa isn't short of wealth. She is short of cash, today, in yen. Her Moonlight Fund earns dollars abroad, uses them to buy crude oil, ships the oil to Japan and sells it for yen, and every step takes weeks. Meanwhile she has just paid two trillion yen up front for a new Shinkansen line through Shinjuku, to get a discount. Picture someone who owns several houses but has an empty wallet on the day a big bill is due. On top of that, a queue of struggling regional banks is lining up to be rescued, and they can't be put off, because, as Tachibana says, "We've only been able to do as we please because it's always ended up benefiting the people of the world." Each of those rescues needs people as well as money, and Runa sums up the problem: "We had the money, just not the manpower for it."

So she stands down, and protects a smaller electronics maker, Shiyo Electric, instead. When her brother asks what she actually wants with Shiyo, she puts her phone on the table, a little PHS handset, and explains: small screens and batteries are the future of the mobile phone. If Japan moves now it can set the world's standard instead of building phones that only work at home. "This is how we bring the world under our control."

Then the narration walks next door, and the chapter turns.

In the executive office, Ichijou, chewing on an unlit cigarette, and her brother Nakamaro are sighing together, and we learn that the whole takeover battle has been a play. When Teikoku Telephone wanted revenge, it hadn't gone after Furukawa at all. It had got every systems company, Japanese and foreign, to cancel their work on Keika Holdings' new core banking system, the central computer ledger that every bank runs on. Ichijou and Tachibana, with Nakamaro's permission, quietly made peace. Angela leaned on Washington. Teikoku's one condition was "Get that young lady under control." So the four of them, Tachibana, Ichijou, Nakamaro and Angela, staged the takeover bid together with Teikoku and the funds, and fed Runa the bank rescues and the Shinkansen payment as the reasons she couldn't fight. "It was all a cover story whipped up to prevent the young lady from opposing them."

Why would the people closest to her do this? Angela has said it bluntly: "The young lady has too much potential. She might even become a dictator at this rate." If she stays this close to the Republican president, the Democrats will punish her when they return to power. Russia fears she could split its government and end up at the head of "a massive Russian-Japanese autocratic empire," and a girl with that kind of power "could very well end up having her blood spilled." So the four of them agreed on one aim: "May Runa be guaranteed a good, happy life."

And the man at the racecourse? That was arranged too. "We had Runa meet with the European nobles in Hong Kong, as was forcibly demanded of us," says Nakamaro, and he means it as a marriage viewing. "She's going to have to make a sacrifice of some kind if she's to live a happy life."

"So she gets to be a bird in a cage?" one of the two men asks. The other answers with the line that sums up this whole half of the chapter: "What she needs to learn is that the cage protects the bird from the outside world." He reminds his companion of September 11th, when a fifth-grader stood in the spotlight and "carried the world on her shoulders," and he says that if those falling towers were counted as her failure, "history will look back and condemn us adults, wondering what we were doing during all this."

I keep coming back to how tender and how mistaken this is at the same time. These are good people, acting out of love, and their cage is built from the one material Runa can always get more of, which is money. They've just told her she can't afford to fight. As we're about to see, she simply goes and finds the money.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Hong Kong Vase**: an international top-grade (G1) horse race at Sha Tin, Hong Kong.
- **Takeover bid**: a public offer to buy a company's shares, usually above the market price, in order to gain control.
- **Blocking (veto) stake**: just over a third of the shares, enough to block mergers and changes to a company's charter.
- **Activist fund**: an investment fund that buys shares to force changes on a company's management.
- **Conglomerate**: a large group of companies under one parent.
- **Moonlight Fund**: Runa's Silicon Valley investment fund.
- **Shinkansen**: Japan's high-speed "bullet train" network; Keika Railway is building private lines.
- **Liquidity**: having cash available now, as opposed to wealth tied up in assets.
- **Regional bank**: a bank serving one prefecture or region.
- **PHS**: the Personal Handy-phone System, a cheap Japanese mobile phone network of the time.
- **Galapagos syndrome**: Japanese phones that evolved to suit only the home market, like animals adapted to a single island.
- **Core banking system**: the central computer system that records every account and transaction in a bank.

**Precision pointers**

## Chapter 2, part two: Four hundred billion dollars and a forged e-mail

The man who breaks the cage is Okazaki Yuuichi, the adventurer from Akamatsu, Runa's trading company. He finds her brooding over the news in the Moonlight Fund's office, hands her a can of coffee that turns out to be black, and laughs at the face she makes. Then he tells her what he's worked out. She doesn't save companies, he says, she saves whole industries: "You're a turnaround manager when it comes down to it, my lady. You look at industries instead of just companies. That must mean you know the computer industry is going to see a decline." He's right, and Runa can't answer him. She knows Japan's consumer electronics makers will crumble within a dozen years, and she believes she could still save them if only she had the yen.

"Why not just go for it?" he says, like a boy proposing a prank. "Losing only a few trillion yen isn't the end of the world." And if it fails, she will at least leave something behind, "just like Suzuki Shoten," the great trading house whose collapse in 1927 set off a famous financial panic and changed the course of Japanese history.

At that moment the television announces that the woman running a big American computer company has been forced out after losing a proxy fight, a battle between groups of shareholders for votes. Runa makes up her mind. "Let's start the game," she says, and she adds a line that is very her: "If we must bow our heads to anyone, let's do it together, okay?" Her first order is to scout that ousted chief executive.

Now we need a little arithmetic, because the size of this bet is the point.

Okazaki works from London, so that Angela and Ichijou won't notice. Runa has told him the United States will soon attack Iraq, and he uses that to borrow about twenty billion dollars from Gulf oil states, at nine percent interest, in names with no link to Keika at all. Runa's description of how carefully he kept her out of it is chilling: it was "the scary sort of money where he could jump off a building and the fallout would never reach me." The money passes through shell companies (companies that exist only on paper) on the Isle of Man, in the Caymans and in the British Virgin Islands, and into computer-run funds.

Then he applies leverage, twenty times over. Leverage means using borrowed money to multiply the size of a bet. It's like buying a house with a small deposit: if the house rises in value you make a fortune on your little deposit, but if it falls even slightly, your deposit is gone. At twenty times, a five percent move in the wrong direction wipes out everything. Twenty billion becomes four hundred billion dollars of bets, and you only make a bet like that if you're certain which way the market will go.

Runa makes certain. Through her maid Erika, who has no idea what she's carrying, a code phrase arrives: "Lady Runa doesn't grasp the taste of black coffee either." It means everything is ready. Runa then shows Angela a printout: a whistleblower's e-mail claiming fraud at WCI, America's second-largest long-distance phone company, which uses "the same accounting firm as General Energy Online," the energy giant that collapsed in Volume 3. The e-mail is a fake. Okazaki sent it from a London internet café. Runa even suggests they pretend they never saw it, to protect the president, knowing full well that Angela and Eva will report it straight to Washington. Her own summary of the plan is cold and clear: "If something was set to blow up, I was going to make sure it happened on my schedule."

It blows up on schedule. WCI's chief executive resigns, and three days later the company files for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection. Okazaki has been short selling, borrowing shares, selling them, and buying them back after the price falls, so that he profits from the collapse, not just on WCI but across the technology stocks of the NASDAQ, America's tech-heavy stock market. He keeps it "just barely legal" by placing his trades only after the first news reports. A fifteen percent return on four hundred billion dollars is sixty billion. After interest, fees and repaying the loan, about thirty-five billion is left, roughly four trillion yen.

The ripples do the rest. With New York in free fall, the American fund behind the Furukawa bid has to sell up and go home. Runa buys Portercon, America's number-two maker of personal computers, for fourteen and a half billion dollars, partly to steady the market and put the president in her debt. She hires the ousted American chief executive, Karin Viola, over coffee at her own café. And on the afternoon news Furukawa Telecoms and Shiyo Electric announce that they're joining together, with Portercon alongside them: "a massive new computer company that spans both the United States and Japan." "My main goal is cell phones," Runa tells Karin.

Okazaki is told to keep a hundred billion yen as his reward, and he says he'll put it aside "for next time." Eva, one of her maids and a transfer from the CIA, answers in a voice that makes the room go cold: "I won't let there be a next time, Mister Okazaki." Angela, we learn, has already punched him.

I want to be fair to Runa here, but I also want to be honest. In Volume 3 she knew General Energy Online would collapse and deliberately did not bet against it; she didn't want to end up in the same place as the people profiting from the crash. Here she does more than predict a collapse. She chooses its date with a forged document and bets against the whole market into it. The trading may have been timed to stay within the law, but faking a tip to move a market you're betting on is market manipulation by any ordinary standard, and the book clearly knows it. The people who love her react as if to a crime. It's the moment Runa becomes what Volume 3 was afraid she might become, and it's also the moment she's rewarded most richly. That's exactly why the scolding, when it finally comes, will have to come from outside her own house.

- **Suzuki Shoten**: a huge trading house whose failure in 1927 triggered the Shōwa financial crisis.
- **Shell (paper) company**: a company that exists only on paper, used to move or hide money.
- **Tax haven**: a place with low taxes and secrecy rules, such as the Isle of Man or the Caymans.
- **Leverage**: borrowing to multiply the size of an investment, which multiplies both gains and losses.
- **Whistleblower**: an insider who reports wrongdoing.
- **Chapter 11**: the American bankruptcy procedure under which a company reorganises under court protection.

- **NASDAQ**: the American stock market where most technology companies are listed.
- **CEO**: chief executive officer, the person who runs a company.

- **CIA**: the Central Intelligence Agency, America's foreign intelligence service; Angela and Eva both came from it.

**Precision pointers**
- *"Twenty billion becomes four hundred billion"* → about $20 billion at 9 percent, levered twenty times; a 15 percent return gave $60 billion gross and $35 billion (about ¥4 trillion) net. (Vol. 4 map, Part II §4c)
- *"Just barely legal"* → the text's own phrase. Whether the scheme as a whole is lawful is my assessment, not the book's verdict. (Vol. 4 map, Part II §4c)
- *"WCI"* → my identification: WorldCom. In reality its fraud surfaced in June 2002 and it went bankrupt in July 2002; the novel moves the collapse to the end of 2001 and makes Runa the cause. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 2; Part III ledger)
- *"Portercon" and "Karin Viola"* → my identifications: Compaq and Carly Fiorina. In reality she won the vote in March 2002 and was forced out only in 2005. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 2)
- *"The book clearly knows it"* → in Volume 3 she refused to short General Energy Online; Volume 5 has her refuse to "pull the trigger" on subprime. This is the middle of that sequence. (Vol. 4 map, Part II §4c and §4g)

## Chapter 2, part three: The people who scolded her

After the money comes the reckoning, and it arrives softly, one person at a time.

Ichijou goes first. He greets her with a smile that she knows is covering fury, and then, when she tells him she still means to swallow Furukawa Telecoms whole, the two of them simply look at each other until he gives in with a sigh. "I owe you more than I can ever repay, my lady," he says. "That's exactly why I don't want you crossing any more dangerous bridges." She can't stop, she tells him, and then she says something under her breath that she doesn't explain to anyone: "If I don't, everyone will turn in their graves…" She has a whole past life behind her, and I've never been able to read that line without wondering whose graves she means.

Then she goes to the family's main house and bows in the study to Kiyomaro, her foster father and the head of the house. "I'm very sorry for going so much further than a child is supposed to." Of all the adults around her, he is, as she puts it, "the only one who could formally scold me." He doesn't shout. He asks her a question: "you've now added a hundred thousand employees and easily over a million connected individuals. Did you understand that before you took action?" She says yes, and that she believes the path leads to happiness for all of them. He doesn't argue. Instead he takes a book from the shelf, a politician's argument for a smaller, more modest Japan, and gives her its motto: "'Know when to quit.' I love that phrase. In a way, it's how I managed to survive."

Runa understands what he means. Bubbles, she reflects, are a game of chicken: the winner is whoever gets out before everything crashes, and the crowd that follows along ends up with the short straw. Then his eyes go to a photograph on his desk, and he says, almost too quietly to hear, "…Don't become like my big brother. Now get out of here." His big brother was Runa's father, whose secret dealings with the East brought his companies down and ended in scandal and an apparent suicide. She leaves with the book and an aching heart. She's touched that he worries for her, and she's afraid he'll be the one to pay for what she's done.

Her brother Nakamaro is waiting outside the study, and for once his gentle face is stern. He reels off what she's built: the megabank, the department stores, the trading company, the hotels, the railway with its own bullet trains, and now a giant computer company. Then he asks the question that nobody in the series has answered yet: "Will you really be able to control it from the inside, Runa?" She gives him a brave answer about a talented American she's hired. Privately she knows the truth, and it isn't flattering. She has never once thought about how her companies are run day to day: "I was prepared to sell everything off if it meant making a profit." Her goal is to revive Japan, and the companies are only a means to that.

Nakamaro sees her face crumple and puts a hand on her head. "You're still a child, Runa. It's only natural for people to protect you at this age. You don't need to rush to become an adult before it's time." And she cries, because she suddenly sees how badly she has frightened these good people. He asks her to smile for him at his wedding in June, and she says yes. As she leaves, the thought she keeps to herself is heartbreaking in its scale: "I've deserted such good people…this country…this era."

Hold on to that word "rush," because it will come back twice more in this book, from two very different people, and the third time she won't have an answer.

The last scolding is the funniest and maybe the truest. When Runa gets home, the head maid, Saitou Keiko, is waiting with a smile so beautiful and so terrifying that Runa compares it to a demon's. Runa protests that her father and brother have already scolded her. Keiko replies with a line I'd like to frame: "You think a woman will be persuaded with reason? You're a child who's made life difficult for her parent. That alone is a perfectly good reason to scold you!" The lecture goes on past midnight. The next morning, Tachibana tactfully says nothing about how tired she looks.

What I love about this sequence is that it treats a scolding as a form of love, and the book never lets you forget that. Not one of these people threatens her. Her uncle gives her a book, her brother puts a hand on her head, her maid keeps her up past midnight, and her banker tells her he owes her everything. She hears them, she cries, and then she carries straight on. That's what makes the rest of the volume necessary. When the people who love you can't stop you, eventually someone who doesn't need your love will have to.

- **Nakamaro**: Kiyomaro's son, Runa's foster brother and the family's heir.
- **Megabank**: one of Japan's giant merged banks; Keika Holdings was the first.
- **"Know when to quit"**: a saying from Laozi, the ancient Chinese philosopher ("he who knows sufficiency shall prosper").
- **The bubble**: Japan's huge rise in land and share prices in the late 1980s, which collapsed in the early 1990s.
- **Head maid**: Saitou Keiko, who runs Runa's household staff and has looked after her since early childhood.

## Chapter 3: The room that decided to keep using her

Chapter 3 moves to Washington in January 2002, to an underground briefing room built during the Cold War. The government is meeting there because, in this version of history, the Pentagon and the White House were both hit on September 11th and are still being repaired. The president has a report from Tokyo in front of him and asks the obvious question: "That young lady's underlings must be behind this. Why isn't anyone stopping her?" The vice president sighs: "These are the selfish whims of a little girl."

What follows is the American government reading Chapter 2 from the other side, and it's one of the most quietly damning chapters in the series. Everyone in that room knows exactly what she did. The CIA has worked it out: the Keika Group "vengefully forced us to back off by secretly reporting on WCI's accounting fraud, crashed the New York stock market, and got the funds in their way to retreat." And yet, one by one, every person at the table finds a reason to leave her alone.

The head of the CIA has his reason. Congress is asking how America's spy agencies and ECHELON, the giant American system for intercepting the world's communications, missed the September attacks, when the warning ended up with a trading company in Japan. He is a leftover appointment from the previous government, and her tips are what make his agency look competent. If he turned on her, the CIA itself might be the next thing the administration turned on.

The president has his reasons too. Congress is also investigating his links to General Energy Online, the energy giant that collapsed in Volume 3, and WCI was another of his donors. Prosecuting WCI lets the White House say it threw out the rotten apple itself. The Secretary of State is almost grateful: "The information she provided us was true, and it let us avoid a fatal blow." Even the forged e-mail is admired. It was sent through a USB stick plugged into a computer in a London internet café, with a virus that mailed it while some unsuspecting customer browsed the web, and the agencies, which "relied too heavily on ECHELON," never found the human behind it.

The president does ask the question a reader would ask. If she could expose WCI's fraud, why didn't she expose General Energy Online's in time, or make money from the attacks she warned about? It doesn't make sense to him. The CIA director's answer satisfies the room: "The girl is generally our ally, and she only counterattacked when we were hostile toward her."

Then the director lays a map on the table, and the mood changes. Runa, he says, has taken ten billion dollars of her winnings and poured it into Bahrain, the small island state in the Persian Gulf linked by causeway to Saudi Arabia, "calling it infrastructure groundwork." Her trading company is also building a mining railway across Saudi Arabia from the port of Dammam to Hafar al-Batin, with a branch line to King Khalid Military City, an American base a few dozen kilometres from the Iraqi border. The budget even includes mercenaries as site guards. Everyone at the table reads the map the same way, and the narration puts it in her voice, as if she'd written them a note: "You're going to invade Iraq, right? Then allow me to help you." It is "the earliest declaration of any party that they would participate in the war," and it frightens the president and vice president more than anything she's done with money, because it looks even more like she can see the future.

The chapter keeps going darker. In Afghanistan, Japanese and Russian soldiers are dying in place of Americans, and "technically retired" Indian soldiers have gone in seeking revenge for the attack on India's parliament and "carried out multiple massacres." The sentence that follows is short and terrible: "The girl's mercenary company was responsible for assuring their status as a PMC." A PMC is a private military company, a business that supplies armed men under contract. In other words, her company gave those men the legal cover to be there. Nothing suggests anyone has told her.

The president also remembers that he's sitting where he is "thanks to a few thousand votes in Florida," a gift he attributes to her company's support. She is now building PACs, political action committees, the American organisations that collect money for candidates, in the swing states that decide elections, by buying stakes in struggling local companies so that they can sponsor them.

Then comes the sentence that matters most for the rest of the series. The White House is preparing to invade Iraq over "weapons of mass destruction," yet "they already knew that Iraq had no such weapons." The choice in front of them is to press on with a cover story, and Runa's discovery of missing Soviet nuclear warheads in Volume 3 has given them the raw material for one: Iraq tried to get nuclear weapons, failed, and paid for the September attacks instead. The president closes the meeting with a line that turns my stomach: "If we'll be paying for the lost money with blood, then let's make the best of it." A few days later he tells the world about an "axis of evil."

What I take from this chapter is how ordinary every motive in that room is: a job to keep, a scandal to manage, an election to win, a war to prepare. Nobody there is a monster. They've simply all concluded that a useful child is too convenient to stop. That's the situation Koizumi will break in Chapter 9, and it's why his refusal will read as integrity rather than spite. The narration also draws a parallel I can't unsee. The CIA building a story "to control the definitive outcome" is "similar… to the girl's recent outrageous business endeavors." A forged whistleblower's e-mail and a manufactured reason for war turn out to be the same kind of thing, just at different sizes.

- **CIA**: the Central Intelligence Agency, America's foreign intelligence service.
- **ECHELON**: an American-led system for intercepting communications worldwide.
- **WCI**: the American long-distance phone company Runa brought down in Chapter 2.
- **General Energy Online**: the energy company whose collapse Runa predicted in Volume 3.
- **USB stick**: a small plug-in memory device.
- **Bahrain**: a small island country in the Persian Gulf, home to a major American naval base.
- **King Khalid Military City**: a Saudi military base near Hafar al-Batin, used by American forces since the Gulf War.
- **PMC**: private military company, a business that provides armed personnel under contract.
- **PAC**: political action committee, an American group that raises and gives money to candidates.

- **WMD**: weapons of mass destruction (nuclear, chemical or biological).

## Chapter 4: The boys who learned how to lose

Chapter 4 is a short breather, and one of my favourites. It's set at Avanti, the café where the three boys like to meet, and for most of it Runa isn't there. Teia Eiichi, Izumikawa Yuujirou and Gotou Mitsuya have just cashed in their Furukawa Telecoms shares for about eight billion yen, which is a fortune for anyone, let alone three elementary school boys. And Eiichi, who "hated to lose," can only manage a dry laugh: "We came out with a win…but Runa's still sprinting ahead of us." Yuujirou lists what she owns now: "She has banks, logistics, railroads, and now electronics."

Eiichi's grandfather, the craftsman who founded the Teia car company, has already given him his own scolding: "Stop playin' around with money." And Eiichi takes it to heart. To bet what Runa bets, he says, he'd have to put the entire Teia Group on the line, and he isn't interested in doing that. But the narration adds the line that defines him for the rest of the book: "admitting defeat and giving up on Keikain Runa were two separate things."

So instead of chasing her, he watches her. From a single small newspaper item, that Keika Holdings has postponed its new core banking system (the central computer every bank runs on), he works out that her whole Furukawa campaign was a gamble, and that she hasn't given the system a thought since. That's an opening. The three of them sketch a business that runs backup computer systems for banks, staffed with American engineers who are cheap to hire now that the American tech bubble has burst. Mitsuya fills in the technical side: let the Americans build the foundations, the databases and the security, now that "the patent on public key encryption expired in 2000," and leave the banking logic to Japanese firms. Yuujirou adds the time zones: when Tokyo goes home for the night, a team on America's East Coast can take over.

It's Mitsuya who voices the worry: they're assuming Runa will go along with it. Eiichi is sure she will, and then he says something that makes all three of them fall silent: "Win or lose, we'll never catch up to Runa. She'll find some other boy to cultivate."

I find that sentence quietly heartbreaking. What these boys fear isn't losing to her. It's being replaced beside her.

Then Runa walks in. "What were you boys talking about?" "How to use you to make money." Her face, which had been in what the narration calls "dessert mode," changes, yen signs appear in her eyes, and within minutes she's offering half the money if they can have servers running by spring. She cheerfully admits she "sort of took on a major gamble" and hasn't looked at the system, and then she suggests they just buy an IT company on the East Coast instead of building one. That's the difference between them, and Eiichi sees it in a flash: he thinks of founding a company, she thinks of acquiring one. She "saw it as buying time with money." He asks her how much she bet on Furukawa. "Ummm, somewhere in the trillions?"

The chapter ends with a quick look ahead. Their company, TIG Backup Systems, buys an East Coast firm with her money, and its first big customer isn't Keika at all. It's Honami Bank, after "a massive system failure in the spring." In its first year the business makes ten and a half billion yen of profit on forty-two and a half billion of sales.

The chapter's title, "To Admit Defeat Is to Grow as a Person," is aimed at Runa over the boys' heads, I think. In the same weeks that a row of adults scold her, and her uncle tells her to know when to quit, and she carries straight on, Eiichi admits a defeat on his own and grows from it. He stops trying to match her and starts positioning himself where she'll need him. And I love that the boring, unglamorous fix, keeping the computers running while the big banks feud over theirs, turns out to be worth ten billion yen a year. Runa's neglected plumbing becomes everyone else's opportunity.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Core banking system**: the central computer system that records every account and transaction in a bank.
- **Backup system**: a second copy of a computer system that can take over if the main one fails.
- **Tech (IT) bubble**: the boom in internet and technology shares that collapsed in 2000–01.
- **Public-key encryption**: the method that secures online banking and payments; a key patent on it (RSA) expired in 2000.
- **East Coast**: the eastern seaboard of the United States, about half a day behind Tokyo.
- **Teia Group**: Eiichi's family business group, built around Teia Motor.
- **Honami Bank**: a big bank formed by merging three banks.

**Precision pointers**
- *"About eight billion yen"* → the text says ¥8 billion here, but ¥6 billion in Chapter 2. It's a continuity slip. (Vol. 4 map, Part II §10)
- *"A massive system failure in the spring"* → Honami = Mizuho (my identification). In reality, on 1 April 2002, the first day the three merged banks operated together, Mizuho's systems broke down nationwide. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 4)
- *"The patent on public key encryption expired in 2000"* → real: the RSA patent expired in September 2000. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 4)
- *Left out:* the Machishita Fund's attempt to recruit Mitsuya alone, and the novel Mitsuya is reading, *Hyouka*. The map suggests (my reading) that the book was chosen because its mystery turns on a student scapegoat later remembered as a hero. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 4)

## Chapter 5, part one: The queen with her head on the chopping block

Chapter 5 is the longest chapter in the book, and it's built to look like one of the series' usual school-life interludes: cake, board games, a photo exhibition, a wedding. Under the frosting, though, it holds a rehearsal of the game's ending, the two frankest things anyone ever says to Runa about herself, and a private conversation with the American president. I'll take it in three parts. This first part is about elections, cameras and the word "villainess."

It begins with a student-council election. Imperial Gakushuukan Academy is run by cliques: the Courtiers (noble children, with rank but not much money), the Lords (zaibatsu children), the Stewards (politicians' children) and the Heralds (bureaucrats' children). Eiichi is running for president of the elementary division, with Yuujirou as one of his team. But the son of a major opposition politician wants to fight Yuujirou for his place in a "primary," on a platform of abolishing school cliques and reforming the council. Outside the gates the press has its cameras ready, eager to cast the four friends as "more of the same Ancien Régime."

Runa sees the shape of it at once, because she has seen it before, in the game. "We were in the era of TV choosing the prime minister," she thinks, and the game's heroine, Takanashi Mizuho, would one day enjoy the same kind of television-made "plot armor." Then comes one of the great self-portraits in the series: "As someone unchanged from the old times, I would be defeated by Takanashi Mizuho, the embodiment of the average citizen. I now understood that I was the queen with her head on the chopping block."

Her answer is to read the rulebook. "Let's ignore them," she says, to the boys' astonishment. The president is chosen only by the six class-committee members from the three classes, and the challenger isn't one of them, so he can't win. Then she adds a little theatre of her own. In the crowded cafeteria, "in a loud voice," she hands the leader of the Courtiers, Asagiri Kaoru, a cheque for a hundred million yen to refurbish the Courtiers' clubhouse. "Smacking someone with a stack of bills is most effective with a captive audience," she explains afterwards, and she has checked that no school rule forbids it. The money is "a premium payment," a warning wrapped as a gift: break our agreement, and you'll need to raise more than that. Among the watching girls, a quiet classmate named Kazuki Shiori lets slip what Runa calls "the brief glimmer of disgust" in her eyes. When Tachibana hears about it, he only smiles and says, "Just try not to imitate your grandfather in all the worst ways."

The result is the game's council, years ahead of schedule: Eiichi president, Runa vice president, Yuujirou secretary, Mitsuya treasurer.

A few days later the opposition politician's son stands on a mandarin-orange crate at the school gate and shouts "We must make a change!" to the waiting cameras. And Runa, walking past, suddenly remembers an illustrated scene from the game in which Mizuho gave a speech on that very spot: "(—Don't you find it strange?! We're all humans here! And yet…)" She walks on, gracefully, for the cameras, thinking: "you're not going to bring me down. Takanashi Mizuho is the only one who'll do that." The next day's weekly magazine runs the headline "Arrogant Duke's Daughter Ignores Inspired Speech by Opposition's Son."

I can't decide whether to laugh or wince. She has just watched a dry run of her own downfall, the reformer, the cameras, the speech about equality, and she has beaten it with the old weapons of procedure and money. It works because the electorate is six children. It is also exactly the kind of politics the game's reform movement exists to end, and Tachibana's joke names whose politics it is: her grandfather's.

The chapter then softens. Her photographer, Ishikawa, puts on an exhibition of every picture he has ever taken of her, from the first "What can I get for you, Your Little Majesty?" poster to the one that won every prize the year before: the burning towers on a television screen behind her, and Prime Minister Koizumi holding her after she collapsed. For once he is serious. "You're going to become a great lady, I guarantee it. So be happy—despair doesn't suit great ladies." Then he spoils it with a joke about nude photographs, she kicks him in the shin, and he settles for a promise to photograph her in a wedding dress one day. "You know I can't promise you that!" she snaps. She means it more than he knows.

After a little concert at a department store, a rough, middle-aged talent scout pushes past her guards, sure she could be "a diva of the Heisei era." "I'm sorry," Runa tells him sweetly. "I make more money than an idol." He had no idea who she was. When she tells Tachibana, his reply is no joke. The television networks want her on screen "twenty-four hours a day, 365 days a year," and real power in the media doesn't sit with the owners Keika can lobby. It lies with "the middle class," the working editors and producers whom no deal between executives can bind.

That sets Runa thinking, and what she arrives at is the most important idea in this half of the chapter. Japan, she reflects, lives by "the nail that sticks out gets hammered down" and by the unspoken pressure to read the room, and between the two, anything too foreign is removed. "Looking at it that way, the 'villainess' known as Keikain Runa was also a sacrifice, taken down by Japanese culture and its systems." For the first time she sees her fate not as a plot against her but as a social mechanism, something the country does to whoever doesn't fit.

Tachibana smiles and offers a way out. "You just have to get married, my lady." Downfalls and weddings both end the coverage. Then he says the gentlest thing in the chapter: "if you want to step down, you can tell me any time. Toudou-san, Ichijou, Angela, your father, your brother, and I all want you to be happy."

That night, alone in her bedroom, she sings the song from the concert to her reflection. It's a song from an anime she loves, and when she read the translation she found it was "a song about an eternal farewell." Her violinist had told her she always seemed to enjoy singing it, and now she understands why. She was "singing it to my future self."

I find that image almost unbearably sad: a fifth-grader, rich beyond counting, singing goodbye into a mirror to the woman she expects to lose.

- **Zaibatsu**: a family-controlled business conglomerate; in this world they were never broken up after the war.
- **Courtiers, Lords, Stewards, Heralds**: the academy's four cliques, of noble, zaibatsu, politicians' and bureaucrats' children.
- **Primary**: a preliminary election to choose a group's candidate.
- **Ancien Régime**: the old order of pre-revolutionary France; here, a jibe at privileged insiders.
- **Plot armor**: in fiction, the protection a story gives its hero so that they always win.
- **Heisei era**: the Japanese era name for 1989–2019.
- **Idol**: a young pop star manufactured by a talent agency.
- **"The nail that sticks out gets hammered down"**: a Japanese proverb about the pressure to conform.

## Chapter 5, part two: Two men, two questions

In the middle of Chapter 5, within a few pages of each other, two grown men ask Runa a question. One is a university professor and the other is the president of the United States. I've come to think the volume puts them side by side on purpose.

The professor comes first. After the Furukawa gamble, Kiyomaro and Nakamaro, on her maid Erika's suggestion, ask Professor Kanbe, a sociologist and television regular she met in Volume 3, to assess her. Runa sits in on his lecture on the sociology of genius. A genius, he says, is "the first person who created the opportunity for an advancement," and Japan produces few of them, because a disaster-prone country rewards people who prepare together, and "the nail that sticks up gets hammered down." What Japan values instead is the able, hard-working ordinary person who develops someone else's breakthrough.

Back in his smoky office, with Erika making coffee, he drops the assignment altogether: "personally, I don't want to pick out those traits." He gives her his finding anyway: "Keikain Runa-kun, you are a 'gifted' child." Not the mystical kind of genius, he explains, but something more like "a normal genius," a child with remarkable abilities who needs more than an ordinary school can give. His advice to her family is blunt: "The right way is to have you leave the country. This place is too small for your talents."

Then he asks her, "Do you know what your biggest problem is?" She guesses: "Is it that I'm gifted?" "No. It's that you're a child." He opens the window, blows his smoke outside, and spells it out: "You can avoid taking responsibility because you're still a child. Sure, you may be punished if you commit a crime, but that punishment will still be light because of your age. The adults around you will cover for it." And then the gentler sting: "You have infinite time and possibilities. I don't know why you're in such a rush, but what comes next is up to you. At this rate, you may become the kind of person who can achieve anything, only to leave nothing behind."

There's that word again, "rush." Her brother used it in Chapter 2.

Runa bristles. "I think that's so arrogant," she says, and Erika gasps. She doesn't think she's a genius, she says, and she certainly doesn't have infinite time. The professor presses her: "In other words, you're saying that right now is the only time that you can be a genius?" "Correct," she answers, without hesitating, and he breaks into a smile.

I think that one word, "correct," is the truest thing she says about herself in the whole book. Infinite time is the one thing she has never had. Her first life ended early, and this one has an appointment with ruin in 2008. That's why no one can talk her into slowing down. And notice that Kanbe has just said, in a quiet office, the thing the prime minister will say to her face in Chapter 9: the trouble isn't her ability, it's that nobody can hold her to account.

The second question comes in February 2002, at the Winter Olympics in Salt Lake City, held under heavy security after the attacks. Runa has flown in via Ground Zero, where she laid flowers for the cameras, and she spends the Games working the private charity events as, in her own words, "the daughter of a dukedom, princess of House Romanov, and owner of the major fund," adding, a little wryly, "All of these were things Americans loved." Meanwhile Okazaki is quietly closing a sale. The ten-billion-dollar logistics complex in Bahrain, with its warehouses, runways, housing, power plant and desalination plant, will be bought on completion for a quarter more than it cost by a paper company from Bermuda that will run it for the American military. The Saudi railway is not yet part of the deal.

Then the door of her waiting room opens and the president walks in with his bodyguards. "It looks like I got the wrong room," he says, which of course he hasn't. He asks her straight out: "Are you interested in setting the world in motion?" "No. I want to change the world, not set it in motion," she replies. What does she want to change? "I want to change the history that will follow me, Mr. President. I'm sure you don't want to bring about a second Vietnam for the United States, right?" By a second Vietnam she means a long war that America can neither win nor leave.

He laughs, pats her on the head, and tells her, "I know that you tried everything you could," meaning her warnings about the September attacks. Then his face changes and he asks what he really came to ask: "When do you see us starting the war?"

"The spring of next year."

She tells him not to waste a victory on November's midterm elections, the mid-term vote for Congress that the president's party usually loses. He should accept a loss at the polls, build an army the size of the 1991 Gulf War force, gather a coalition and win Congress's approval. In return she will keep his losses "within acceptable limits," with two and a half billion dollars of political donations and ten billion more spent rescuing American companies. Her private reasoning is the part that should worry us: "If I lost in Japan, I would make it up in the United States. If the mass media and Prime Minister Koizumi's policies were going to get in my way, I would simply have the US put pressure on them."

She knows, even as she says it, that a war will hand Koizumi enormous power, and she shrugs it off with a line that sounds like a joke and isn't: "The world simply won't allow a child to set it in motion." The chapter's last image of the trip is Runa on American television, a patriotic little ambassador, "singing the tune of the next war over and over and over again."

Put the two scenes side by side and you can see what the book is doing. One adult asks what her problem is, and the answer is that she's a child who can't be held to account. The other adult, the most powerful man on earth, asks that same child when to start a war, and takes notes. Nobody in Washington thinks to ask whether she should be in the room at all. That's the question the next chapters will ask.

There's a small coda in Texas, at Portercon's headquarters, where Karin Viola is grilling her new managers. Her rule of management is the opposite of Runa's whole method: "Be sure you know what you don't know. Then find someone who does know and ask them."

## Chapter 5, part three: Half a bouquet

The rest of Chapter 5 is made of small, ordinary things, and I think it's the most tender stretch of the volume. After the election, the cameras and the president, the book lets Runa be a girl for a while, and lets her notice how rarely she gets to be one.

There's a party with board games, and Hotaru, the quiet friend who can't be found at hide-and-seek, wins both of them. In the second, a game of pre-1914 Europe in which nobody's promises are binding, she agrees with Runa to keep the Black Sea neutral and then sails her fleet straight into it on the first move. Everyone agrees afterwards that Hotaru "was the last person we should ever become enemies with."

There's a girls' tea party in the glass garden at the top of Runa's tower, with white Darjeeling and mandarin cake made from the endless crates of oranges her friend Asuka keeps bringing. Kazuki Shiori, the girl who looked at Runa with disgust over the cheque, has come too, and when Runa asks how she likes the garden, Shiori answers in a voice that, Runa notices, "wasn't directed at me, but at someone else." Runa looks around the table at the chatter and the flowers and catches herself: "Thinking about it now, this was something right out of a normal life. I realized just how much I'd lost sight of myself."

In April they all move up into sixth grade, the top year of elementary school, and Runa finds herself president of the girls' kendo club on top of being vice president of the student council. She also starts preparing for two national exams that would let her skip straight to university, at a college that is changing its rules so that she can come. It was Professor Kanbe's idea, and his reason is kind: "when you become a normal person, you'll need time to think." Her own reason she keeps to herself, and it's far sadder. If her life goes the way the game says it will, she won't be going to university at all. The certificates are for a life after ruin.

Mitsuya, being Mitsuya, assumes she'll study abroad, and her answer surprises him. "I truly do love this country," she says. She has been treated unfairly in it, in two lives, and loves it anyway. When he asks what exactly she loves, she says, without a pause, "The food, miso soup, the four seasons, and light novels."

Then there is Keiko, the head maid, explaining what a "fine young lady" is with a line borrowed from Coco Chanel: "One's shortcomings may be forgiven, but never her negligence." Keiko's version is practical. Live simply, buy the machines and hire the help that free your time for the next task, and never be careless. On every maid's birthday she gives a bottle of expensive perfume with a handwritten card and Chanel's words, "A woman who doesn't wear perfume has no future." Runa decides that she'll truly be a young lady on the day she receives a bottle from Keiko. She's waiting for that day, and it hasn't come yet.

And then, in June, the wedding. Her brother Nakamaro marries Asagiri Sakurako, Kaoru's elder sister, in a small church in Karuizawa, the mountain resort where wealthy Tokyo families keep their summer houses. Even here business follows Runa in: the bride's grandfather, President Iwazaki of Imperial Iwazaki Bank, takes her aside to say that another drug company wants to merge with the family's pharmaceutical business. Runa remembers that in the original game, Keika's pharmaceutical company lost its strength through just such "a poor merger," and that this time they could avoid it. The decision will be Nakamaro's first as heir, and on the station platform he whispers to her, "I have every intention of bowing my head to you when the time comes."

But the moment I remember from this chapter is the bouquet. Instead of throwing it, Sakurako calls Runa and Kaoru over and gives each of them half. "Don't tell the others. I know I'll be happy, so I want you two to have happy lives too." Earlier, in the waiting room, Kaoru had asked dreamily whether it wouldn't be lovely to be a June bride, and Runa had thought of her past life and of the future she believes is coming: "I'd spent my past life just desperately trying to survive, and I knew a future of ruin awaited me in this world. I looked at the bridal garments from afar, feeling certain that I would never wear them myself."

On the bullet train back to Tokyo she sits in first class with half a bouquet in her lap and says, mostly to herself, "Will I really be able to wear that kind of dress someday too…?" Tachibana, who has heard her, answers firmly and with a smile: "I'm certain you will, my lady." And the chapter ends on her silent question, as the train races on toward Tokyo:

"But who will be the one at my side on that day?"

I love that the book doesn't answer it. A few pages earlier, a photographer promised to shoot her in a wedding dress, and a butler suggested marriage as a way out of the spotlight. Here, for the first time, she lets herself want it, not as a strategy, just as a girl holding flowers. Then she lists the boys in her head, Eiichi, Yuujirou, Mitsuya, "Or someone else?", and the question stays open, in the best way.

## Chapter 6: Reign, but don't rule

"Prime Minister Koizumi has good luck." That's what everyone in Japan's political quarter is saying in the spring of 2002, and Chapter 6 opens by showing why. One scandal after another takes out the prime minister's critics rather than the prime minister himself. The party's secretary-general is pushed out, members suspected of embezzling secret funds leave the party, the opposition politicians investigating them resign under suspicion of their own, and Katou, Koizumi's "sworn friend and rival," quits over a scandal. In the cabinet reshuffle, Runa's ally Izumikawa becomes foreign minister with orders to clean up the Foreign Ministry, the minister for financial affairs from Izumikawa's faction is removed, and the reforming economist Takenaga takes that job on top of his own. The economy is sagging after the American tech crash, and the narration says plainly what is keeping it upright: "the massive railway work being done with the Keika Group at its center."

That's a striking thing for a novel to admit. A private fortune is doing the government's job of public spending in a slump.

We watch her do it. In Fukui she comes to buy a failing local railway, sees an enormous Buddha built during the bubble by a taxi millionaire and now surrounded by a ghost town, and buys the temple too: "If they forgot to add a soul to the Buddha statue, we need to be the ones to put it in." In Osaka she launches a new line costing about four hundred billion yen, which the narration notes was also "the amount needed to pressure the Osaka Diet members." When Tachibana asks whether, if they're going to oppose the prime minister, they shouldn't think about forcing him out, she nearly shouts: "Definitely not! Then the opposition party will get all the power!!" She remembers what the opposition did the last time it governed, and she reads Koizumi's unspoken threat to his own party as applying to her too: "If you drag me down, I'll break you up." In Nagoya she buys three freight lines that happen to serve Eiichi's family's home ground, and Eiichi, at the opening ceremony, mutters, "…You know, I don't hate this side of you."

Then comes the first crack. AIRHO, the little Hokkaido airline, is losing billions as nobody flies after the attacks, and it's about to pull the hotel group, "the first company that I directly controlled," into the red. The hotels want it cut loose. Runa moves it under the railway instead.

All this leads to the real subject of the chapter, which is how her empire is run. Runa describes it honestly: "The Keika Group was currently nothing more than a one-woman company with me at the top." Her motto is "reign, but don't rule," the old formula for a constitutional monarch, who is the head of state but leaves the actual governing to others. She owns everything and interferes in nothing so long as it makes money. But every decision still has to be reported to her, the presidents' council is "a social gathering," and the government is now considering breaking the group up.

Tachibana explains, kindly, why none of her companies has ever asked to go its own way: "you're the most convenient person to have on top of the companies. You reign without ruling, yet you still protect the workers' employment, collect new achievements, and even guide them onto the right paths." It's like sheltering under a very large tree. "However, for our sake—and for yours, my lady—it's about time for that course to come to an end."

So the adults draw up a new structure, and the centrepiece is listing Keika Holdings, the group's bank, on the stock market. Listing means selling shares to the public, so that outside shareholders watch the company and the owner no longer decides everything alone. The government wants half the bank's shares out of Runa's fund. Ichijou reads out the compromise to a room of seven: ten percent to a foundation the Keikain family controls, ten percent each to the trading company and the railway, nineteen point nine percent kept by the Moonlight Fund, and the rest sold to the public. Each friendly holder stays just under the levels that would trigger regulators' attention, and together they still hold almost half. It's a bit like keeping control of a club by spreading your votes among trusted friends, each with just under the amount that would get them noticed. The few trillion yen raised are already earmarked for railways.

The meeting quietly rearranges her household. Ichijou asks Angela to join the bank's board and build "Japan's first investment bank," and admits, with a sad smile, "In the end, I haven't been able to find a successor." Nakamaro announces he's leaving the bank's board; the former big-city bankers have used the listing to push the family out. When Runa eagerly offers the fund's money for a coming pharmaceutical merger, her foster father says just one word, "Runa," and smiles. Then: "That job belongs to Nakamaro and me. If you involve yourself in every little thing, you'll have no time for yourself. Isn't that why all of this started in the first place?" She goes red. For once, she hears it.

A second, unlisted holding company under the railway will hold the hotels, the department stores, the ferries and the resorts. The railways themselves will stay private, because, as Tachibana says, "Railroad projects are long-term endeavors. They can't be visualized from quarterly reports." He'll stay three more years, organise everything, and pass the baton. She needs, he thinks, "at least thirty more years" before she can lead the group herself, and she does the sum in her head: she'd be in her forties, and Tachibana would be… The sentence just stops. "Live a long life so that you can keep on supporting me," she tells him. "Please don't forget that I'm farther along than you," he answers. Then he hands her a blank sheet for a company motto, and she writes, "Connecting your present to the future."

When the meeting is over, she's left with three realisations, and the last one is among the loneliest lines in the series: "Everyone would leave someday," she still doesn't have enough people, and in the game she had even fewer. "No, I was all alone in the first place."

And then, in the same chapter that was supposed to shrink her role, she buys three more companies. The government wants the trading houses consolidated, so her trading company is to absorb two weak ones carrying nearly three trillion yen of debts, much of it in Southeast Asia. "Fine, then. Let's eat them up," she says. A pun on a company name reminds her of a textile firm that is about to collapse over accounting fraud, and she adds that too, telling Okazaki, "I'm a girl, and I'm old enough to be interested in makeup now," because its cosmetics arm is the real prize. She privately knows that they're "on the verge of coming face-to-face with hell." Toudou, who runs the trading company, asks to rename the merged firm "Keika Corp," so that the staff feel they belong to the family.

There's also a lovely scene with the boys. Their backup company is thriving, Karin wants to buy Runa's half, and Eiichi is asked what he'd do if he ran his family's group. His answer shows how differently he thinks: he won't even think about running a car company until he has a driving licence. "Watching Runa has made me realize she has the foresight of the gods, but I can't say the same about myself. If I just kept rolling the dice over and over again like that, I'd go bankrupt in the end." And then: "I would be betting the lives of the Teia Group workers and their families." He wants a failing data-centre company that has come to him for help, and Runa, unsure, calls Karin, who arrives within the hour and gives her verdict in one breath: "You need to buy this company. If you don't, then I will." Data centres "sort it, analyze it, and sell it. They're gold mines." Forty million people will carry Keika's new all-in-one card, and their shopping records alone will repay the investment. The old owners failed because "They built a birdhouse, but never got the birds to put in it."

When Eiichi says they founded their company so they could see what Runa sees, Karin gently corrects him: "I don't mean to be rude, but my lady is the investor, not the president." "…Ah," says Eiichi, and you can almost hear something click. Runa settles it by letting Karin take half and "boss around the four of us." "My tuition fee is quite high, children," Karin warns them.

The chapter ends with Runa at her most mischievous. Carrying a lunchbox and a guest pass, she walks into the headquarters of the troubled Honami Bank as a "niece" bringing lunch to her uncle, observing that in Japanese organisations "the outer layer was impenetrable, while security inside was laughably weak." Her backup company is being bullied by a project manager from Furukawa Telecoms, whose firm helped cause the bank's computer meltdown and is now pushing the blame down onto subcontractors. "What idiot brought this brat in here?!" he shouts. She hands him her business card, then her phone, with Karin, the woman about to run his entire company, on the line. He drops it. Her company pulls back to backup work only, Furukawa Telecoms withdraws from the bank's main system altogether at a cost of nearly ten billion yen, and Runa tells the exhausted engineers, "Let's get rid of this system and build a real one."

It's a fun scene, and I won't pretend I didn't enjoy it. But this chapter has a sobering shape underneath the comedy. The adults are answering Volume 3's worries with ordinary corporate medicine: listings, holding companies, the gaze of outside shareholders. The price is her household, as the people she leans on are each given a leaving date. And she can't stop adding to what she owns. Karin's little sentence names the gap better than anyone: Runa has never actually run anything. She buys and protects and commands from above, and the man in the machine room only finds out who she is when the phone rings. In a few chapters, the prime minister will say the same thing much less kindly.

## Chapter 7 and the railway thread: The girl the cameras couldn't see

After the boardrooms, the book gives us a joke, and it's a very good one. Chapter 7 is told almost entirely in overheard radio chatter, scraps of conversation and sound effects: *trot, trot, trot*, *ding*, *staaaare*. The premise is simple. Kaihouin Hotaru, Runa's small, silent friend from a house of exorcists, has been challenged to walk into Keika Tower unseen. If she reaches Runa and Asuka on the top floor without being caught, she wins the decorations off their cake.

Against her stands the most heavily guarded building in Tokyo. And it's Angela, not Runa, who has mobilised everything, after Hotaru "crushed Lady Runa in a game of hide and seek." There are station guards, the whole maid corps, plainclothes men "from the company," and suits of powered armour, armoured exoskeletons of the kind that became a symbol of terror in a northern city's underground mall, now wearing sashes that say "Guard Training." Commuters complain that it's frightening. In the café on the ground floor, a police detective on a stakeout looks down to find his apple pie gone and a hundred-yen coin in its place. The thief, it seems, is an honest one.

The guards know what they're up against: "she doesn't show up on surveillance cameras," and it turns out the armour's heat sensors can't see her either. The express lift arrives at the top floor completely empty. Okazaki, stepping into a lift with the guards, says aloud to the air, just in case she's in there with him, "I bet some money on you, so do your best." His verdict on Hotaru's gift is less playful: "If someone evil had that power, they would probably use it to become a terrorist."

Then, on the stairs, comes the moment that tips the joke into something stranger. A voice answers the silence: "Who goes there? Who called me here…? Ah, it's you. Trying to scare the girl?… I'm a supernatural tanuki too. I love to startle people, and I won't complain, since you're the one who found me when I was collapsed in the city street." A tanuki is the Japanese raccoon dog of folklore, a playful shape-shifter. A guard shrieks that "Something just touched my neck," the cordon collapses, and a moment later there's a knock on Runa's door. "See, Angela-san?" crows Asuka. "You can't beat Hotaru-chan when it comes to hide and seek!" Angela, who has spent the whole afternoon losing to a schoolgirl and a raccoon dog, replies with great dignity: "Yes, I've come to understand that all too well."

The final gag is my favourite. A few days later, someone finds Runa hiding in a cardboard box, wailing, "I wanna have hide and seek powers like Hotaru-chan too!"

It's a sketch, but it isn't empty. The whole volume is about adults pouring resources into controlling a child, and here is the one time they fail and nothing bad happens. A tower with a police tunnel, a private army, powered armour and two intelligence services can't keep out one little girl who wants cake. There are things in this world that no money, law or surveillance can reach, and Hotaru keeps winning, at board games and at hide-and-seek, for exactly that reason.

The chapter is followed by an interlude, a page from an anonymous railway fans' message board, "Keika Railway Thread #38," and it does something I find quietly brilliant. It shows us Runa's empire through the eyes of the public.

The fans are amazed and a little appalled. They marvel at the new Shikoku bullet train line through a region where, as one puts it, "That area's got no people in it!!" Demand is partly manufactured, with Hokkaido pilgrims flying down on Runa's airline to walk Shikoku's eighty-eight temples. They joke about the maids on board: "those maids aren't sky marshals, they're train marshals," and they also sell snacks. They gasp at the money: "They've already spent over a trillion yen!" They report that local governments asking Tokyo for help now "make the pilgrimage to Keika Railway in Kudanshita" as well as to parliament and the ministries. And in her lobby, two neighbouring prefectures squabble over a proposed extension like children: "No more water from Yoshino River for you!" "You eat too much udon!!"

Among the posters is one with the user name **lunakeikain**, who seems to know a great deal. Pressed on where the money comes from, she drops a hint about the group's bank, and on the politics she sounds aggrieved: after the big Shinjuku line, Keika was "labeled as opposition and got bullied in secret," and she wonders "how long will they hold out?" The other posters spot the danger the main story keeps offstage. Keika has borrowed against its shares, and "if those paper gains turn to paper losses, they'll collapse as fast as Super Taiei did." lunakeikain's last word is a small, wry complaint: "The stock market doesn't appreciate them, though…"

I love the double view. The fans see what the boardroom said in Chapter 6: the railways are regional policy paid for privately, financed by a bank the state is forcing onto the stock market, and the prime minister's office has noticed. And if lunakeikain is who the name suggests, which the series has been hinting since Volume 3, then under an anonymous name Runa gets to say what she can't say in public.

## Chapter 8: Who punishes the duke's daughter?

Chapter 8 has no narrator at all. It's a transcript of a daytime television *wide show*, the Japanese format that mixes news, gossip and studio chat, complete with stage directions for what the monitor behind the panel is showing. Nothing dramatic happens in it. And yet, by the end, I think it's the most dangerous thing anyone has done to Runa so far, because all it does is explain her.

The host opens on the cabinet reshuffle and the scandal at the Foreign Ministry: officials dismissed, the prime minister's approval falling. "No one is walking away from this unscathed," he says, and then adds that a few people have "managed to avoid drawing the short straw." A guest asks who. "Ambassadors and envoys from noble families."

From there the show glides into a cheerful little primer on the peerage, the five ranks of nobility that in this version of Japan were never abolished after the war, and then promises a look at "the daily lives of nobles that few people get to see." Up on the monitor comes Runa, running in a marathon through central Tokyo.

The host is careful and fair. He introduces her as the girl who leads the Keika Group, with total assets "somewhere close to twenty trillion yen," and he notes that critics point to its closeness to politicians, but also that it "has constantly worked for the good of this country." Then the pictures change: Runa eating with the then prime minister, Fuchigami, at his official residence; Runa with her arms raised in a politician's campaign office on election night. She is, the host explains, "the source of funding for the Izumikawa faction."

A guest asks the obvious question: why is a little girl involved in all this? The answer is "the special noble privilege of diplomatic immunity," and the show explains it in plain terms for the audience at home. The privilege "allows the heads of noble families to decide what happens to their own households." If a member of a noble house is accused of a crime, it's the family that judges them, under its own house rules. The nobles in the Foreign Ministry scandal avoided arrest exactly this way. There's meant to be a check, the Privy Council, an old advisory body of senior statesmen described as the "watchmen of the constitution," but since the war it has left such matters to the families and has never punished anyone.

The best way I've found to picture this is a private club that's allowed to judge its own members by its own rulebook. If one of them is accused of something, the police step back, the club holds its own hearing, and it sends a short report to a committee that never overrules it.

Then the host says the sentence the whole chapter has been building towards: "Now, if this girl were to do something wrong, it wouldn't be the police, prosecutors, or judges who would punish her, but only her father, Duke Keikain Kiyomaro." A guest is shocked: the parent punishes the child? How would anyone know if he went easy on her? "Indeed," says the host, "and that's probably on purpose. The Keika Group has placed all of its assets in the hands of young Keikain Runa." A diagram appears showing the whole group bound together by the Moonlight Fund, "headquartered on the west coast of the United States. In fact, the fund is owned by the girl herself."

The studio erupts. "No one would believe an elementary schooler owns a company worth almost twenty trillion yen!" "How can we believe that punishment will be fair and just?!" "We're standing by and watching injustice in action." And on the monitor, as if on cue, comes the photograph from Chapter 5 of Runa walking past the opposition politician's son as he gives his speech about equality at the school gate.

The host wraps up with a political note. Koizumi can be expected to go after this privilege. But reform of the Privy Council is being led by its chairman, the deputy prime minister Izumikawa, and Fuchigami, the former prime minister and Runa's old ally, now sits on it as a count, which makes the council "a stronghold for these opposition forces the Koizumi administration speaks of."

What unsettles me about this chapter is how fair the programme is. It never calls her a villain. It credits Keika with cleaning up the bad debts. It only lays out the structure, and the structure is exactly as it says: a child who owns everything, answerable to nobody but her father. Back in Chapter 2, Runa herself admitted her father "was the only one who could formally scold me," and when he did, he handed her a book. Every image on that monitor is a scene we watched from the inside as charm or triumph, the marathon, the lunch, election night, the walk past the speech. Re-cut for daytime television, they become a case against her. Tachibana warned her that the media wanted to see her fall. The truth is subtler and worse: they only need to describe her.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Wide show**: a Japanese daytime television programme mixing news, gossip and studio discussion.
- **Peerage**: the system of noble ranks (in Japan: duke or prince, marquess, count, viscount, baron).
- **Duke (prince)**: the highest noble rank; the Keikain house is a dukedom.
- **Noble "diplomatic immunity"**: in this world, the right of a noble house to judge its own members under house rules, keeping them from ordinary arrest and trial.
- **Privy Council**: an advisory body of senior statesmen meant to oversee constitutional questions, including noble privileges.
- **Faction**: an organised group of politicians within a party, loyal to a leader.
- **Moonlight Fund**: Runa's Silicon Valley investment fund, which owns the core of the Keika Group.

## Chapter 9, part one: The professor's two hands

Chapter 9 is the heart of the book, the chapter the author says the whole volume exists for, and it's long enough that I'll tell it in four parts. It opens quietly, in Professor Kanbe's seminar room, with a question scrawled on a whiteboard.

"Do you know how much money you'd need to save this country?" He works it out in front of her. Say a decent life costs four million yen a year, for eighty years: that's three hundred and twenty million yen per person. Multiply by Japan's hundred and forty million people and you get about forty-five quadrillion yen. The country's entire wealth is only a little over one quadrillion. Then he grins and offers a "trick": borrow against all of it, the way the financial markets do, fifty times over. Leverage, the same tool Okazaki used against WCI, would conjure the money on paper. "Can you actually do that?" Runa asks. "Probably not. But isn't it a fascinating thought experiment?"

So why not make everyone happy? First, he says, define happiness. Runa picks the family in a long-running television cartoon, a house with a garden near Tokyo, and privately thinks it "a life that felt like a distant dream in my past existence." Kanbe shows her why not everyone can have even that. There isn't room for everyone in the cities, so it becomes musical chairs, and the people already sitting down win simply by not getting up. That, he says, is a handicap.

Then comes the passage that is the intellectual centre of the volume. "Neoliberalism. This country has an image of personal responsibility, but its true essence is in the way it gives handicaps." Giving everyone the same four million yen would be equality of results, "what's called communism or socialism," and he notes that the Eastern Bloc failed that "pilot study" around the time she was born. Neoliberalism is something else: "'I'll give you a chance, but it comes with a handicap.'" Think of a race in which everyone is allowed to run, but not everyone starts from the same line. "But isn't that unfair?" Runa asks. "Sure. It's based on the premise that people aren't equal."

Then he asks her why humans protect the weak at all, when many animals leave their weakest behind in a hard winter. "Because humans are special?" she guesses. "No, it's because we want to believe we're special." He writes "privileged class" on the board, and beneath it, in red, *noblesse oblige*, the old idea that privilege brings a duty to help others. The privileged prey on the weak and also give to them, because their own sense of being special depends on it, and "Those people are called nobles in this country." His instruction to her is gentle and merciless at once: "you only have two hands, and that's not enough to save all of Japan." Come to understand the weak person in front of you, he says, decide whether to save them or let them be sacrificed, and "select as many of the weak as your hands can hold."

Runa hears it through the lens of her own future. "One such weak person was going to bring about my downfall," she thinks, and she surely means the game's scholarship-student heroine, Takanashi Mizuho. "If I was going to go down in history as the loser no matter how much I struggled, I needed to be a good loser at the very least." Kanbe, not knowing any of that, closes the class: "Noblesse oblige. From the bottom of my heart, I'm praying that you become a true noble."

The next scene is one Runa never sees. Takenaga, now in charge of both the economy and the banks, has invited Kanbe to a members-only club in a hotel, and he comes straight to the point: "I want you to know this—because she might turn against us." The next session of parliament will bring the complete clean-up of bad debts, a new accounting rule and pressure on banks to list their shares, and every one of those lands on something Runa owns. "The world is currently at the mercy of the young lady's whims," Takenaga says, and the prime minister "isn't going to allow that." Washington has told Tokyo it can no longer overlook the Foreign Ministry's paralysis, and its "axis of evil" speech shows it means war. "Having the two of them at the very top during a war would be a total nightmare," he says. As I read it, he means two centres of power in one country, the prime minister and the girl. And then, more softly: "The prime minister says he doesn't want the girl dealing with any more matters of blood. She's still young enough that she needs to be protected."

Kanbe, worried for her, says he's told her to leave Japan because the country will kill her talents. "Just killing them would be better," Takenaga replies. "She's going to be unfairly judged." And Kanbe, trying to help, mentions that she runs gated communities across the United States, including one in Florida: "If she felt like it, she could escape. Don't forget that." The narration adds, almost sadly, that he "didn't fully comprehend what it meant to give that information to Prime Minister Koizumi." He has just told the government where her exit door is.

Then the programme is announced, in seven points. It's dense, so here it is in kitchen terms. The banks must value what they own at today's real prices, the pudding at what it would actually fetch rather than what was paid for it. They must stop classifying the same big borrower differently from one another, and publish the gap between their own assessments and the inspectors'. Rules are set for injecting public money. A bank may no longer count future tax refunds as solid capital, which is a bit like counting a voucher you can only spend if you make a profit later. Banks that miss their targets get improvement orders. And on top of everything comes the seventh point, full market-value accounting, which the narration calls "the real bomb," because any losses still hidden at old prices would surface all at once. The target is to halve the big banks' bad loans by March 2005, with nationalisation for those that fall behind. Takenaga also names Runa's bank on television: it should list its shares "as a symbol of zaibatsu dissolution." Ichijou answers at a press conference that they are "a private company now and nothing more."

The prime minister's enemies inside his own party want Keika on their side, and her ally Izumikawa offers to sabotage the session from within. Here is the detail I think readers most often miss: Runa says no. "Taking out the minister will cause a backlash that reaches the prime minister too. I'll hand over the achievements and step back myself." Izumikawa marvels: "sometimes I still wonder why you're willing to sacrifice yourself. Yet you always achieve more than you give away." She warns him not to fight: "The prime minister is a wounded beast right now. Friend or foe, he's not going to go easy on anyone who stands against him." His old friend Katou has just been forced out, his approval ratings have fallen, and he has purged enemies and allies alike.

So she yields on the banks. She steps back. And it doesn't matter, because the banks were never really what the prime minister wanted from her. As the chapter's next morning will show, what he wants to take out of her hands is the war.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Quadrillion**: a thousand trillion.
- **Leverage**: borrowing to multiply the size of an investment.
- **Neoliberalism**: a belief in markets, competition and personal responsibility over state protection.
- **Equality of results vs equality of opportunity**: everyone ending up with the same, versus everyone getting a chance from different starting points.
- **Eastern Bloc**: the Soviet Union and its allied communist states, which collapsed around 1989–91.
- **Noblesse oblige**: French for "nobility obliges," the duty of the privileged to help others.
- **Market-value (current-value) accounting**: valuing assets at what they would sell for today.
- **Deferred tax assets**: future tax savings a bank counts as part of its capital.
- **Public funds injection**: the government putting money into a bank in return for shares.
- **Nationalisation**: the state taking ownership of a company.
- **Zaibatsu dissolution**: breaking up the family conglomerates, which in this world never happened after the war.
- **Gated community**: a walled housing estate with controlled entry.

**Precision pointers**
- *"About forty-five quadrillion yen"* → ¥320 million × 140 million people = ¥44.8 quadrillion, against national assets of just over ¥1 quadrillion; leverage of fifty times gives ¥50 quadrillion. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 9)
- *"A race in which everyone is allowed to run"* → my analogy for Kanbe's "chance with a handicap." His own example is musical chairs in the cities. The map's full discussion is in Part II §5, and the context file §10a explains the passage. (Vol. 4 map, Part II §5)
- *"The programme… in seven points"* → nearly word for word the real Takenaka Plan (Programme for Financial Revival, 30 October 2002), moved four months earlier, with market-value accounting added as a seventh point. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 9; Part II §4f)
- *"The real bomb"* → in this world the Nikkei stands near 19,000 and the biggest banks have already cleaned up, hence the in-world doubt "whether it was necessary to go this far." Market-value accounting could still send even clean banks back to the start. (Vol. 4 map, Part II §4f)
- *"Two centres of power"* → my reading of an ambiguous line; the text does not say who "the two of them" are. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 9, movement 2)
- *"Runa says no"* → she declines both the anti-Koizumi factions and Izumikawa's offer to cut short the session extension. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 9, movement 3)

## Chapter 9, part two: Half past five in the morning

"My lady! Please wake up!" It's half past five in the morning, and Tokitou Aki, one of the senior maids, is shaking Runa awake with a grave face. The special investigations unit of the Tokyo prosecutors' office, the elite team that goes after politicians and big companies, is about to search Tachibana and Keika Railway.

To see why, you have to follow a thread back a long way, so I'll go slowly.

During the bubble, Runa's late father ran a company called Far Eastern Developments, which planned a big chemical complex at Sakata on the Japan Sea coast. People with inside knowledge of such plans could buy the land cheap and sell it dear once the project pushed prices up, and that, Runa admits, "was what my father had done." His company later went bust, was absorbed into Runa's railway group, and became Keika Development. Now for the second strand. The office of Katou, Koizumi's old friend and rival, had bought land at Sakata at the very top of the bubble, and later sold it to Keika Development at the old bubble price. In effect that was a payment to Katou, and his office never declared the profit. That's tax evasion, and it's why Katou resigned earlier in the spring. I want to be clear about one thing, because it's easy to get wrong: the tax evasion is Katou's office's, not Runa's. Keika is caught by what she calls "a stray bullet."

Except it isn't really a stray. Tachibana sees it at once. The prosecutors' office, he says, "isn't going after former Secretary General Katou." He believes they are "digging up the old issue of Far Eastern Development's collusion with the East," her father's secret dealings with communist Northern Japan, which were buried long ago under the family's noble privilege. Think of an old sweater. Pull on the loose thread of Katou's tax case, and the whole sleeve of her father's crimes starts to unravel.

So before breakfast, her foster father, Duke Kiyomaro, invokes the family's immunity for its companies. Runa only hears about it at the table, and she wails: "Why?! Why is the Keikain family invoking diplomatic immunity over this?! It's like they're saying there's a reason to suspect us!" It's the same trap as refusing to answer questions in court: the right exists, but using it makes you look guilty. It's the family's decision, though, and she has no say.

She asks Tachibana how the privilege actually works, and his answer leaves her "dumbstruck." Each noble house may enrol a set number of people, more for higher ranks and a lot for a duke. The house judges them under its own family rules and simply reports the result to the Privy Council. And because houses rarely fill their quotas, it has become normal to take in outsiders, "criminals such as tax evaders in exchange for a kickback." Lately the government has sometimes pressed the council to overturn a family's verdict. This is the "colossal privilege" her grandfather invented, and the daytime show of Chapter 8 has just finished explaining it to the whole country.

By seven o'clock every channel is carrying the story, with reporters at the foot of her tower, and Runa, sipping grape juice, suddenly sees the whole design: "This was an attack from Prime Minister Koizumi himself, and his ultimate target was me."

The rest of the day is a war council in the Moonlight Fund's operations room, and it's devastating, one honest adult at a time. The opposition party sends people to offer help. "Send them away," Runa says, since any link would only arm the press, and Koizumi already has the public with him. Half joking, she asks Angela how long she'd survive if she fled to America. Angela doesn't blink: "If you ran away now, Eva would kill you before you even crossed the international date line." Runa knows why. America hasn't forgotten the market crash she caused, and it protects her only as long as it chooses to.

Anisha, a former KGB agent, calls the attack "half-hearted" if the aim were to destroy her. Tachibana and Ichijou are under fire, but Toudou's trading company and the family's drug company are untouched. "They're only trying to cut off your own functioning limbs, my lady." Kitagumo, a former Northern Japanese agent, adds that her allies in government are trapped inside the cabinet and can't move.

Then Okazaki stands up with "an unpleasant report." The Cabinet Intelligence and Research Office, the prime minister's own intelligence service, has found the Bahrain project. Runa drops her head onto the table. Of everything she's done, this is the one thing "the prime minister had every right to be furious with me over": an elementary school girl preparing a war in place of her government.

Tachibana, the man at the centre of the raid, is the one who says it. The prime minister has one goal: "'Make the girl behave.'" Everyone has acknowledged what she's done for the group and the country, he says, "However, this needs to be said: why don't you take a break for a while?" And then, speaking for all of them: "My lady, please withdraw from Iraq."

Angela and Eva say nothing, and their silence tells her that Washington and Tokyo have decided this together. "Is that what the whole world wants?" she asks. Tachibana answers with the kindest cruelty in the book: "I don't believe that's something for an elementary school child to feel."

She isn't jailed. Her money isn't touched and her title isn't taken away. She notes, with a flash of her old dry humour, that "Compared to the usual condemnations and banishments of villainesses, this was a rather lukewarm outcome." But she knows what it is: "my conclusive loss." Then the prime minister makes his announcements: public money for all the megabanks, a state scheme to buy up banks' shareholdings, a bill to outlaw "the recent use of nobles' special privileges in criminal cases," and peace talks with Russia over Northern Karafuto.

What strikes me hardest, rereading this, is how precisely the attack was built. The prosecutors couldn't touch a duke's household, so the government arranged things so that the household had to invoke its privilege on live television, weeks after a talk show had explained that privilege to the nation and just before the government announced a bill to abolish it. Her father's crime, the payout to Katou's office and her grandfather's legal invention were all cashed in on a single morning. And she had already given in on everything except the war.

- **Special investigations unit**: the elite team in the Tokyo prosecutors' office that pursues political and corporate crime.
- **Compulsory search**: a raid carried out under a warrant.
- **Bubble**: Japan's late-1980s boom in land and share prices.
- **Tax evasion**: illegally failing to declare income to avoid tax.
- **Noble immunity ("diplomatic immunity")**: in this world, a noble house's right to judge its own members under house rules.
- **Privy Council**: the advisory body that is supposed to review noble houses' judgments.
- **Kickback**: an illegal payment in return for a favour.
- **KGB**: the Soviet Union's security service.
- **CIRO**: the Cabinet Intelligence and Research Office, the Japanese prime minister's intelligence agency.
- **Megabank**: one of Japan's giant merged banks.
- **Northern Karafuto**: the northern half of Sakhalin; in this world it is claimed by both Japan and Russia and governed by neither, which is the "crisis" the talks are about.

## Chapter 9, part three: A dream of a vote, and the man who caught her

That night Runa dreams of the game. She calls it "a scene I loved from a game I loved," and I think that's the first sign of how strange and sad this dream is going to be.

She is in high school, or rather the game's Keikain Runa is, and Runa is watching her the way you watch yourself in dreams, from slightly outside. The class committees of the whole academy are voting on whether to abolish the special privileges of noble and zaibatsu children. The majority are "scholarship students who'd reached their boiling point over the discrimination they'd faced," and the face of the reform is the heroine, Takanashi Mizuho. Outside, the building is ringed with students shouting. At the top table sit the council: Eiichi as president, Yuujirou and Mitsuya on either side of him, and an empty chair, because the vice president, Keikain Runa, is on her feet and screaming.

"I promised we would improve! We even tried to make things more convenient! You! And you! And you! You were all lying when you said you'd join me!"

And in the dream, Runa finally understands something about the girl she was supposed to be. The game's Runa had built her own faction, split the opposition, and searched frantically for a compromise between her side and the reformers. "Keikain Runa was a reformist despite being a noble. Still, the game made her the loser in the end."

The vote is tied. The deciding vote goes to the president, her fiancé, Teia Eiichi, and the game's Runa smiles with relief, because of course he'll stand with her. "As president, I have decided to adopt this proposal." The students cheer. Mizuho smiles. It's the perfect triumph of good over evil, and afterwards Eiichi says the line that Runa remembers above all the others:

"Keikain, both of us are behind the times."

Still half inside the dream, she has some of the most honest thoughts in the series. She doesn't mind losing, she realises. "Not to Takanashi Mizuho and not to Teia Eiichi. Not to Koizumi Souichirou." They are all "first-rate actors—stars, even," and losing to them after giving her all would be bearable. "However, the one thing I didn't want to defeat me was posterity. Eras of time were like gigantic waves that destroyed everything in their path, no matter how much you struggled or how hard you worked." In the game's history the nobles had already been broken by "the gray-haired politician, Prime Minister Koizumi," and the global financial crisis of 2008 then stripped the zaibatsu of their power. "…A dream? Or is this whole life a dream?" she murmurs, and opens her eyes, still in her child's body, in her bed at the top of her tower, to a clear blue Tokyo sky.

Then she admits something that changes how the next scene reads. In her first life she liked Koizumi. "Like many others, I believed that he could destroy the sense of hopelessness we felt."

That afternoon she goes to the prime minister's residence, officially as Hokkaido's goodwill ambassador, to hand him some of the region's food in front of the cameras. They both smile for the photographers ("This is delicious," he says), and the moment the press is ushered out, she asks him straight: "Have I done something to upset you?"

"It's an adult's job to stop children when they're heading in the wrong direction."

She thought she was heading for the right outcome by the shortest road. He doesn't let her finish thinking it. "A child is trying to help kill people. Don't you think any adult would put a stop to that?"

Then he tells her about the day of the attacks, when she collapsed in front of the cameras and he caught her. "When you fainted during the terrorist attacks and I caught you, I was shocked at how light you were. I had to chastise myself for letting such a small child take on the fate of this country, and even the whole world."

She fights back the only way she can, with logic: isn't it a politician's job to bear that sort of thing if it's the best course? He answers with democracy. The person in his seat represents the majority of the people. "Keikain-kun, do you intend to leave both me and the people who chose me in the history books as those who helped a child commit murder for the good of their country?" She remembers the old line about democracy being the worst form of government except for all the others, and she knows she can't argue, because "I knew that I'd been like a sort of dictator." All she can manage is "…That's a cowardly way of putting it."

"This is what it means to be an adult, Keikain-kun," he says. And then he tells her what's really wrong, and it isn't her age. "You simply aren't qualified."

He lists it, calmly, with a smile that makes it crueller. "You aren't a Diet member. You may belong to a dukedom, but you aren't a duke. You aren't a soldier, and while you probably control the Keika Group, you don't represent them. You leave everything to proxies." Even the old political fixers, he says, came out to face the public when the time came, and she can't even choose to do that. "That's why you'll end up betrayed in times like these." Nor can she ever step forward at the very end and offer herself as a sacrifice for the country: "The people around you wouldn't let you."

That's the moment she breaks. "In that case, what if I don't become qualified until it's too late?! What if I've met with my downfall?!"

"You'll just have to live with it. Accepting the past from one's predecessors and giving the future to people like you is the job of adults."

She's crying now, and the reason is the saddest thing in the chapter. She knows what's coming. For her, the years ahead are a past she has already lived through, and she can't say so. "I knew I just couldn't convey what a terrible past we'd been given. He wouldn't believe me." He offers her a handkerchief, "not knowing that his words wouldn't reach me," and tells her that he believes in the people of this country and in their will to change. And then he asks her for one thing, almost gently:

"So, Keikain-kun, can't you wait a little longer? Don't take on the dishonor of letting the people sacrifice you for this country's prosperity."

I've read this scene many times and it still gets me, for two reasons. The first is that Koizumi is right. For four volumes we've cheered as Runa worked through proxies, Tachibana and Ichijou and the fund, because a child can't sign contracts. It was the clever workaround. Koizumi names it as the flaw. Nobody can vote her out, sue her or question her in parliament, and nobody can even name the office she holds, because she doesn't hold one. "Can't you wait a little longer?" is her uncle's "know when to quit," her brother's "You don't need to rush," and the professor's "I don't know why you're in such a rush," said one last time by someone with the power to make it stick.

The second reason is that she can't tell him why she's in a hurry. She is a woman who has already lived through the years he's asking her to trust, and the man asking is the one she once believed in, the one who caught her when she fell. She's being rescued, and she's too heartbroken to know it yet.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Scholarship students**: in the game, students admitted on merit, without noble or zaibatsu backing.
- **Zaibatsu**: a family-controlled business conglomerate.
- **Posterity**: future generations; Runa uses it to mean the tide of history itself.
- **Global financial crisis**: the crash of 2008, which in the game destroyed the zaibatsu.
- **Goodwill ambassador**: an honorary role promoting a place or cause; Runa represents Hokkaido.
- **Diet member**: a member of Japan's parliament.
- **Proxies**: people who act on someone else's behalf; Runa's managers sign and decide in her name.
- **Fixer**: a behind-the-scenes power broker; Runa's grandfather was one.

## Chapter 9, part four: Three phone calls

Chapter 9 doesn't end with the prime minister. It ends in the back of a car and then in an empty room, with three phone calls from three boys. In the game, these are the boys who betray her. And Runa, still raw from the residence, asks each of them the same astonishing question.

Eiichi calls first, before she's even home. "You reached out to Yuujirou when everything happened last time, right? I figured it was our turn now." Hearing his ordinary voice, she suddenly feels better, and the question just comes out: "Hey, Eiichi-kun, if you were going to betray me, when do you think you would do it?"

There's a silence, and then a very Eiichi answer: "Right now, I'd go find the version of me that betrays you and beat him up." She'd be a "wounded beast" by then, he reasons, too hurt to manage her risks well. Then he says the honest part: "Still, if I had to go up against you, I'd never win unless I took you down with me."

And in that moment Runa understands the line from her dream, the boy who voted against his own fiancé and told her they were both behind the times. "He'd gone down with me so that he could win." It isn't cruelty. It's the only way a boy who can't beat her could ever stand level with her. "I'm not going to lose to you so easily, you dummy," she tells him, and the narration lets us see the small, warm thing it does to her: "Just slightly, my mood improved. Just slightly, I felt my heart leap."

Yuujirou is next. He laughs that she can't keep a secret, since obviously Eiichi told her to call, and answers without a pause: "If I were going to betray you, it definitely wouldn't be my own choice. Either Eiichi-kun talked me into it or something had me blinded." It's a politician's son's answer. She realises he admires Koizumi, and he admits it: "he never goes back on a public commitment." Runa thinks that the heroine of the game must have seen the same appeal in Yuujirou that a stuck, frustrated country saw in its prime minister.

Then he describes her, and I think it's the most accurate portrait of Runa anyone gives in this book. "After all, you compromise, Keikain-san. You can't just keep running through everything like the prime minister, so when you can't get a major victory, you aim to avoid a major loss instead." She takes big risks with money, he says, but in politics, which isn't her real battlefield, there's no need for her to. She's embarrassed to be seen so clearly. Before he hangs up he lets slip, carefully, that his father is worried about her too, "and I think he's making moves," and stops there, because saying any more would hurt her.

The last call is to Mitsuya, alone in her room at the top of the tower. When she asks her question, he corrects her first. Betraying isn't the same as opposing; it means you used to be allies. And there are two reasons for it. "One reason to betray someone might be disappointment." Then, after a pause: "The other reason is good intentions."

She's startled. Good intentions, not malice? "Right, good intentions," he says. "Malice would just turn two people into enemies, and good will turning to malice would fall under the 'disappointment' category. But there's no way to defend oneself against a betrayal born of pure good will. After all, I would only betray you out of concern for you, Keikain."

"I watched myself gasp in the window's reflection," Runa says, and I can see her there, phone in hand, the city spread out behind her own face. Because that's exactly what has happened to her. Tachibana, Ichijou, Angela, her foster father and her brother all tried it, quietly and out of love, and she brushed every one of them off until the prime minister had to step in.

"Now let me ask you," Mitsuya says, "are you still going to stay on the same path, Keikain?" She doesn't answer. He sighs. "If we ever betrayed you, that would probably be why. I'm warning you as your friend."

The chapter's last line is a promise of worse to come: "Soon, I would learn the truth—the truth of what lay ahead when Prime Minister Koizumi stopped me."

I think these three calls are among the best things in the series. Each boy gives her a different route to the game's ending. Eiichi would bring her down so he could finally win. Yuujirou would be talked into it, or dazzled by a leader who never compromises. Mitsuya would do it out of worry for her. Mitsuya also gives the whole volume its key idea, betrayal born of good will. It names what her guardians did in Chapter 2 and what Tachibana did that morning, and it quietly rewrites the game. Maybe the villainess was never abandoned by enemies at all. Maybe she was overruled by the people who loved her.

- **PHS**: the Personal Handy-phone System, the cheap mobile phone Runa carries.
- **Wounded beast**: Runa's own phrase from earlier in the chapter for a cornered prime minister, which Eiichi turns back on her.
- **Risk management**: limiting how much you could lose.

**Precision pointers**
- *"I think he's making moves"* → his father is Izumikawa, the deputy prime minister and Runa's main ally in the cabinet. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 9)
- *"Different routes to the game's ending"* → the map reads the three answers as the game's three routes, and their portrait of her as the most accurate in the book. (Vol. 4 map, Ch. 9, Deeper significance)
- *"Betrayal born of good will"* → the map's table lists every act against her in the volume as this kind, from Ch. 2 to Ch. 12. (Vol. 4 map, Part II §2)

## Chapter 10: Two men agree to get their hands dirty

Chapter 10 is barely two pages long, and it's nothing but dialogue: an unattributed phone call between Prime Minister Koizumi and the American president, shortly after Runa left the residence in tears. No narration, no Runa. It's the adults' own account of what they've just done, and of the price they agree to pay for it.

"That was quite a show," the president begins. "You did a good job shutting up the young lady." Koizumi doesn't take the credit: "I couldn't do it on my own. I was able to silence her thanks to the people around her."

The president is not a sentimental man, and he goes straight to the money. Her strategy was built around the United States and was "linked to profits." Now that she's been silenced, does Japan mean to take those profits for itself? "That's only natural, don't you think, Mr. President?" Koizumi replies, and suggests they get through the crisis "together as partners." I appreciate that the book doesn't hide this. The man who stopped a child from profiting from a war is perfectly happy for his country to profit from it.

The president admits he feels guilty, "having silenced the girl who said that in the first place." Koizumi answers him with the argument he used on Runa: "Can we really allow the history books of those people to one day say, 'A young girl successfully brought prosperity to her country by becoming a murderer'?" And the fear behind it isn't only moral. "Your country also fears that she might turn into a monster, do they not? Russia feels the same."

The president can almost laugh about it. "'An elementary school girl started a war against the American, Japanese, and Russian governments.'" But she sees clearly and moves wisely, he says, and some in Washington have a name for her: the "Moon Mistress." Koizumi recognises it at once: "Is that from the Heinlein novel?" In Robert Heinlein's *The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress*, a colony on the Moon wins its independence from Earth in a revolution run from behind the scenes by a handful of people and a computer that nobody elected. The nickname fits her Moonlight Fund, and it fits her uncomfortably well.

The president turns the novel into policy. "A cruel mistress of the night doesn't suit our world today. We can't allow Karafuto's independence, even as a federation." The same goes, he adds, for Japan and Russia ending up "governed by one leader": nobody knows how the world order would change "with that young lady in control of a nation." Koizumi agrees, and the two men share a dark joke about their own histories, Japan's war on the Asian mainland half a century ago and America's lesson in Vietnam, and laugh.

Then Koizumi makes the promise the whole chapter turns on. "Mr. President, let's promise to stain our hands with blood. Can I have your word on this? You don't want a second Vietnam." "I'll do anything to avoid a second Vietnam," the president answers. "We'll take out Iraq's dictator and leave the rest to the Iraqi people."

They agree on the story they will tell the world. Japan has learned "the full story about those terrorist attacks from suspects we arrested here," and the two countries will publish it together: Iraq's dictator tried to get nuclear weapons, failed, and turned to terrorism instead, and even if Iraq has no weapons of mass destruction now, it could use them in the future. Then comes a timetable to "next fall": publication, a vote in the United Nations Security Council (the UN body that can authorise military force), and a multinational force.

The president sums up the principle he and Koizumi share: "right now, it's the people who set the world in motion. Their will means everything. Even if it's justified, it wasn't a divine message coming from a young maiden." And Koizumi answers with the line that names two earlier volumes of this series: "Indeed. That's why we need her to become Cassandra, although it's hard to say how long she'll need to stay in that role." Cassandra was the Trojan princess of Greek myth who could see the future but was cursed never to be believed.

The call ends with a fairy tale. She'll grow up, the president says, "fall in love someday and use her powers for her partner." "And then the spell on the princess will break, and she'll become a normal girl?" Koizumi muses. "That would make us the clock that strikes midnight." The president has the last word: "At the very least, why don't we have her lose her glass slipper?"

This little chapter is cold, and I think it's meant to be. Volumes 2 and 3 were titled after Cassandra, a prophet who was believed and still couldn't stop what was coming. Here the curse is put on her deliberately: the men who believed her decide that she must stop being listened to. They aren't her enemies. The president feels guilty, and Koizumi is protecting her from becoming something history would never forgive. But they aren't innocent either. They take her war plan, her evidence and her profits, and they take on the blood as their own responsibility, which, if you think about it, is exactly what Volume 3 meant by "a job for adults." And the glass slipper? In the story, it's the one thing that lets the prince find Cinderella again after midnight. Read beside Chapter 2's marriage viewing and Tachibana's "you just have to get married," I suspect the adults' shared plan for Runa is that she fall in love, and stop.

- **Heinlein**: Robert A. Heinlein, American science-fiction writer, author of *The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress* (1966).
- **Karafuto**: the Japanese name for Sakhalin; in this world, Northern Karafuto is a disputed territory and a hoped-for state.
- **Federation**: a union of self-governing states.
- **Second Vietnam**: a long war America can neither win nor leave.
- **UN Security Council**: the United Nations body that can authorise the use of force.
- **Multinational force**: an army made up of several countries' troops.
- **WMD**: weapons of mass destruction (nuclear, chemical or biological).
- **Cassandra**: the Trojan princess in Greek myth who foresaw the future but was never believed.
- **Cinderella**: the fairy tale in which the magic ends at midnight and a glass slipper lets the prince find her.

## Chapter 11, part one: A bad debt of twenty-three million people

*A content note before this piece: the chapter contains an invented plan for the mass killing of Iraqi civilians. No such plan existed. I explain below how the book presents it.*

A few days after the prime minister's scolding, the man who runs Runa's private military company, an American commander from the 1991 Gulf War known as the "Desert Hero," pushes past Angela into her rooms with a document. "Boss, I've got something you need to hear. The folks in Washington are fighting each other." The Pentagon and the State Department are at war over Iraq. She reads the paper he gives her, and she vomits.

He watches, and lets out a breath of relief. "Good. I was planning on quitting this job if you finished reading the whole thing and kept your cool." Angela is furious with him: "Everyone worked so hard to keep this a secret…!"

The paper comes from a think tank in Washington, and it calculates "the most efficient returns the US could receive in the Iraq war." The answer it reaches is to use nuclear, biological and chemical weapons on every front, at a cost of twenty-three million Iraqi civilian lives, while taking care not to damage the oil fields.

Angela, the former financial spy, explains the logic as calmly as she can. Iraq is what economists call a rentier state, a country that lives on the income from its natural resources the way a landlord lives on rent. Its government doesn't depend on its people's work, few of them are needed to pump the oil, and foreign experts can run the fields. "Because they want Iraq's oil, not their people."

And Runa, who has spent four volumes sorting good loans from bad, recognises the method at once. "A 'bad debt' of twenty-three million people." It's the pudding lesson turned on human beings. The planners have looked at a country and seen an asset worth less than what it costs to carry, something to be written off. "To them, the Iraqis weren't humans. They were debt. They weren't even people—just numbers."

Eva adds a second, colder piece of logic, an exchange rate. After Vietnam and Somalia, where American deaths forced America to give up, and after September 11th, some in Washington want "to show the world that the murder of a single American civilian would be repaid a hundred or a thousand times over." Bomb the cities, wreck the railways and highways, then use nuclear weapons "to set an example." Runa thinks: "Ah, it's like the Empire of Japan in 1945." And then the narration adds the line that quietly changes how you understand this whole world: "They were apparently going to reenact the history that never happened in this world here in Iraq." In this version of history, there was never a Hiroshima or a Nagasaki. Nobody has ever seen what one bomb does to a city.

The book names the authors of the paper as "a Jewish think tank," which Eva says is also "supported by the government of Israel," with revenge for Iraq's missile attacks on Israel in 1991 as the motive. I need to be straightforward about this. The plan is the novel's invention. No such plan existed, and pinning an imaginary genocide on a religious group and a real government repeats a well-known conspiracy theory about the Iraq War. The book partly complicates it: the person explaining the paper and nursing Runa through it is Eva, who, Runa later learns, is Jewish herself, and America's own diplomats and generals are shown opposing the paper. But the label is the text's, and I think it's the volume's least defensible choice.

For Runa, the real horror is closer to home. "Now I finally understood why the adults had forced me to distance myself." She had been working on the outbreak, the supply lines and the intelligence of this war. "Before the adults chased me away, I was so close to signing off on the execution of twenty-three million people." She cries, and the narration is careful about why: "tears from learning the true kindness behind the adults' rejection of me," and tears because some of those adults would approve such a thing for profit.

So why did the soldier show her? Her own preparations were built for a Gulf War-sized army of three hundred thousand men. With this paper going around, some in Washington now want even fewer troops. The State Department and the generals in the field are against it, but the Pentagon's leadership is keen. "I need your help to hold them back, my lady." She realises that even now, after being ordered out, "The battle in Washington continued to drag me across the Rubicon," the point of no return.

She stops eating. "I don't really feel like eating," she says, pushing her plate away, and then, very calmly, she asks for an intravenous drip, a feeding line into her arm. The tower has its own medical floor, and Angela, it turns out, already has a doctor who specialises in acute stress disorder waiting.

On the drip, half asleep, she pieces together the rest. The war's most committed advocates, the neoconservatives, learned from Vietnam that every American death moves the country closer to defeat, so they want a war won with technology and fewer than fifty thousand soldiers, and then to leave the rest to the UN. "That was why they were so overjoyed by my participation in the war. Foreign mercenaries, so long as they weren't Americans, could die without any effect on the management of the conflict." Iraq itself is "a mosaic," a Sunni-led dictatorship over a Shia majority close to Iran and over the Kurds, whose independence Turkey, Iran and Syria all dread. Its neighbours, too, might find the Iraqis' disappearance convenient.

Then she dreams. In the dream Okazaki tells her that Iraq is tied to Japan's peace talks with Russia: Russia won't give up Northern Karafuto's oil without something in exchange, and Iraq is the exchange. "Personally, I think this genocide is going to start with Russia," he says. Russia's conditions include that "the young lady never be made the head of Russia or any neighboring state." And then: "My lady, if you wished, you could even create your own kingdom in Iraq after the genocide."

She wakes retching, and what sickens her this time is herself. "I couldn't stand the ugly side of me who'd just sensed that a genocide was, from my perspective, the optimal solution."

And then, for the first time in the whole series, she tells us her first life plainly, with no jokes.

"I was alone. I had to love loneliness. I loved no one and received no love."

She was born into a modest middle-class family whose house was finished just as the bubble burst. Its value collapsed, and the loan repayments squeezed them. She went to university on student loans, believing things would get better, and then watched the financial crisis happen with her own eyes. To repay the loans she joined a company "that would later become known for its exploitative conditions." The work cut her off from her friends and ruined her health, "and in the end, I was abandoned. The only things I enjoyed in my short life were video games."

Then come the sentences that, for me, explain the whole series:

"I'd lived my second life in the world of this game. That was why it took me far too long to realize the truth. This world wasn't just made of beautiful things. There had to be losers to go along with each victor. This wasn't just playing a game—other people here had wills of their own."

I find this the hardest chapter in the book to read, and the most necessary. Until now a reader could still side with Runa against a prime minister who wouldn't listen. This paper turns his "a child is trying to help kill people" from rhetoric into arithmetic. What makes her sick isn't really the planners. It's the family resemblance: the method she has used for four volumes, writing off what doesn't pay, applied to a nation. And under it all is a woman who died young and alone and treated her second life like the game she loved, until the losers started having faces.

## Chapter 11, part two: Yes, I'm happy

Three days later, Runa is still on the drip and still can't keep food down, and her friends come to visit. The boys come, and so do Asuka, Kaoru, little Mio, Hotaru, and the other girls from the tea party, even quiet Kazuki Shiori. Runa receives them sitting in a chair, because she doesn't trust her legs. Asuka has brought a jar of her family's mandarin jam, which she proudly calls "Great Master Tanuki" jam, with "Hotaru's seal of approval." Yuujirou has brought notes from the classes she's missed. And when Mio hears that Runa hasn't eaten in three days, she bursts into tears: "Waaaaaaah! Runa-oneechan will die if she can't eat!"

Runa says she thinks she might manage a drink, Russian tea with Asuka's jam. While the maids go to make it, her maid Erika comes in with a videotape that one of the head maids has asked her to bring, "since you're all here together." Runa takes one look at it and wails, "Wait! Noooooo!" It's the video of her school entrance ceremony, five years ago, the little speech she recorded in the first volume for the grown-up she would become. Asuka and Eiichi, who are in it too, cringe along with her, and everyone laughs and squeals at the tiny versions of themselves.

Then the small girl on the screen starts to speak to the girl watching.

> Um, this is a message for the adult me.
>
> You haven't gone bankrupt, have you?
>
> You haven't joined a company that forces you to do useless tasks and drives you to bad health, right?
>
> Your future isn't pitch-black, is it?

Think about who recorded that. It's a woman who had already lived through exactly those things and died young, speaking through a six-year-old's mouth to the adult she was afraid of becoming again. And the eleven-year-old watching has just spent three days unable to eat because she has learned what her happiness might cost other people. She understands, sitting there, why she's been unable to let herself be happy. "I'd only ever known misfortune. I'd only ever known loneliness. But now that I'd experienced happiness and understood the necessary price of that happiness, I felt like I couldn't allow myself to feel joy."

The child on the screen goes on:

> Right now, I'm happy.
>
> Are you happy in the future?
>
> Even if you're not, please remember that you had this happy past once.
>
> Remember how you smiled like this.

And she sends her future self a smile, "so that you'll never forget it."

Runa smiles, and the tears spill over. Mio looks as if she's about to cry again too. And through her tears, Runa answers the little girl on the screen.

"Yes, I'm happy. So please don't worry."

Then the tea arrives, steaming, with orange jam stirred into it, and everyone in the room goes quiet and watches her pick up the cup. The words come to her without her thinking about them: "It's okay. Everything we eat involves taking a life. That life, that blessing, is something we must digest with the utmost respect." They are her mother's words, from her first life, the only gift from that life the series has ever shown us. "Thank you for the drink," she says, and she drinks, and for the first time in days, it stays down. It tastes of mandarins.

I've read a lot of light novels, and I can count on one hand the scenes that have moved me like this one. The chapter is called "Time to Eat," and it answers the last line of Volume 3, where Runa decided she would "rather eat before I get eaten." Here eating is neither conquest nor guilt. It's simply what being alive costs: a life taken, to be received with respect. It's the same thought as the little grace Japanese people say before a meal. And the person who says yes, I'm happy, is someone who has just learned exactly what her happiness can cost, and chooses to accept it rather than refuse to eat.

The rest of the chapter is a gentle recovery. Angela prescribes two weeks of rest at the family's summer villa in Karuizawa, "time to herself where she can relax without thinking about anything else," and Tachibana stays in Tokyo at Runa's request. There are walks, violin, a shopping mall, and baking with the maids. Erika, who has her own troubles, gently tells her off: "You are still a child, my lady. You should be treasuring these times more than you do." Erika is being flooded with marriage offers from company presidents' and politicians' sons, because she is both Ichijou's daughter and Runa's maid. Another maid, Tokitou Aki, has offers too, and Runa later learns why. Whoever marries Aki will take the Tokitou name, and the Tokitous will stand as a branch of the Keikain family. It "would sort out grandfather's bloodline, but also make the Tokitous the family that protected me." Even her household's love lives are part of her protection.

Then, over tea, Erika asks with total innocence, "Do you have feelings for one of those three boys?" and Runa spits out her grape juice. Aki follows up: "Didn't you meet with that family of European nobles in Hong Kong?" Runa protests that she just happened to be sitting next to them. "My lady," says Aki sweetly, "don't they always say there are no coincidences in high society?" So now she knows what the racecourse was.

Erika closes the subject with her own philosophy of life, and it's one of the sanest things anyone says in the volume: "I don't understand complicated things, but I'm smart enough to ask people who do understand them. I think it's fine to live that way. Trying to figure out everything myself would be wrong if it ended up making me fall apart." Then they eat the shortcake they've baked together, and Runa notices that the one she made tastes clearly worse than everyone else's. "At the very least, I wanted whoever ate my cakes to say they were 'delicious.'"

And then Eiichi turns up in Karuizawa. His family has a villa there too, he says, and he just thought he'd check on her. They meet in a little café by the station, rented out entirely for security, and through the window they watch children running about with their parents, every child smiling, except the two of them. He admits he knew something about the position she'd been put in. "I was even thinking about telling you to stop, but I didn't." Why not? "I guess I really do want to beat you."

Runa, teasing, plays the wise old woman: "Becoming an adult isn't a very good thing."

"Then why are you rushing to become one, Runa?"

She has no answer. She sips her tea instead. It's the third time in this volume that someone has asked her about rushing, after her brother and the professor, and this time she has no answer at all. She could argue with the professor that right now is the only time. She can't argue with the boy across the table.

Then she gets her own back. She mentions, casually, that she might be introduced to a prospective fiancé, from Europe, "even though I'm Japanese." As she says it, her blonde hair moves, and she thinks about how she can never quite be a "Japanese person," and how even in Europe people would treat her as different. Eiichi takes a moment, and then says, simply, "I see. But I don't want to give you up, Runa."

Her heart, the narration says, goes *ba-dump*. She pretends not to notice and offers to walk him to the station. He holds out his hand, and she takes it as if it were the most natural thing in the world, and they both start laughing. The walk takes a few minutes. "They were memories that were never in the game, just for Eiichi-kun and me."

At the station Erika slips him a bag of "cookies made by Lady Runa," which were in fact made by Erika and Aki. Runa scolds her all the way home, then quietly adds baking to her holiday timetable. Her cookies, we're told, still turn out worse than the ones Eiichi took home.

I love that the book ends this chapter on something so small: a girl who has bought trillions of yen of companies, practising cookies because she wants someone to say they're delicious. It's the first thing in the series she has made rather than bought, and she's bad at it. After a chapter about twenty-three million lives, that feels exactly right.

## Chapter 12: The second guy she scouted

Runa doesn't appear in the last chapter. It belongs to the men who work for her, clearing away what she built for the war, scheduling their own departures, and, in the very last sentence, naming the next crisis.

It opens with the Saudi railway. The line from the port of Dammam toward Hafar al-Batin, with its branch to King Khalid Military City near the Iraqi border, is fifty-one percent owned by the Saudi government and forty-nine percent by Runa's trading company, Akamatsu, which runs it. The Bahrain base has already gone to a private military company under American control. This time, the narration notes, the US military "didn't even try to hide their identity." It wants the railway.

Okazaki, who has to handle the sale, catches himself thinking, "That young lady is made of different stuff than I am…" He's proud to work for someone who can see the future so clearly and still feel for people, and he hasn't noticed how much more he's been smoking since she fell ill. The chief of her private military company, the soldier from Chapter 11 (the text now calls him the "Gulf Hero"), arrives with "an unofficial offer from the US military": the Defense Department wants the railway's internal files to prepare for the war. Okazaki hands them over. The line is eighty percent complete, and it was signed off in February 2000. "Actually, maybe I should say we've been compelled to have it finished by the start of the war."

Pause on that date, because it's the single hardest piece of evidence inside the story that Runa knew what was coming. February 2000 is nineteen months before the September attacks. And then Okazaki reveals her full design. The line was meant to run on to Amman, Jordan's capital, and all the way to Aqaba on the Red Sea, as her answer to what some of Washington's hard-liners whisper as "Baghdad departure, Jerusalem arrival," the idea that remaking Iraq would unlock peace between Israel and its neighbours. With that railway, American troops could move "with frightening ease." "Lady Runa has predicted the future of Iraq," Okazaki tells the soldier. "Be sure to reflect that in the final sales price as well."

The soldier's next question is the human one: "is that boss of ours really all right?" "She's not well," Okazaki says. "She nearly broke down." And the soldier says the line that I think states the volume's whole ethic, coming from a man who kills for a living: "We need to be grateful that this country's prime minister brought the boss back down to earth and scolded her. But at the same time, we need to shoulder the responsibility for the future, so that she doesn't fall apart." Okazaki, a little embarrassed, tells him to say that to her face himself. The next day, Akamatsu sells its stake to the United States, for a price nobody discloses.

Then comes a lunch, in a private room at the Keika Hotel, with no alcohol on the table, which tells you everything about the two men eating it. "I thought you hated me, Ichijou-san," Okazaki says. "I do. You led Lady Runa down a dangerous road. But in the business world, you can't just refuse to meet someone because you hate them."

Ichijou lays out the future. Tachibana will step down as head of Keika Railway before parliament abolishes noble immunity. Ichijou himself will "go out on top" when the bank lists its shares, becoming a chairman without the power to represent the company. Toudou will stay through the mergers. After them comes the "second generation," two Americans: Angela Sullivan, who will run the securities arm and then the whole bank, and Karin Viola. The Moonlight Fund, he warns, is "under heavy surveillance by the United States" now. Okazaki might lead "two generations after that," in three to five years.

What Ichijou says next shows how much he has changed since Volume 1. Angela, he says, made him realise something: "when Lady Runa plans to do something, she doesn't think of herself at all." She wiped out hundreds of billions of yen to clear the banks' bad debts. The giant electronics company was "just a bonus" on her real goal of reviving an industry. That's why, he thinks, she was so shaken when she had to argue "not just over money, but over life itself." He watched borrowers after the bubble "offer up their lives as repayment," and he knows what that kind of gambling costs. "Like you, Lady Runa is a gambler deep down. However, she's willing to put her life on the line to get a good rate."

Then he gives Okazaki a job. "Look for replacements for Miss Angela and Miss Karin. Japanese people would be preferable." The three senior men intend to move Okazaki out of the Moonlight Fund, partly so that no one can say he was bought by foreign money, and partly because of the Iwazaki family, the great zaibatsu that is now related to the Keikains by Nakamaro's marriage. The danger Ichijou sees is clear. As the narration puts it, the group is still "a one-woman shop" that stops if she stops. Angela and Karin will recruit abroad, and their people will be loyal to them. The Iwazaki people who come in through the marriage will be loyal to Iwazaki or the Keikain family. "At that point, the Keika Group will become a hodgepodge colony for the Iwazaki zaibatsu, with no one remaining who was there from the start… and Lady Runa will still be a minor."

There's a political tangle too. Some ultranationalists, people who want a Japan fully independent of America, now call Keika a "sellout company" and see Runa as an enemy "because of her birth and blonde hair." Oddly, they back Iwazaki, the one zaibatsu that grew up alongside modern Japan, and dream of a merger that would found "a Heisei Restoration." "I don't understand the logic myself," Ichijou admits. Okazaki asks who's pulling the strings, and Ichijou's answer is a single, chilling word: "The 'Ministries.'" The war is coming regardless, and somebody has to finish the preparations she began. Now Japan's foreign, defence and trade ministries, and America's Defense and State Departments, are fighting over her plan in "a terrible mudslinging contest." Koizumi didn't take the plan to run it himself. He handed it to the bureaucracies, and the bureaucracies are at war over it.

Okazaki asks why Ichijou has come to him. "The Keika Group has grown far too big, but Lady Runa still has a few years until she can truly make her debut in this world. When that happens, I don't want it to be in a future where her house of cards has collapsed and nothing remains for her." And Okazaki suddenly understands something about his employer that takes his breath away: "Lady Runa built the entire Keika Group while taking it for granted that she was going to lose everything…"

Then Ichijou, the cool, careful banker, raises his voice. He tells Okazaki what Tachibana once told him: she holds qualifications in bookkeeping, secretarial work and English, and she's aiming to graduate from university early. "She clearly believes the Keika Group may collapse and is preparing for it to happen. However, Tachibana-san and I refuse to let her grow up only to have nothing left!"

Okazaki quietly asks the waitress for a beer, the only alcohol at the table, and pours it into Ichijou's glass. "You really love Lady Runa too, don't you, Ichijou-san?" Ichijou pours some back into Okazaki's glass. "Of course I do. I'm the second guy she scouted." Tachibana was the first, Okazaki remembers, and Toudou the third, and he touches his glass to Ichijou's and drinks.

I can't read that exchange without smiling, and without a little ache. The banker she cornered when she was five years old is now organising a faction of loyalists so that something will be left for her when she grows up.

The very last lines are almost an afterthought. As they part, Okazaki mentions that he's heard Wall Street is up to something, and that Angela is supposedly involved. He's a trading-company man and doesn't "know anything about financial engineering." Ichijou, who calls himself "only a third-rate banker," promises to look into it. "What are they supposedly plotting?" And Okazaki answers:

"Apparently, there are these things called subprime loans…"

That's how the volume ends, and it's a perfect last sentence. The whole series opened on 15 September 2008, the night Lehman Brothers fell. Here, in 2002, two men who don't understand financial engineering name the instrument that will produce that night over one shared beer. Runa has spent the volume being told she can't save the world by herself. Now the thing she'll most need to save it from has just been named.

## Afterwards: What Volume 4 says Runa is

Every volume of this series is, underneath, an attempt to answer one question: what is Keikain Runa? Volume 4 answers it differently from the others. It stops asking what she is and starts asking what she's allowed to be.

Count the names people give her in this book. To Professor Kanbe she's "gifted," a child of remarkable abilities, and so, in his clinical phrase, a child who "can avoid taking responsibility." To the leaders of America and Russia she's a monster in waiting; "Your country also fears that she might turn into a monster," Koizumi tells the president, and Russia does too. To Washington's insiders she's the "Moon Mistress," named after a novel about a revolution run by a mind nobody elected. To the two heads of government on the phone she's Cassandra, not because the name fits, as it did in the two volumes before, but because they've decided she must become one, a prophet nobody listens to. And to herself, thinking about the game in a sharp, sad moment, she's "a sacrifice, taken down by Japanese culture and its systems."

Those are the grand names. The plain ones matter more.

She is someone who died once. Chapter 11 finally tells that first life straight, without jokes: the house that lost its value as soon as it was built, the student loans, the job that wore her down, the friends she lost, the early, lonely end, and video games as the only thing she enjoyed. And it explains, in her own words, the blind spot the whole series has been built around: she lived her second life "in the world of this game," and so it took her far too long to see that "other people here had wills of their own." The game was a licence, and this is the book in which the licence runs out.

And she is a child. That's the word every adult in the book uses, her brother, the professor, the prime minister, her maid Erika, and it's the one thing about her that is simply true. The book's quiet proof is the tape. The same person, at six years old, was so afraid of the future she had already lived that she recorded a message begging her grown-up self not to have gone bankrupt, not to have been worked into the ground, not to be facing a future that was "pitch-black."

What I find so moving about Volume 4 is that it lets all of this be true at once. She's brilliant, and she's out of her depth. She's generous, and she's dangerous. Her guardians aren't wrong to fear a dictator, and the prime minister isn't wrong to say a child shouldn't be helping to plan a war. And yet every single act against her in this book comes from love, or from duty, or from a decent person trying to keep her from becoming something history would never forgive. Mitsuya gives it a name: betrayal born of good will. The villainess isn't abandoned by enemies. She's overruled by the people who love her.

The author knew some readers would hate this. He wrote that his heroine "had a reason to be defeated," and that "There's no such thing as a child who hasn't been scolded by an adult." I think he's right, and I think the book earns it. The defeat turns out to be a rescue. It doesn't take her money or her name. It takes the one thing that would have ruined her, and in Chapter 11 she sees what she was saved from and agrees.

So what is Runa, at the end of Volume 4? I think the book's answer is the one she gives the tape. A little girl on a screen asks the girl watching whether she's happy in the future. And an eleven-year-old who has just learned exactly what her happiness can cost other people wipes her eyes and says, "Yes, I'm happy. So please don't worry."

Then she drinks her tea, and she keeps it down.

- **Cassandra**: the Trojan prophetess of Greek myth who was never believed; also the title word of Volumes 2 and 3.
- **Betrayal born of good will**: Mitsuya's phrase for turning against someone out of concern for them.
