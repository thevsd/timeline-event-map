# Modern Villainess Vol. 5 — Complete Event Map (Second Edition)
### Chapter-by-chapter, with the subprime chapter read as an economics lesson, the Iraq confession, the game's downfall reconstructed, and the Koizumi–Ishihara question

**Second-edition changes (8 October 2026).** This edition replaces the first entirely. It comes from a complete, end-to-end read of the volume — every chapter, glossary, the message-board thread, the cast list and the afterword. The first edition was built on a partial read (Chapters 1, 3, 6 and 7 had only been sampled; Chapter 2 was known mostly from the Kanbe scene), and it under-read the book badly. What changed:

1. **Chapter 5 changes the meaning of Volume 4.** Runa says in her own voice that mass-casualty deterrence — nuclear strikes on Iraq, with Fallujah as the target — was the plan she was pressing on Washington, and that she broke down because she saw her own thinking in the report. The first edition called this her "nuclear reasoning"; it is a confession. The chapter also states as fact that chemical and biological weapons were used in Afghanistan in this world (the "Nagashima Doctrine"), which **corrects the Volume 3 map**: the Kandahar column in Vol. 3 Ch. 8 is this world's history, not a road not taken.
2. **Chapter 4 (subprime) is rewritten** with Angela's three layers — the loans, the plan to swallow the Japanese banks that copy her, and her government's instructions — her assumption that Runa would short the collapse, the car-loan compromise, and Ichijou's oil chart, which gives a *material* cause for Koizumi's hostility. The pharmaceutical takeover (Nakamaro chooses foreign capital; "we'll end up clashing someday"), the Kansai headquarters and Governor Iwasawa's confession are added.
3. **Chapter 2 is rewritten.** It is the longest chapter and the volume's hinge: four student-council episodes that restage the Volume 4 defeat in miniature, the kendo final she loses, the photographer and the gods, Kanbe's "forcing your services" and the Hosokawa Masamoto parallel, the price of re-entry ("be the one to bury the people you love"), and the president's "difference between a company and a nation."
4. **Chapter 3 is new to the map:** the film director, the television station, the newscaster who wants "the story of Icarus," the film and its social argument, the Academy Award, the three endings ("queen… dictator… or maybe getting married").
5. **Chapter 1 is rewritten:** the family's "trial in absentia," the terms set for her adulthood, the nationalist movement that wants her as an icon (and what Tachibana hides from her), the euro note.
6. **Chapters 6 and 7 are rewritten:** a three-way break-up of Iraq and an American debate over nuclear use; Karafuto as Japan's internal low-wage zone; K-Cards as identity documents; who the Shinjuku plotters were; the embassy's view of who controls the group; the failed honeytrap; the purchased orphans and the body double; Hotaru's choice to become human.
7. **Chapter 8 is rewritten:** the game's hidden financier, the tarot spread and its dated prophecy, the theory of fortune-telling as "a curse that sets the future in stone," Eiichi's two proposals.
8. **Chapter 9 is completed.** The tax-haven revelation and "Poor Keikain Runa" were in the first edition; the five conversations that lead to them (Suzune's timetable, the nobles' offer, "the heroine on the guillotine," the North's informants in the opposition, her father) were not.
9. **The author's afterword** names the volume's subject (anti-American sentiment) and its question ("Why did the people of that time period choose Koizumi Junichiro over Ishihara Shintaro?"), and confirms that Governor Iwasawa is Ishihara.
10. **New analysis in Part II** (twelve sections) and a rebuilt synthesis in Part III.
11. **Back-references to Volume 2** (added after that volume was reread in full): the Courtiers' first offer of a Karafuto state and the first account of the game-Runa's fall (Ch. 9); the orphan plan's origin (Ch. 7); the school committees and Ichijou's toolkit (Ch. 2). **Correction:** the Keika Opera Company is the former KDK troupe of Vol. 2 Ch. 4, not a stand-in for Takarazuka (Ch. 3).
12. **Necessary context — the missing demonstration** (added 9 October 2026, from a reader's observation). In this world no nuclear weapon has ever been used on a city: there was no Hiroshima or Nagasaki (Vol. 4 Ch. 11). New Part II §3a explains why that makes the series' focus on weapons of mass destruction coherent; the Chapter 5 line on the Manchurian War is now marked as conflicting with Volume 3; §3's and §12's judgement that the nuclear debate "strains belief" is revised.

**Coverage:** every chapter, glossary, interstitial document, the cast list and the afterword have been read in full.

**Verification note.** Live web checks were not available while most of this edition was written, so real-world statements in the chapter entries rest on the glossaries, on sources already cited in the earlier volumes' maps, and on general knowledge. A verification pass was made afterwards (8 October 2026): "Sources and verification status" at the end lists what was confirmed, with links, and what still needs checking before being relied on. Identifications marked *my identification* are inferences from the text, not statements by the author.

**Scope:** 9 chapters, all glossaries, "Keika Railway Part 57," cast list, afterword, plus analysis and synthesis. Built from the EPUB (Tofuro Futsukaichi, illus. Jaian — a new illustrator from this volume; Seven Seas English ed.).

**Coverage period:** "summer 2002 to roughly spring 2003" (afterword). Chapter 3 runs ahead to the film's release and award season; Chapter 6's news items reach the summer of 2003.

**Naming notes.** Katsuki Shiori (this volume) is "Kazuki" in parts of Vols. 3–4. Nagamori Kaori is sometimes "Kaoru." The megabank Futaki-Yodoyabashi appears once as "Futaki Shiromizu." The group's trading arm is "Keika Corp" and "Keika Corporation" interchangeably — a confusion the characters themselves joke about. The cast list still describes Okazaki as a "young employee of Keika Holdings"; in the text he runs the Moonlight Fund from Akamatsu Corporation.

**Content notes.** Chapter 5 and Chapter 6 discuss the mass killing of civilians as policy. Chapter 8 mentions, briefly and without detail, that a twelve-year-old character was sexually abused and works in a house the cast list describes as "fortune tellers/high-class prostitutes." Chapter 7 describes children "purchased" from orphanages as retainers. The map reports these as the text gives them.

**Structural note.** The volume is called a "moratorium" by its own narration and by the afterword: the protagonist "takes some time off from center stage." Five of nine chapters are told without her (1, 3, 6, 7 and most of 5's weight rests on Okazaki's speech). The chapter titles mark the design: "The Lady's Parting Gift," "The Lady's Moratorium," "The Lady Spectates," "The Lady's Supporting Roles" — and then, for the two closing chapters, the game's words: "The **Villainess** Meets the Fortune Teller," "The **Queen Bee**'s Tea Party." As she steps back from the adult world, the game's plot steps forward.

**The author's stated thesis (afterword).** "This story covers the time period when that anti-American sentiment finally erupted." The alternative to following Washington into Iraq was "a route to diplomatic independence," which Ishihara Shintarō advocated. The volume's real-world question: "Why did the people of that time period choose Koizumi Junichiro over Ishihara Shintaro?" The author's own short answer: Ishihara "became lost in the maze of literary history instead of focusing on postwar history."

# Part I — Chapter-by-Chapter Map

## Chapter 1 — The Lady's Parting Gift

**Narrative position:** Third person; late summer–autumn 2002, directly after Volume 4. Runa never appears. The chapter is the household's and the group's response to her defeat — who now holds her, on what terms, and against whom. It opens "PEOPLE ARE FOOLS." *(Read in full for the second edition; the first edition sampled it.)*

**What happens**

*The trial in absentia*
- At the Keikain main house in Shirokane, Duke Kiyomaro stages a family council that the narration calls a "farce": a "trial in absentia" of Runa, held so that the dukedom's inner circle can "blow off steam."
- **The accusers** are branch and vassal families, led by the **Kitayama clan** — descendants of the original ducal house whose line was given to Hikomaro, holding nothing higher than an earldom, long dependent on the noble immunity that is about to be abolished, and holding inheritance rights in the Keikain house. They demand that Runa's rights be placed under the main family.
- The main family's position: it is separating ownership from management and will not touch her stake.
- **Tachibana's answer** is practical, not moral: "Say you stripped her of her rights. How would you manage them? And how would you inform her ladyship of your intentions?" The main family "couldn't manage everything Runa built" — four companies: Keika Holdings, Keika Railway, Keika Corp, Keika Electronics Union.
- State of the adults: Nakamaro has resigned as director of Keika Holdings; Tachibana, "implicated in a scandal that brought Diet member Katou down," escaped arrest through immunity, has lost power, and both Imperial Railways are after his presidency.
- An Iwazaki-branch relative: "Shouldn't we just leave it all for Iwazaki to handle?" (Kiyomaro's wife is Iwazaki Ruriko; Nakamaro's is Asagiri Sakurako; the Iwazaki main family are only barons.) Tachibana: Imperial Iwazaki Bank privately "has no intention" of taking Keika Holdings, and dividing the rest among Iwazaki's pillar companies would start a war among them.
- **Kiyomaro on Washington:** the American ambassador has thanked him. The Americans "scolded her too, but they apparently don't want to take any power out of her hands" — they will use her groundwork in Iraq and look to her if the war goes badly.
- Other houses named: the **Myoujou clan** (the name taken from Fujiwara no Sadaie's Buddhist name) and families granted "Katsura" or "Kei" names. One speaker calls Runa "the young lady from Sakata" — a slur through her father Otsumaro, though she was born and raised in Tokyo.
- Nakamaro, aside: "We've grown too large. We can't even voice our true intentions anymore."
- **The settlement.** Tachibana: "We will temporarily manage my lady as a stopgap measure until she reaches adulthood. At that time, we'll take on independent directors from the Iwazaki zaibatsu to keep her in check." Iwazaki and Myoujou relatives volunteer for the seats. Kiyomaro: "I'll continue to leave the responsibility for Runa to Tachibana-kun."

*The duke's office*
- Kiyomaro admits he once sent Tachibana to Runa "as if banishing him": the old fixer's Shōwa methods would not survive in Heisei.
- **The American paper.** Nakamaro produces a US investigation: "the ghost of antiquity has been trying to possess Runa." It is a domestic political movement that wants revenge for the Pacific War — the current that "indirectly caused the Second February 26 Incident" — wants Japan to stop following America, and believes in Mackinder's land power. Its people restored Southern Karafuto after the Cold War and "went too far" in the north (the "Northern Karafuto Problem"). Runa, with Romanov blood, is being made its icon; after her defeat, Russian elements reached out.
- **Governor Iwasawa and former Policy Research Council chair Tsurui have aligned with it.**
- Tachibana will keep this from her: "If she learns of this, she won't be able to act of her own free will anymore."
- Washington and Koizumi are already using her Middle East structure. Asked who is pulling the strings behind the movement, Tachibana lays a **euro note** on the desk — France and Germany, distancing themselves from the United States.
- The chapter's ethic, in narration: "the adults stopping her now had to take the responsibility in her stead." Tachibana is named by his old title, the "Hungry Wolf of the Far East": for him "the job was only just beginning."

*Keika Railway*
- At the railway's temporary headquarters near Akihabara (¥2 trillion is committed to the Shinjuku Shinkansen), Tachibana arranges his own exit: chairman without representation rights.
- His successor is **Mikihara Kazuaki**, the "line master" — a timetable-diagram specialist from East Japan Imperial Railway who "acted like a mere worker" but can play politics. The president after him is to come from West Japan Imperial Railway. Suspects in the Shinjuku Geofront plot have been arrested.
- **Mikihara's first act:** promote **Nagamori Kaori** to Keika Hotel executive and "Keika Railway Holdings concierge." Officially this tidies the group's overlapping maid organisations after a magazine piece titled "The Bloated Keika Group" — family maids; Kitakaba Security's bodyguard-maids (who are to become Runa's "associates" in junior high); Keika Maid Services under Keika Corp; hotel concierges; Café Vesuna. In fact it is office politics, and a channel for "a constant grasp on the young lady's next moves."
- Housekeeping: the moon-and-cherry-blossom crest was first given to Keika Hotels; the Moonlight Fund is earmarked to become a subsidiary of Keika Corp.

*The governor*
- Tachibana meets **Governor Iwasawa** at Keika Hotel Shinjuku: "You have friends trying to trick my lady into taking a stance against the United States."
- Iwasawa: "I'm not senile enough to betray the young lady who made me governor and advanced the huge Shinjuku Shinkansen project." But: "If she picked a fight with the prime minister or the United States, though, I'd happily run to her aid." Japan has obeyed America since the war and bled for it; he is angry that she was made "an icon for American propaganda" — and reads in it that the war is revenge for the attacks, "not for any moral or just cause."
- Tachibana: "I'll start by removing the politicians who'll most likely get in my lady's way. You used to be on that list."
- Iwasawa: "If she were an adult… No, if she were a boy, I'd gladly have made life hell for the prime minister." A knock: his next engagement — a newspaper company's party attended by exactly those friends — has been cancelled.
- Last line: Runa "had been in a moratorium in the form of a normal school life."

**Revelations**
- Runa's stake survives the family council because nobody else can run it, not because anyone concedes her right to it.
- The terms of her adulthood are set in her absence: independent directors from Iwazaki "to keep her in check."
- Washington scolded her but wants her kept in reserve — as an asset and as cover if Iraq goes wrong.
- A named domestic movement — anti-American, land-power, heirs of the February 26 current, the people behind Karafuto's restoration — wants her as its figurehead, with Russian and (Tachibana implies) Franco-German interest behind it. Two politicians she helped, Iwasawa and Tsurui, are inside it.
- Tachibana chooses to hide this from her so that her choices stay her own.
- The group's new management starts by placing a watcher beside her.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** separation of ownership and management; *shizoku* (the former samurai class, the rank below the peerage); independent directors; "fixer" as the Japanese term for a behind-the-scenes political broker; "drawing iron for your own field" (the railway version of "drawing water to your own field" — politicians bending routes to their districts); concierge.
- **Real:** Halford Mackinder's "heartland" theory of land power (1904, 1919); the euro entered circulation as cash on 1 January 2002; France and Germany's public break with Washington over Iraq, which hardened from Chancellor Schröder's election campaign in the summer of 2002; Fujiwara no Sadaie (Teika), the poet-courtier, whose Buddhist name was Myōjō.
- **Altered / in-world:** a surviving peerage with legal immunity; the Second February 26 Incident; Karafuto's partition and reunification; the "Imperial" railways (the real JR East and JR West); a Tokyo governor — **Ishihara Shintarō, as the author's afterword to this volume confirms**; his 1989 book *The Japan That Can Say No* is the best-known statement of the position Iwasawa takes here.
- **Fictional:** the Kitayama and Myoujou clans; the American paper; Mikihara and the concierge post.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **"A job for adults" becomes an institution.** In Volume 4 it was a sentence said to her face. Here it is a governance structure — stopgap managers, a date, outside directors — negotiated by people who love her, people who resent her and people who merely want a seat. The chapter title is exact: what she "gave" on leaving is a problem none of them can carry alone.
- *Strongly supported:* **Tachibana's concealment repeats the pattern the last volume condemned.** Mitsuya named it: betrayal out of good intentions. Tachibana's reason is the best version of it — knowledge of the movement would end her free will — and the narration lets it stand without endorsing it. The reader now knows something about Runa's position that she does not; that gap drives the volume.
- *Strongly supported:* **The author's stated subject arrives on page one.** This volume's afterword names it: the eruption of anti-American sentiment. It is introduced not as a mob but as a respectable lineage — war-revenge, land power, Karafuto — with a governor, a party elder, Moscow and a euro note behind it.
- *Strongly supported:* **Iwasawa's lament is the volume's gender line.** "If she were an adult… No, if she were a boy." Her age is a temporary bar; he corrects himself to the permanent one. It answers Tachibana's "you just have to get married" (Vol. 4 Ch. 5) from the other side: the men who would follow her and the men who would retire her agree on what she is.
- *Plausible:* **The family council is the villainess genre's condemnation scene, held early and defused.** In the game she is denounced and stripped in public. Here the denunciation happens in a closed room, in her absence, as theatre managed by her guardian — and it fails on the question of who would do the work.
- *Plausible:* The slur "young lady from Sakata" shows where the old stain still lives: not in the press, which adores her, but in the house. The Sakata land deal was also the lever used against Tachibana (Vol. 4 Ch. 9).
- *Plausible:* The maid reorganisation is surveillance presented as rationalisation — the same move, at household scale, as Washington's watch on the Moonlight Fund through Angela.

**Connections:** Vol. 4 Ch. 6 (reorganisation; the exits), Ch. 9 (the raid, immunity, the three phone calls), Ch. 10 (the leaders' pact) and Ch. 12 (the loyalist faction; ultranationalists; "the Ministries"); Vol. 3 Ch. 11 (the fear of a second "imperial police") and Ch. 9 (Moscow's condition); Vol. 2 (Iwasawa's election; Tsurui's faction); Vol. 1 (Otsumaro and Sakata); this volume's Ch. 2 (her "moratorium"), Ch. 4 (Iwasawa confesses to her), Ch. 6 (the war, the Karafuto talks, who the Geofront plotters were) and Ch. 9 (the nobles' plan to use her against Koizumi; the game's Runa as a figurehead for Karafuto).

## Chapter 2 — The Lady's Moratorium

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV, 1 September to early November 2002 — the second term of her last year of elementary school. The longest chapter in the volume and its hinge: a run of school and hobby episodes, each of which replays the Volume 4 defeat in miniature, followed by the two conversations (Professor Kanbe; the American president) that tell her what the defeat meant and what the way back would cost. *(Read in full for the second edition. The first edition covered only the Kanbe scene.)*

**What happens**

*1 September — the mascot*
- Keika Electronics Union is launched at Kudanshita Keika Tower. Runa listens to the guests from a service corridor: "All they did was suck up to whoever was in office at the time"; "I thought she might go belly-up, given that Koizumi's administration couldn't stand her."
- Kiyomaro does the greeting. "My role was to stay quiet and smile as if I were the company mascot." Koizumi's "You simply aren't qualified" is quoted back: "I'd accomplished so much, but there was still no place for me." And: "maybe if I hadn't fainted when I saw those terrorist attacks…"
- Tachibana Yuka: "We're all so relieved to see your appetite has returned." Runa: "People die when they don't eat, you see…"
- Karin Viola's public speech promises profit; in private: "we'll be fighting a war of attrition for a while." The union is afloat on Shiyo Electric's profits; Furukawa Telecoms is declining; Portercon needs another acquisition. "If you can be patient three more years, my lady." Runa's order: "take over the world with cell phones." Karin's slogan: "a cell phone even a young lady can use." Narration: "customer supremacy" would spare the company from "stock price supremacy and technological supremacy."
- "I'm just taking a short break from playing grown-up."

*The student council (four episodes)*
- **The donation drive.** Runa offers to pay the whole alumni donation herself. Eiichi: "Next year's student council will suffer for it." Yuujirou: it means more if all share it — "School spirit and the feelings of camaraderie that come from it form this country's upper classes." Mitsuya: the school's old boys are a faction in Kasumigaseki who "get drunk and sing the school song." They pose for a brochure photo, write bureaucratic copy, and bow to every faction. Eiichi: "'The boughs that bear most hang lowest.' I've found out that learning to humble yourself is good experience."
- The quota came in a postscript from **Shisuka Lydia**, a junior-high class-committee leader from Karafuto's rising faction. Her verdict: "You four are an extraordinary Quartet… You'll definitely make history in this country, if not the entire world" — "both praise and a warning." Runa suspects Lydia "was taking what would've been my path in the game."
- **The budget.** The elementary council is the "sub-subcontractor" of the high-school and junior-high councils. Club spending has crept up because "a certain idiot" qualified for a national athletics meet. "Organizations existed for the purpose of spending money." Runa, privately: "I had no real plan for the future… I'd been focusing on how to survive instead."
- **The flower beds.** Asuka and Hotaru want to plant twenty derelict beds on the boundary with the junior high. The junior-high beautification committee refuses to transfer them; Mitsuya finds why — it bills contractors for maintaining beds that are bare dirt. Runa remembers the game: there, the *heroine* revived the beds and Runa, as villainess, obstructed her, using the contractors to obtain the school's rubbish "for information."
- Runa's instinct is war ("I can beat them up illegally so long as I don't get caught"). Eiichi corrects the whiteboard: "The goal is to be able to use the flower beds." Mitsuya's solution: planters on elementary land, registered as a science project, movable if challenged — a plan the corrupt committee cannot oppose without exposing itself. It works.
- Runa's "not too extreme" insurance: she passes the evidence to Lydia in the library, in Russian, facing a bookshelf, for use as mutual deterrence. Lydia: "You'll both be in trouble if it comes out. That's your weapon, right? I really hate that side of you." Runa's realisation: "If I wanted to accomplish something, I needed everyone, not just my own willpower. **It was the first time I'd recognized that since being born into this world.**"
- **The manual.** Eiichi: "We tried, but it didn't change much." Their improvements were personal skill, "hardly a reformation of the system"; next year's council will revert. The boys read national politics off it: prime ministers change yearly, bureaucrats carry the state and grow corrupt, the zaibatsu are too weak to correct either. Runa: "we should at least make a maintenance manual for our underclassmen" — spreadsheets (Mitsuya), tape-recorded minutes (Yuujirou), a year-view wall calendar she saw at the Fellowship of Constitutional Government (Runa). Eiichi: "What am I supposed to do?" — "Stamp the papers, please!"
- In passing: Honami Bank, after Keika withdrew its system, demanded a cutting-edge replacement and abandoned it under criticism.

*Gods*
- Her donations have set three Orthodox churches competing for her — the Tokyo church (cut off from Moscow since the Revolution), the far larger Karafuto church, and the Russian Orthodox Church, drawn by her Romanov blood since the debt rescue. New religions, multiplying since the bubble burst, want her as a face too. "No matter which church I joined, I'd start a fight." "My body notwithstanding, my soul was probably pure Japanese."
- "Despite everything, I felt myself seeking a god… it was almost time to make that decision for myself."
- With the photographer **Ishikawa Nobumitsu** she visits the Keika Hotel's wedding chapel, Meiji Shrine and Kawasaki Daishi. "My god is on the other side of this lens… you're a goddess to me." Television is "a god that does nothing."
- Asked what she prayed for: "No. I'm a goddess, after all. I guess I'm saying 'pardon the intrusion.'" Ishikawa: most people ask to be saved or forgiven. Runa: "So that's why those with power hold on to religion? Because they're aware of their own sins?"
- "**Even if you lose your way, I'll take lots of pictures of who you used to be so that you can make it back home, my goddess.**" She is "shocked to see what my face actually looked like in the photos."

*The kendo final*
- Regional tournament, elementary individual final: Runa against her classmate **Takahashi Akiko**, a police chief's daughter who has trained at a dojo since kindergarten.
- Runa's teacher, the bodyguard **Kitagumo Ryouko**, teaches the "Eastern" style — sport as a Cold War instrument, where "all that matters is getting a win." Runa's maids film every competitor. Her own footwork drill is "scoring a point and then running away until the time is up."
- From the stands Yuujirou explains: kendo is "a path… a way of living through the sword"; "most kendo practitioners would hate her style if they found out about it, since it's such a waste." Katsuki Shiori, "cold and empty": "She's the treasure of the Keikain family, after all." Akiko had let them film: "If you want to see everything I can do, you'll have to be there at the match."
- Akiko reads her after one exchange and takes the first point; Runa equalises with a rehearsed counter; the third point goes unnarrated. Runa loses.
- A reporter calls her "a Cleopatra of the Heisei era." Her answer: "I started practicing with a sword for self-defense, so **my priority is just to survive**. My opponent… trains strictly to win." Akiko: "Let's compete again sometime." — "Really? I don't want to. I'll just lose." Headline: *Young Lady Turns Down Rematch after Championship Defeat.*
- The press now operates as "a sort of private club" bargained with Tachibana; she courts it because "the current administration [is] watching us like a hawk."

*Games*
- She plays an online game's beta (and buys her rabbit ears when the servers fail). "At this point in history, online games were still harmonious places."
- In an Akihabara arcade with Okazaki she decides two purchases between rounds of a light-gun game: **Zugagaga Entertainment**, a console maker leaving hardware (¥50 billion; "I'll manage them out of my own pocket"), and a ¥200 billion subsidy for Russia's bankrupt **GLONASS** satellite system through the Moonlight Fund. Angela and Karin reportedly go to tell Zugagaga's staff they are *not* getting back into hardware.
- Lunch-break music: each girl names a song. The broadcast committee accidentally plays a tune from an adults-only game; nobody who recognises it says so.

*Professor Kanbe*
- Runa: "I have no desire to fight the prime minister… I can't figure out what's going on in his head at all."
- Kanbe writes the medieval bond on the board — "**Favors and services**" — and then, in red, what she has been doing: "**Forcing your services.**" The child of a great provincial house "is forcing the shogunate to purchase her services before she even comes of age."
- Which house? "You're the Hosokawa… I'm comparing you to **Hosokawa Masamoto**." Runa: "Are you saying I should start another Meio Incident?"
- "If you wanted to stage a coup, you should've done it in spring of 2002." Koizumi "has eliminated any potential puppet who could stand center stage in your place while you're still a minor."
- "A mere child would never get involved in the Iraq situation." War preparation has "helped ease the pain of the IT bubble bursting"; Takenaga and Izumikawa were "completely shocked that you predicted the war in Iraq and prepared your investments accordingly."
- The issue stated: "Should one approve of a child who hasn't even come of age holding up the enemy's head on the battlefield?" Adulthood begins at twenty; killing outside war is a crime; would she be tried as an adult? Her noble immunity is being abolished. "The prime minister isn't bullying you. If you're going to insist on special rights, then you have to shoulder that responsibility."
- **The one open path:** "Drive your father and brother into retirement and inherit the dukedom yourself… take control of the Privy Council… **Be the one to bury the people you love.** If you do all that, I think the prime minister will be willing to listen to you."
- **Two triangles.** The ruling party's is "politicians, bureaucrats, and financiers"; the opposition's — now reorganised as the **Democratic Allied Fellowship (DAF)** — is "politicians, mass media, and agitators," a product of television: "actors, and the Diet as their stage." Koizumi is "the greatest 'actor' of our generation."
- **The policy map.** Koizumi: cities, less public spending, America. His enemies the Hashizume faction: the regions, public works, continental Asia. "Keikain-kun, those are the same policies you've adopted." She touches her hair: "I could never escape the curse of the continent either."
- "Television is waiting for a showdown between you and the prime minister… the public will just consume you as a form of entertainment." Runa: "You're telling me to abandon my father and brother, all for that fiction? I'll have to refuse."
- Kanbe: "Power is a solitary thing… you can't make use of power unless you're solitary." And: "Your goal should be to find your own happiness, not this country's… A single genius isn't enough to save us… you've debuted too early."
- That night she tells Kiyomaro. He is only relieved: "Runa, have you finally had enough…?" — "I haven't had enough, but **I don't want to go against the times**." "This era was choosing Prime Minister Koizumi, not me."

*The president*
- Runa backed the Republicans in the midterms, "in part as an apology for the American IT bubble bursting." They hold the House, take the Senate and about thirty governorships.
- The president calls: "Thank you, Your Little Majesty." Runa: "I was thinking about death like some vague concept… how can you shoulder the weight of that burden?" — "That's exactly why I repent to God."
- **The in-world case for war:** the report to Congress links Iraq to terrorism through "the nation's *intention* to gain such weapons." "It was true that Iraq had tried to obtain nuclear weapons from the former Soviet Union after the Gulf War." The Denver train hijacking and the failed Tokyo attack were attempts at nuclear material; the September attacks are presented as the diversion. Runa is its "poster girl" — the girl who prevented nuclear terrorism and fainted at the news. "The adults wouldn't let me be stained with blood; it seemed they'd chosen to treat me like a beautiful doll instead."

## Chapter 3 — The Young Lady, the Television, and the Movie Director

**Narrative position:** Third person throughout, assembled from scenes and documents: two drinking sessions, a film shoot, a television station's planning meeting, a film review, two interviews, a greenroom conversation and a broadcast commentary. Winter 2002 to early 2004 in story time, running ahead of the chapters that follow. It is the volume's study of Runa as an image — who wants to capture it, sell it, expose it or bring it down. *(Read in full for the second edition; the first edition did not cover it.)*

**What happens**

*The director*
- **Shirosaki Kouji**, an internationally awarded film director, rants to his friend the idol scout: sponsors will not let him cast Keikain Runa. "Everyone's got tunnel vision over her looks and singing voice… The girl is a natural-born actress… she's spent her life acting without ever once breaking character. **She already sees this world as a stage.**"
- "She's a monster. She won't resemble anyone else, nor will anyone influence her. It's my duty — the movie industry's duty — to preserve her on the silver screen!" He will direct the planned *Imperial Bodyguards* film to get her: "What I'd hate more than anything would be someone worse than me having the chance to film her."
- Flash-forward: the film becomes "a historic work of Japanese cinema," and "the Academy Award for Best Supporting Actress would have her name on it someday."

*The shoot*
- Her part was a cameo — "sing and be protected." Shirosaki lies that the script is unfinished and invites her to film "whatever scenes you'd like," intending to rebuild the story around them.
- She hands him an airsoft gun — "Would you mind shooting me with that?" — and shoots his pellet out of the air. She can also deflect with a blade; "that idol scout there taught me."
- "Japanese movies don't have any good gunfights these days!" — regulations. "In that case, let's film in Hollywood. I'll handle the bills!"
- An overheard exchange between Angela and her superiors — the "Chief" and the CIA's **Head of Foreign Leadership Analysis**: "I'm pleased that you've taught the young lady about the dangers of violence. But I don't remember ever telling you to turn her into a comic book heroine."
- Runa on set: "Over budget?! I haven't even spent a billion yen yet!… I want to blow up a building next!"
- At the wrap party, Shirosaki to the photographer Ishikawa: "Know why they never stopped this shoot?" Ishikawa: "**At the end of the day, none of them really knew her. That's why they couldn't stop it.**" Her secretary had said: "My lady puts too much of herself into everything she does."
- Shirosaki's claim: "If I'd just ignored her, she could've turned into much more than a monster. Maybe even a dictator. But she showed me the extent of her monstrousness. Monsters are monsters because their true nature is unknown… People will eventually get a handle on her mysterious nature and counter it. Then it'll fade into this era of history… **I took her, I took this era, and I sealed them into the silver screen!**" He "had once been a monster just like her."
- Runa keeps working with him: "no one in the world can film me better than he can."

*The station*
- Planning a late-night tie-in special aboard the Keika cruise ship *Agate*, the head of programming begs again to move *Imperial Bodyguards* to prime time. Runa refuses: prime time means multiple sponsors, the big advertising agencies and loss of control. "You don't just want *Imperial Bodyguards*. You want me too, isn't that right?" She softens it by letting slip that Keika companies will sponsor the station's loss-making late-night slots.
- The station's logic is laid out: Keika's production company makes the show and buys all its advertising; production and sales therefore side with Keika; programming and the agencies want the slot.
- **The newscaster.** The anchor of the station's nightly news: "I just want to see what the young lady is hiding behind her mask… I bet viewers are eager to see a showdown between Prime Minister Koizumi and the young lady!" The ruling party is split; "the anti-prime-minister faction has no one to turn to for money except the young lady."
- Why it has not happened: "It's all because of Iraq. The U.S. is trying to cool the antagonism." "Prime Minister Koizumi and the young lady were the only two people in Japan who could object to the United States."
- His creed: "The people want entertainment, not facts and real life. What could be more entertaining than someone else's war?… **You know what people love? The story of Icarus.**… maybe we can show everyone the moment when one stumbles and loses everything! It'd be even better to stir them up and see both of them sink."
- Narration: the newspapers are questioning the "Coalition of the Willing"; television agrees not to disturb the "entertainment" of Iraq. A third career path for broadcasters is opening — the Diet.
- Forecast: "The Koizumi Theater's show was going for an extended run. **Its villain, the young lady, would stand her ground as opposition, despite being continuously attacked all the while.**"

*The film*
- A review of ***Imperial Bodyguards: The Tomboy Lady's Improv Theater***: the bodyguards discover that the girl they protect "is striking down evil in the name of restoring the world order"; they try to stop her, then fight beside her when a terrorist organisation targets her.
- Set pieces: shooting bullets out of the air; a bulletproof umbrella used as a sword; a leap from an exploding building, filmed in the United States; "Micaela's Aria." Co-stars: a top actor from "Iwasawa," a top star of the Keika Opera Company, "actors hailing from Hokkaido."
- **Its social argument.** The heroine acts under **noble immunity**, so the police cannot stop her: "Let's say what I'm doing is evil. After failing to track down all those evildoers I punished, can you really sit there and call yourselves 'just'?" The villain is a young Russian from the annexed north, whose people are called "second-class citizens": "**We have nothing! No money, no country, and no pride! So we'll steal everything this country has!**"
- A real private military company staged the firefights; US forces in Japan play her rescuers; the American president's voice appears in a phone call; the US had to raise the film's rating to stop children imitating her.

*Two interviews*
- To Japanese television, after her **Academy Award nomination**: she turned down every film offer; school comes first; she did her own stunts ("it's hard to find a child stuntwoman"); she is called "a twenty-first century Sarah Bernhardt."
- To foreign reporters, in English, every question is political — the Romanov succession ("I'm actually just a Japanese person named Keikain Runa"); Russia awaiting her return; the Moonlight Fund as a Romanov treasury (she concedes "a private Swiss bank account"); European "blue bloods" making new demands as the EU expands; Florida and post-attack publicity ("A Japanese elementary school student controlling American politics? You couldn't even get away with something so silly in a movie"); profits from Afghanistan and Iraq; her conflict with Koizumi; northern poverty. She deflects each one. "I have no plans to leave Japan right now."

*The greenroom*
- Staff change: Angela has become a director of Keika Securities; Eva, Ichijou Erika and Tachibana Yuka (her classmate from junior high) now attend her, with "a softer approach."
- Shirosaki: "What depths do you plan to sink to next?… **Most people in this world will never amount to anything**, my lady. They call themselves 'adults' and pretend that it means something."
- "Master one thing, and people call you 'great.' Master two things, and people call you 'a genius with a gift from above.' But do you know what people think when you master three things or more? **They fear you sold your soul to the devil.**" Runa "could only chuckle sadly and admit that… wasn't an unreasonable explanation."
- "People will both fear and deify you… I want to warn you to **start thinking about the end**. Monsters in movies pretty much always end up the same way. They either kill people, or they're killed by people. In special cases, a monster turns back into a human. The gimmick that does it is 'love.'… You'll rule as a queen, or the masses will overthrow you as a dictator… Or maybe getting married will turn you from a monster into a woman."
- "Does that make me Audrey Hepburn?" — "I'm sure you'd have shone in *Roman Holiday* too."
- Narration: "She would always be grateful to Director Shirosaki for hinting what her final choice should be."

*Kanbe on air*
- Japan has pledged to join Britain behind the United States; a special law for dispatching the Self-Defense Forces passed "during last autumn's extraordinary session."
- The **Russo-Japanese summit on Karafuto** should be read as "existing solely to address the issue of Iraq" — Japan mediating between Washington and Moscow.
- A scandal among nobles tied to the Foreign Ministry and the Privy Council has brought down the party's secretary-general and other members. Koizumi delegates: Izumikawa (diplomacy and security), Takenaga (the economy).
- The stock average is at "the fifteen-thousand-yen level"; mark-to-market accounting and "measures… to break up zaibatsu" are in force; Takenaga will inject public funds and nationalise if necessary.
- **Keika Holdings' listing:** it was "a kind of government-affiliated bank." Listed, it is the model exit from the bad-debt era; unlisted, it faces the new accounting — and either way it is being asked to rescue others.
- Parties: the ruling coalition; the DAF (which "might not last long"); the **Karafuto Social Democratic Party**, the former northern government's successor, supporting the cabinet from outside while Karafuto's unemployment mounts.
- The **Privy Council**: "a bastion of fifty nobles," enlarged in the 1990s, now chaired by the deputy prime minister by law, fighting the abolition of noble privilege and weakened by the scandal.
- Next item: "the failed terrorist attack on the Shinjuku Geofront."

**Revelations**
- Runa becomes a film star and an Academy Award winner; the film is made because she wanted to play.
- The CIA has a named desk watching her, and Angela reports to it.
- Her own entourage "never really knew her."
- The broadcast industry's plan for her is a public duel with Koizumi and, ideally, a fall. The narration confirms the duel comes and that she is cast as the villain and holds out.
- The United States is actively damping the Koizumi–Runa conflict until Iraq is done.
- Abroad she is discussed as a Romanov claimant.
- The narration states that Shirosaki's warning pointed to "her final choice."

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the CIA's leadership-analysis office; Japanese prime-time and late-night slots; the Hokkaido actors' bus trip refers to the Hokkaido variety show *Suiyō Dō Deshō*; "Most people in this world will never amount to anything" adapts a line from the anime *Penguindrum* (2011).
- **Real:** Japanese advertising agencies' wholesale purchase of airtime; newscaster-led evening news as a political force and broadcasters entering the Diet; the "Koizumi Theatre"; newspapers' unease over the "Coalition of the Willing"; Sarah Bernhardt; *Roman Holiday* (1953) — a princess who escapes her duties for one day and goes back; Icarus; Micaëla's aria in Act III of *Carmen*, "Je dis que rien ne m'épouvante" ("I say that nothing frightens me"), sung by a girl who admits in the next line that she is dying of fear; very young winners of the supporting-actress Academy Award exist (Tatum O'Neal at ten, Anna Paquin at eleven).

- **Plausible (my identifications):** the "Iwasawa top actor" points to the Ishihara action troupe (the real governor's brother founded it, and its police dramas were famous for explosions); the "Keika Opera Company" is the former KDK troupe that Runa took over from Kansai Dentetsu Railway in Vol. 2 Ch. 4 — on that description the OSK Nippon Revue, which Kintetsu gave up, rather than Takarazuka *(corrected)*; the star newscaster to the anchors of the commercial 10 p.m. news programmes of the day. **Uncertain:** whether Shirosaki has a single model — his method (shoot first, rewrite after), foreign prizes and taste for gunfights suggest more than one real director.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The film is Runa's Volume 4 fantasy, legalised.** Its heroine does what Koizumi forbade — "striking down evil in the name of restoring the world order" under noble immunity — and the screenplay makes the police ask whether they, who failed to catch the evildoers, can call themselves just. It is filmed in the very winter her own immunity is being abolished and the real war is being prepared, with a real military contractor and the president's voice. She cannot do it, so she is allowed to act it.
- *Strongly supported:* **Shirosaki's theory is a containment doctrine.** A monster is dangerous while unknown; show its limits on a screen and people will "get a handle on" it. He believes he has defused her by filming her — the artist's version of what Koizumi and the president did by making her a symbol (Ch. 2: "a beautiful doll"). Whether a filmed monster is a tamed one is left open: the chapter also says she will be "continuously attacked" and stand her ground.
- *Strongly supported:* **The three endings are the volume's map.** Queen, overthrown dictator, or a monster turned human by love — said as a half-joke, flagged by the narrator as the hint to "her final choice." Each already has a sponsor: Kanbe described the first (Ch. 2), the newscaster wants the second, and every guardian since Vol. 4 has proposed the third.
- *Strongly supported:* **The media are the mob the game needed.** In the game, the villainess is denounced by classmates. Here the same appetite — "the story of Icarus" — belongs to an industry, and it does not care who falls. The newscaster's "someone else's war" is the chapter's most damning line: Iraq as content.
- *Strongly supported:* **The film's villain speaks for the people her empire was built on.** Northern Japan's absorbed citizens — "no money, no country, and no pride" — are the constituency of her bank, her railway and her bloodline. A Russian-blooded heiress shoots a dispossessed Russian youth on screen. The review calls it social criticism; it is also an image her nationalist admirers and her northern clients can read in opposite ways.
- *Plausible:* **"She already sees this world as a stage" is literally true.** She has known since birth that she is in a scripted world. The director intuits the one fact about her that nobody has been told.
- *Plausible:* The choice of **Micaëla** matters. In Volume 1 the Carmen material framed Runa against the dangerous woman; the aria she is famous for belongs to the dutiful girl who walks into the smugglers' camp claiming not to be afraid.
- *Plausible:* Ishikawa's remark — nobody stopped the shoot because nobody knew her — is a quiet rebuke to the adults of Volume 4, who stopped her on Iraq and assumed they understood what she was.
- *Speculative:* Kanbe's reading of the Karafuto summit as a channel between Washington and Moscow suggests that Runa's own fate (Moscow's condition that she never rule; Washington's reserve use of her) is among the things being traded there.

**Connections:** Ch. 2 (Kanbe on television; Ishikawa; "a beautiful doll"); Vol. 4 Ch. 8 (the wide show) and Ch. 10 (Cinderella; "fall in love… and become a normal girl"); Vol. 4 Ch. 5 and Ch. 9 (immunity; its abolition); Vol. 3 Ch. 3 (a bomber on her birthday) and Ch. 4 (Angela: "you could become a monster"); Vol. 1 (the Carmen programme); this volume's Ch. 6 (the war on television), Ch. 7 (junior high; the foreign press's Romanov questions return) and Ch. 8–9.

## Chapter 4 — The Lady Spectates

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV, winter 2002–03, as she returns to work after her illness. Four business scenes — a drug-company takeover, a Kansai headquarters, Angela's subprime proposal, and a confession from the Tokyo governor. In each she is asked to approve, veto or forgive something that others have already set in motion; the title is exact. *(Read in full for the second edition.)*

**What happens**

*The pharma takeover*
- The Keika executive lunch has grown from Tachibana and Ichijou to a hall of several dozen subsidiaries. The group's five pillars: Keika Holdings, Akamatsu Corporation (to become Keika Corp), Keika Electronics Union, Keika Railway, Keika-Iwazaki Pharma. "Since I was the group's true owner my poor health had significantly influenced them all."
- Keika-Iwazaki Pharma is merging with Hatabe Pharmaceuticals (equity split 4:3:3, Keika : Iwazaki : Hatabe). Angela: a "megapharma" means to buy the merged company.
- Why: an instant Japanese sales network; the company's supply contracts with US forces in Japan and the Self-Defense Forces, both of which are "secretly stocking up on medicine" ("Medicine to combat chemical weapons?" — "I'll leave that to your imagination"); and "**the biggest reason… is you, my lady**" — since she stepped back, Wall Street judges the pharma arm could be "cut off" from the group.
- The bidders: **Arts Nova**, which wants a partnership modelled on **Eflaro**'s 50.1% "strategic partnership" with **Gainai Pharmaceuticals**, and **Charles & Earhart**, preparing a ¥1.3 trillion offer for all three companies (market value about ¥1 trillion, a 30% premium) paid in shares once the Diet legalises **stock swaps**; a hostile route through Iwazaki's and Hatabe's stakes would cost ¥600–800 billion.
- Runa offers the Moonlight Fund for a defence. **Nakamaro**: "I'm actually the one eager to take this offer up." Iwazaki's staff resent Keika's control; a foreign owner is a "common enemy" that unites the house — and it is the model for the group's future. "The Keika Group is too large for you to carry all on your own. Then again, if you're going to take on the world in battle, those companies are still too small. **The government will take action to dismantle the zaibatsu someday.**"
- "Would you lead the entire Keika Group in my place?" — "**I have plenty already, Runa. Since you don't have enough yet, we'll end up clashing someday.**… I won't get in the way as long as you don't do anything dangerous."
- He tells her Sakurako is pregnant. "Someone who hadn't been alive in the game story was going to bring forth a new life. It was like an affirmation of everything I'd done."
- Outcome: Arts Nova takes 50.1% of Keika-Iwazaki-Hatabe Pharma, which keeps its name, management and listing. The Keikain family sells half its stake for ¥200 billion, which "a doting aunt" sets aside for her unborn nephew.

*Kansai*
- Before more than a hundred managers from Akamatsu, Teisen Ishii, Teimen and Kanegana Textiles, Runa announces the purchase of **Kansai Airport Rinkai Tower** as the reorganisation headquarters of the future Keika Corp: "start with a room you aren't using, fill it with all the things that need to be organized, then work on emptying that room." The district cost ¥600 billion to develop and became bad debt; the tower is "priced at six hundred thirty billion yen" (*the figure looks like a slip — see below*).
- Okazaki fields questions: twin headquarters in Tokyo and Osaka; Kobe Airport considered and rejected (not yet built, not 24-hour); no involvement in Kansai Airport's second phase, only "indirect" relief of the region's bad debts. Toudou and Ichijou sign a cooperation memorandum on small Kansai borrowers.
- **Tenman Hashimitsu** of Teisen Ishii asks whether Akamatsu's "resource management department" — the Moonlight Fund — will leave Kudanshita, and suggests that "in terms of governance" it be defined firmly. The room looks at Runa. She smiles: "If I fired an executive for this level of meddling… I'd be no different than a dictator." Toudou: "you've found an ambitious person to succeed me."

*Subprime*
- Angela, about to head Keika Securities' North American business: "**My lady, are you familiar with subprime loans?**" It "sounded like a demon whispering in my ear." Runa pretends ignorance: "Only I was aware that those were a trap."
- The pitch: wartime-low interest rates have flooded the United States with money; cars and houses confer status; lend to non-prime borrowers at 8% with no down payment, low payments for four years and a step-up in the fifth, refinanced out of rising prices. Ichijou: "That's insanity!" — and, on pooling: "like combining a glass of wine with a glass of mud; neither will be drinkable."
- Angela's answers: blend prime and subprime; fund in yen at 0.25%; securitise with government bonds mixed in; have the agencies stamp it AAA; buy insurance on it; sell the servicing; "**let's walk away with only the commissions.**" Target: $100 billion, 1% commission, $1 billion — "no one can complain when I become CEO of Keika Holdings."
- Runa: "And you're okay with this?!" Angela: "if both parties agree on these deals, they're proper transactions, even if they're basically a grift." Ichijou: "more like a trick played while gambling."
- **The second layer.** Japanese banks, their bad debts cleared in this world, will copy Keika and overreach. Angela: "I welcome that, of course!… Keika Holdings just has to take over any financial institution that sustains a fatal blow… **a megabank that can compete with the rest of the world, just like you used to want, CEO Ichijou.**" Candidates: Honami and Gowa Osan.
- **The third layer.** "Is the U.S. government ordering you to do this?" — "The government's given me some orders… to teach financial systems in Japan a bit of Wall Street logic." The listing of Keika Holdings will open a third-party share allotment to foreign capital.
- **The misunderstanding.** Runa asks whether the bomb going off is part of her orders. Angela: "I don't believe the bomb will go before 2009… you can probably conduct yourself as an adult by that point… **What… You mean you don't want to short it?**" She had assumed the whole scheme was a gift: sell the loans, then short the collapse. "It's the rebirth of King Midas."
- **The line.** "Having come as far as I have, I've resorted to sinister means of making money. I know I've sent those whose top priority is money to hell… **This plan will cause too much damage, though. I won't be the one who pulls that trigger.**"
- Angela: "investment banks on Wall Street are already on board. The funds you burned while you were playing with fire are particularly eager. Whatever you decide, my lady, that trigger is going to be pulled."
- **The compromise.** Runa cannot abstain entirely — the fund is watched by "politicians and financial entities — as well as the CIA, which Angela worked for," and the bubble is "the plan of the United States." So: car loans only, a ¥100 billion consortium with Keika's share capped at ¥30 billion, **Japanese cars only**, cleared with the trade and foreign ministries and announced "as a means of supporting the United States."
- **What Ichijou shows her.** On the monitor: oil. WTI has reached $40 on war fears, and "a certain fund keeps buying cheap twenty-dollar" Russian crude through options taken as payment on its Russian bonds — then selling it to Europe. $5 billion in one quarter; the fund touches "20 percent of crude oil and 60 percent of Russian oil." "**Prime Minister Koizumi sees you as his enemy precisely because you've been doing this for so long, starting even before the war.**"
- Angela: "Okay, Boss. I'll join you on this train of yours." Afterwards Ichijou explains her haste: jealousy of Okazaki, "the dealer who brought you into a huge operation that turned out successful."

*The governor's confession*
- At a ceremony for books donated to the academy library by Keika's foundation, the **Choufuu Council**, Governor Iwasawa finds her alone: "'We are all worms, but I believe that I am a glowworm.'" — Churchill.
- "I had you come here so I could atone… **my people tried to deceive you and strike back against Prime Minister Koizumi.** Tachibana-san already gave them a warning, but if you were a boy, they probably would've demanded action from you. That's just how ugly the United States' actions are right now."
- He is holding a novel by a friend — "a story of politics, money, and women," over which the author was taken to court. "If only I'd had your power, this war could've been avoided." Runa: "I don't have that much power. Besides… wasn't this inevitable?"
- "If you had to bet on Prime Minister Koizumi or me, you'd choose the prime minister, wouldn't you? **What is it that the prime minister has that I don't?**" She knows the answer and only smiles. "I almost got caught up in my friend's anger just from holding his book."
- The head librarian, **Takamiya Haruka**, an old friend of his, has been listening.

**Revelations**
- Nakamaro, the heir, is choosing dilution and foreign capital over family control, expects the state to break up the zaibatsu, and tells Runa to her face that they will clash.
- A child is coming who did not exist in the game — because Nakamaro did not survive there.
- Angela works for the CIA (stated outright), carries government instructions about Japanese finance, and wants Ichijou's chair.
- Angela's loyalty is real and her ethics are not Runa's: she offered her mistress the 2008 crash as a present.
- Runa knows the crash is coming, refuses to lead it, cannot stop it — and compromises with a token position.
- In this timeline Japan's banks are healthy enough to walk into subprime; Runa's rescue of the 1990s has made the next crisis worse for Japan.
- The funds she ruined in the dot-com crash are among those building the bubble.
- Her oil trade — Russian crude bought below market and sold to Europe ahead of the war — is, by Ichijou's account, the real root of Koizumi's hostility.
- Iwasawa confirms the Ch. 1 plot to her directly; the "ghost of antiquity" is no longer wholly hidden from her.

**Historical references**
- **Real:** the wave of pharmaceutical mega-mergers from the late 1990s; **Roche's alliance with Chugai** (announced December 2001, effective October 2002: Roche took 50.1%, Chugai kept its name, management and Tokyo listing) — the model the text describes; Japan's lifting of the ban on cross-border share-swap mergers (debated through the 2000s, effective 2007); Kobe Airport (opened 2006) and Port Island; Kansai Airport's debts; the mechanics of subprime lending — teaser rates and resets, originate-to-distribute, pooling and tranching, AAA ratings, bond insurers and credit-default swaps, the yen carry trade under Japan's zero-rate policy; the Federal Reserve's cuts to 1% by mid-2003; subprime auto lending; oil's spike toward $40 a barrel in February–March 2003; Churchill's "glow-worm" remark (made to Violet Asquith in 1906, when he was a junior minister in his thirties — not, as Iwasawa says, a boy).
- **Altered:** in reality Japanese banks largely missed the subprime boom because they were still clearing bad loans — the narrator says so herself ("In reality… their hands were full"); the pharma partnership is this world's version of a deal that did not happen (the real Tanabe merged with Mitsubishi Pharma in 2007).
- **Plausible (my identifications):** **Eflaro = Roche** and **Gainai = Chugai** (the deal terms match exactly; *chū-gai* reversed gives *gai-nai*); **Arts Nova = Novartis** (the real name was coined from *novae artes*); **Charles & Earhart = Pfizer** (founded by the cousins Charles Pfizer and Charles Erhart); **Kansai Airport Rinkai Tower = the Rinku Gate Tower Building** opposite Kansai Airport, a bubble-era landmark that became a notorious bad asset; **Ayukawa Motors = Nissan** and Teia = Toyota (from earlier volumes); **Gowa Osan = UFJ** and Honami = Mizuho (earlier volumes) — the two megabanks that really were weakest in 2003–04; the settlement "net bank" resembles PayPal (*uncertain*); **the friend's novel is Mishima Yukio's *After the Banquet*** (1960), the subject of Japan's first privacy lawsuit, and "my friend's anger" is Mishima's — the writer who died in 1970 calling on the army to rise. (Iwasawa himself is Ishihara Shintarō — confirmed by this volume's afterword — and Ishihara and Mishima were friends and rivals.)
- **Text slip:** the tower's price. The real building cost roughly ¥66 billion to build; "six hundred thirty billion" against a ¥600 billion district reads as a misplaced digit (¥63 billion), in the same family as the "sixty billion" slip in Volume 4.
- **Fictional:** the Choufuu Council; Tenman Hashimitsu; the consortium.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **This is the scene the series has been walking toward since its first page.** The prologue showed 15 September 2008. Here the instrument is laid on the table by her own secretary, with the mechanism explained correctly and the date nearly right. Runa's foreknowledge, which made her rich in every earlier crisis, is for the first time a reason *not* to act.
- *Strongly supported:* **"I won't be the one who pulls that trigger" is the first use of her Volume 4 lesson.** She does not claim clean hands ("I've sent those whose top priority is money to hell"); she draws a line at harm to people who never chose the game. It is the distinction the think-tank report taught her — borrowers as "bad debt" — applied to American households.
- *Strongly supported:* **And she compromises anyway.** The car-loan consortium is a fig leaf, and she knows it ("But you'll still keep up with it"). The chapter is honest that refusal has a price she will not fully pay: the fund is watched, the bubble is American policy, "everyone had to get on board to some extent." Her virtue is real and partial.
- *Strongly supported:* **Angela is the book's most exact portrait of good-faith harm.** "You mean you don't want to short it?" is not cynicism; it is devotion expressed in the only currency she trusts. Volume 4's theme — betrayal out of good will — is here inverted: loyalty that would make her mistress the author of a catastrophe.
- *Strongly supported:* **Runa's success has changed the shape of the next disaster.** By clearing Japan's bad loans early she freed its banks to buy what, in our history, they were too weak to touch. The alternate history is not a rescue but a displacement of risk — a point an economist would insist on, and the novel makes it itself.
- *Strongly supported:* **Ichijou's oil chart corrects the Volume 4 story.** The quarrel with Koizumi was presented as moral — a child and a war. Ichijou says it is also material: she has been making billions from the approach of that war, selling Russian oil to the Europeans who oppose it. Read with Tachibana's euro note (Ch. 1), her fund is financially aligned with exactly the camp Washington distrusts.
- *Strongly supported:* **Nakamaro states the succession problem without malice.** "I have plenty… you don't have enough yet" is a diagnosis of appetite, and "we'll end up clashing someday" is the family's version of Kanbe's Hosokawa warning (Ch. 2). His child raises the stakes: there is now a blood heir whose existence Runa made possible.
- *Plausible:* **The governor with the book is the chapter's literary key.** If the novel is *After the Banquet*, the "friend's anger" is that of a writer who ended by demanding a military restoration — the lineage Ch. 1 called "the ghost of antiquity." In this world that anger had consequences: Volume 3's prologue says the Second February 26 Incident "was kicked off when that author talked about 'the fulfillment of the Showa Restoration'" (identified in that map as this world's Mishima), with "zaibatsu dissolution" and "breaking down the privileged classes" as its slogans — and Ch. 8 of this volume says the Keikains were among its targets. The governor is confessing to the granddaughter of a man his friend's followers tried to kill. Iwasawa confesses that the lineage tried to use her, and in the same breath asks why she prefers Koizumi. Her unspoken answer is left for the reader. *(Speculative: what Koizumi has is the willingness to be hated — to act and take the blame — where Iwasawa wants to be right. The text does not say.)* Volume 4's last chapter was titled "An Unappealing Banquet."
- *Plausible:* **The afterword backs the literary reading.** The author says he felt Ishihara "became lost in the maze of literary history instead of focusing on postwar history" — which is this scene: a governor holding a dead friend's novel, asking why the country prefers the other man.
- *Plausible:* **The tower she buys overlooks the place where the game left her.** The original Runa's last image is at Kansai Airport (Vol. 3 Ch. 10; this volume's Ch. 9 suggests she never left it alive). The present Runa buys the bankrupt skyscraper beside that airport as a room "to fill… with all the things that need to be organized."
- *Plausible:* Tenman's "governance" question is the first time a salaried manager has pressed publicly on the Moonlight Fund's place in the structure. The group is becoming a normal company, which means her private treasury is becoming a problem.

**Connections:** Vol. 1 Prologue (15 September 2008); Vol. 4 Ch. 12 (the last line; Angela vs Okazaki; "the Ministries") and Ch. 11 ("bad debt" of people; "other people here had wills of their own"); Vol. 4 Ch. 5 (the Hatabe merger: "in the original game, Keika Pharma ended up losing its power due to a poor merger"); Vol. 3 Ch. 6 (the nightmare of Nakamaro's death and the devil's bargain) and Ch. 12 (the wedding gift; "eat before I get eaten"); Vol. 2 (the megabank mergers; Russian bonds); this volume's Ch. 1 (Iwasawa; the euro note), Ch. 2 (Kanbe: war spending and the IT bust) and Ch. 7 (an American report on how the group is now governed).

## Chapter 5 — March 20

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV, 20 March 2003, in the vault beneath Kudanshita Keika Tower; one scene with Okazaki as the Iraq War begins. Two pages that rewrite the meaning of Volume 4: here she states, in her own voice, what her plan for Iraq actually was. *(Re-read in full for the second edition; several first-edition and Volume 3 map statements are corrected by it — see "Revelations.")*

**What happens**
- The Moonlight Fund's core treasury — over ¥10 trillion in securities, bullion, crated cash, gifted jewels and art. In its front room sits a single hundred million yen: "the money I'd once stared at in the manor, when I began the bad debt disposal that led to where I was today. I kept the money there… so that I'd never forget those times."
- Okazaki: "It's started. The war in Iraq." — "Very well. The winner's already been decided, after all." Others are positioning for the aftermath; "I didn't join them."
- "I hope it doesn't turn out like Vietnam." — "Why do you think the U.S. lost in Vietnam?" Okazaki: "**They confused their methods with their objective.**" If the fear was communist dominoes, they should have fought all-out in the North.
- His counter-example: "The eastern general secretary was clearly smarter… **In the Manchurian war, his country mercilessly nuked both friend and foe and stopped us.**… his country was victorious in the Second World War because they sacrificed tens of millions of lives." Runa: that country "no longer existed."
- Okazaki, on what he has worked out: "You've been trying to focus on that point, and now you're here with your wings clipped because you were thinking ahead." She built the Gulf bases "because you predicted this future… But you did it while looking at the proxy war in India and Pakistan, hundreds of thousands of people dying… **You're a monster for thinking up the plan you did.**"
- "You broke down after that. If human lives really meant nothing to you, you wouldn't have felt all that weight. You didn't just break because you were disgusted, did you? **You broke because you realized you were capable of something so disgusting.**"
- **Her reasoning, stated.** The war "wasn't truly caused by religious or economic issues. It was a conflict over the going rate for a human life" — "a mere variation on the Cold War's mutually assured destruction doctrine." Dragged into asymmetric war, America need only "forcibly make it a conventional war. That was possible in this world."
- **What has already happened in this world.** "Now the '**Nagashima Doctrine**' — the act of bombing areas to destroy guerilla fighters alongside other local inhabitants — had formed, and Afghanistan had been reduced to an irreparable scene of genocide." Pakistan is deterred by fear of India, to whom Washington signals it will not stand in the way. "Afghanistan's war of annihilation, in which chemical and biological weapons had been used and Indian mercenary soldiers sent in, was starting to function as a tool of fear."
- **What she wanted.** "What America was trying to do — **what I'd tried to do by displaying my political strength to the neocons** — was simple. They wanted to show the world their revenge." Nuclear or equivalent strikes; "a genocide of millions of Afghans and Iraqis"; an exchange rate of "millions of people to a few terrorists" whose terror would suppress any further attack. Machiavelli: "it is better to be feared than loved."
- "**That was why I thought I could avoid Iraq's future quagmire through the sacrifice of those millions of lives. And it nearly broke me.** When I saw the report about actions being taken in Washington, I realized that my thoughts were actually being implemented in real life."
- The sentence she heard herself saying: "'**You people haven't done anything to me, but I want millions of you to die so that I can build a better future with my country.**' When I realized that was essentially what I was saying, I fell apart. Prime Minister Koizumi saw that I was being a reckless child, scolded me, and forced me out."
- The target: not Baghdad, which must be governed afterwards, but "the center of the Sunni Triangle, **Fallujah**."
- "I'd predicted that far ahead, put that much thought into it, and been stopped just as I was about to move. I wasn't sure whether that was good or bad."
- Runa: "I don't actually think they'll use nukes, at least." Okazaki: "That's why Iraq might turn into another Vietnam. People sure are fools."
- "Are you satisfied with this option for the future?" — "I am. I get to watch your chosen future from the front row, and **now I know you're not an omnipotent, omniscient god.**"
- "I was a child, and so the adults in my life made me step back to avoid that judgment. For that, I was grateful. But on the inside, I lamented those adults' stupid actions. **This world is so foolish and so kind.**"
- "The Iraq War ended within a month, as I'd predicted, and the seemingly endless postwar period was on the horizon. **The truth that I'd tried to be involved in that war no longer existed.**"

**Revelations**
- **The exterminationist logic of the Volume 4 report was hers first.** In Vol. 4 Ch. 11 she read a think tank's paper and recognised "the optimal solution" in a dream. Here she says plainly that mass-casualty deterrence was the plan she was pressing on Washington's hard-liners, as the way to spare America (and Japan) the quagmire she remembered. The report sickened her because it was her own thought, implemented.
- **Koizumi stopped more than a child's war profiteering.** By her own account he stopped a child advocating the nuclear destruction of a city.
- **The Afghan atrocity is canon.** Chemical and biological weapons, area annihilation and Indian mercenaries in Afghanistan are stated here as fact by the narrator. *This corrects the Volume 3 map (third edition), which treated the "Kandahar Massacre" column in Vol. 3 Ch. 8 as "almost certainly a road not taken."* It is this world's history, and her military company is inside it (Vol. 4 Ch. 3).
- She still half-believes she was right: without the terror, Iraq becomes Vietnam. The chapter does not resolve it.
- Okazaki deduced all of this unaided, and his loyalty rests on her having broken.
- Okazaki says the Soviet leader "mercilessly nuked both friend and foe and stopped us" in the Manchurian War. Read literally, that is battlefield use against a force that included Japan. **It conflicts with Vol. 3 Ch. 3**, where the war ends after the Soviets "held a successful nuclear test" and everyone backs off to avoid "an all-out nuclear war." It may be a translation or author slip, or loose talk about the test used as a threat. Either way, no nuclear weapon has ever been used on a city in this world (Vol. 4 Ch. 11; see Part II §3a).
- Her part in the war's preparation has been erased from the record.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary (which sits at the end of this chapter but annotates Chapter 4):** Charles & Earhart is the "megapharmacy that made ED drugs" — confirming the Pfizer reading; Arts Nova is a top European pharma; the "insurance" is the credit-default swap, with AIG's fate cited; "nearly two trillion dollars' worth of subprime loans" at Lehman's fall, about 16% non-performing; Angela's 2009 is the year General Motors failed — she expected car loans to break first, whereas in reality mortgages did and "GM caught fire from that blaze"; King Midas.
- **Real:** the invasion of Iraq began on 20 March 2003 (Baghdad time); Baghdad fell on 9 April and major combat was declared over on 1 May; Fallujah, in the "Sunni Triangle," became the centre of the insurgency and the site of the war's two heaviest urban battles in 2004; Machiavelli, *The Prince*, ch. 17; mutually assured destruction; the domino theory.
- **Altered / in-world:** no atomic bombing of Japan in 1945 (Vol. 4 Ch. 11), so no city has ever been destroyed by a nuclear weapon; a Soviet nuclear test — or, by Okazaki's account, battlefield use — at the end of the Manchurian War (the two volumes disagree); an Afghan war of annihilation; a Washington in which nuclear use against Iraq is a live option (Ch. 6).
- **Plausible (my identification):** the **"Nagashima Doctrine"** is named for Oda Nobunaga's destruction of the Ikkō-ikki at Nagashima in 1574, where a besieged religious league — fighters and families, some twenty thousand people — was fenced in and burned. It is the standard Japanese example of annihilating armed believers together with their community; the novel uses it as the name for doing the same from the air. The "eastern general secretary" is Stalin.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **This is the confession the series withheld for a volume and a half.** Volume 4 let the reader believe Runa was an over-reaching logistician whom adults removed for her own good, and that the monstrous arithmetic belonged to others. Chapter 5 removes the alibi. The "villainess" of the title is, for these two pages, meant literally.
- *Strongly supported:* **Her foreknowledge is what made her dangerous.** She knew the real Iraq War became an eight-year quagmire. A person who knows the cost of the mild path and holds a utilitarian's ledger will reach for the terrible one. The series' premise — a woman who has seen the future — is here shown to lead, by honest steps, to advocating atrocity.
- *Strongly supported:* **"Methods and objective" is the chapter's key, and it cuts at her.** Okazaki's diagnosis of Vietnam is also the diagnosis of Runa that the flower-bed episode gave in miniature (Ch. 2: she wrote "recover the rights" when the goal was flowers). Her objective was to spare her country; her method became millions of dead strangers.
- *Strongly supported:* **"Foolish and kind" is her verdict and the book's.** The adults' decision is foolish, in her reckoning, because it buys a worse war; kind, because it kept her from being its author. She holds both. The novel refuses to say which weighs more — and the next chapter immediately shows an Iraq that is breaking apart.
- *Strongly supported:* **Okazaki's loyalty is to a fallible person.** Angela wants a Midas (Ch. 4); the nationalists want an icon (Ch. 1); the director wants a monster (Ch. 3). Okazaki alone is "satisfied" precisely because she turned out not to be a god. It is the healthiest relationship in the volume, and it belongs to the man the others want removed.
- *Plausible:* **The hundred million yen is a relic.** She keeps the first money she ever stared at — from the Volume 1 bank rescue — as a reminder of scale: the last time her decisions were about a sum she could see. The scene sets it against a war.
- *Plausible:* **The Nobunaga reference is double-edged.** Nagashima is remembered both as the ruthless act that ended a decade of religious insurrection and as a massacre. Kanbe has just compared Runa to a warlord-era kingmaker (Ch. 2); here the implied comparison is to the deed for which Nobunaga is called "the Demon King."
- *Speculative:* "The truth… no longer existed" may be literal as well as rueful: the records are sealed (Vol. 3 Ch. 8 says so), which is the kind of buried fact a downfall plot digs up.

**Connections:** Vol. 4 Ch. 3 (her briefing to the White House; massacres under her company's licence), Ch. 5 (Salt Lake City: "invade in spring 2003 with a Gulf War-sized army"), Ch. 9 (Koizumi), Ch. 11 (the report; the dream; the fast); Vol. 3 Ch. 8 (the Afghanistan column — now confirmed), Ch. 9 (Eva: "We want to, but we have nothing to fire at"; Japan in the "Manchurian, Vietnam and Gulf wars") and Ch. 2 (the downfall sentence); Vol. 1 (the manor's hundred million yen); this volume's Ch. 2 (the president's "tens of thousands of lives… to save a single American"; "why did I die?"), Ch. 3 ("monster") and Ch. 6 (what the war becomes).

## Chapter 6 — Various News Excerpts

**Narrative position:** A document chapter: eleven unattributed news items and a long magazine feature, spanning autumn 2002 to spring–summer 2003. No Runa. It supplies the public record of everything the surrounding chapters show from inside — the economy, the war, the plot against Shinjuku, and how the press now describes her group. *(Read in full for the second edition; the first edition sampled it.)*

**What happens — the news items**
1. **Restructuring.** Unemployment is "roughly 4 percent." Matsuyuki Electronics cuts 6,000 by early retirement; Shibaura Electric 10,000; Somy 10,000; the former Furukawa Telecoms 8,000. Keika Electronics Union draws attention by "temporarily refraining" from layoffs.
2. **Karafuto as the cheap interior.** Since the 1995 yen shock (¥80 to the dollar), firms that would have gone abroad build in Karafuto, where labour costs 20% less. Karafuto's unemployment is still above 20%; "no solution to the issues of migrant workers and second-class citizens has emerged."
3. **Mark-to-market accounting** is to be legislated; the banks object; the Minister of Financial Reconstruction promises public-fund injections.
4. **The zaibatsu dissolve themselves.** To avoid the new accounting and public money, groups sell affiliates' shares. Futaki-Yodoyabashi Bank's holdings were found to breach the Antimonopoly Act; Honami is "still struggling with bad debts" and has lost trust "after this spring's system failure"; **Iwazaki "has decided to dissolve its parent company."** The selling has pushed the Nikkei into "fifteen-thousand-yen territory." Some hope to keep "business groups that loosely connect" the old companies.
5. **Geneva.** A Russo-Japanese summit on Karafuto, with the US Secretary of State present: talks continue; more Siberian oil and gas for Japan; the venue was really about lining up opinion on Iraq — "Japan, Russia, and Great Britain have joined with the United States" to persuade a reluctant EU.
6. **Congress.** The president seeks authority for an Iraq offensive "that included authorization for weapons of mass destruction," revealing that "the U.S. planned to use WMDs, including nuclear weapons, against Iraq." The inquiry's account of 2001: the hijacked planes, the anthrax, and "a shooting spree… at India's parliamentary building" were "diversionary measures" for the real attack — hijacking a train in Denver "and loading it with nuclear materials," with a detonator being built in Akihabara. Iraq financed the terrorists and tried to obtain a Soviet nuclear missile. A general, anonymously: "the crux of the issue isn't Iraq's possession of weapons of mass destruction, but their intentions… This is essentially revenge… **the Iraq War has to become a symbol through asymmetric war.**"
7. **Iraq breaks in three.** Baghdad has fallen and the leadership has fled. The Sunni centre is occupied by multinational forces and slipping out of control; "the United States' plan to dismantle fighting power early… is steadily becoming a fantasy." **The Kurds have declared independence** (Turkey is under martial law). **Iran has moved into the Shia south** "to protect inhabitants," and American and Iranian forces now face each other. Washington "had no intentions of destroying Iraq itself as a nation."
8. **K-Cards.** Keika's new IC card merges the cards of Keika Holdings and Teisei Department Stores with East Japan Imperial Railway's transit card, and serves as employee ID "as well as personal identification for residents of Tokyo, Hokkaido, Chishima Prefecture, and Karafuto" — giving undocumented Karafuto labourers "a personal ID and bank account." "Some express fear that their personal information will become free for the Keika Group to use."
9. **The Shinjuku Geofront plot.** Planned after 11 September; prevented, "unlike the attacks in London and Spain." Investigators found "an underground group of organizations" — "radical leftists, environmentalists, religious extremists, and crime syndicates, with **participants in the Second February 26 Incident and former Northern Japanese military members** named as those slated to carry out the attack." The target was the underground city being built around the Shinjuku Shinkansen. "Anonymous tips from within the group" led to the arrests.
10. **Consumption recovers** on the "three sacred treasures" — DVD players, digital cameras, mobile phones. Factories go up in Karafuto, lowering unemployment there and raising it on the mainland. The former Shiyo Electric bets further on small liquid-crystal panels; Keika Electronics Union plans a large Karafuto plant.
11. **The Diet.** The budget is through; talk turns to dissolution. "As events in Iraq continue to defy the United States' expectations," the opposition demands the Self-Defense Forces' withdrawal; ruling-party members fear losing office if the forces take casualties.
12. **Public funds.** The cabinet approves injections "into all existing megabanks"; mark-to-market, "finally introduced this spring," has exposed fresh bad debts; unstable banks may be nationalised. Keika Holdings, Imperial Iwazaki and "Futaki Shiromizu Bank" will repay immediately.

**What happens — "Zaibatsu in Crisis, Part Three! The Bloated Keika Group!"** *(Economics Monthly; Parts One and Two were "Iwazaki's Obsessive Betting on Karafuto" and "Futaki-Yodoyabashi's Thoughts of Marriage and Dissolution")*
- **Pharma:** the group's "originator" has "essentially sold itself" to Arts Nova. History recalled: the Finance Ministry made Far Eastern Bank its model case; Keika Chemicals, Shipping and Storage were merged into Iwazaki companies. Its small size inside the new group "has become a source of shame"; some family members want to place relatives in the newer companies.
- **Holdings:** "Japan's very first megabank," made by "a monstrous level of income through the Moonlight Fund." No internal successor to Ichijou has emerged from the rescued banks or the ex-Finance Ministry recruits; **Angela Sullivan** — formerly a fund manager at the Pacific Global Investment Fund, headhunted before she could join Silver Woman Securities — is tipped.
- **Railway: "A Time Bomb for the Keika Group?"** Investments exceed ¥3 trillion (Shin-Tokiwa Railway, the Shinjuku Shinkansen, the Naniwasuji line); the department-store and resort land was written down under mark-to-market and would need writing down again if land falls. "That is a major reason the government insists on Keika Holdings going public." On Tachibana: "the only person who can handle the shady figures who come with land development"; the government "reportedly told" the special prosecutors "to set their sights on that man"; "many feel that the Koizumi administration made an example of him."
- **Keika Corp:** "crying out in agony" over a four-way merger. The question of moving the "resource management department" — the Moonlight Fund — under the merged company's control "caused tension with Keika Holdings"; Ichijou and Toudou have agreed to leave it. An insider: with Tachibana targeted, "the only person who can take charge of the bloated Keika Group now is Toudou-san."
- **Electronics:** Karin Viola's three-year moratorium on layoffs "can be interpreted as a declaration that major restructuring will occur three years from now"; Furukawa and Portercon had planned to shed 8,000 and 8,500.
- **"Moonlight Fund: The Keika Group's Inner Sanctuary?"** A few dozen staff in Kudanshita; assets rumoured at over $100 billion; "the Keika Group's effective leader." Owned by Runa; rumoured to be "a front for the Russian Romanov family"; "those involved with the Romanovs repeatedly demand those funds be returned," and Moscow's wariness is "becoming a diplomatic issue." It uses "untraceable private Swiss bank accounts," and the group's official position is that it has "no public relationship" with it. Only four people are said to know its scale: Ichijou, Tachibana, Toudou and Angela. "**Rumors that the group's present executives set up young Lady Keikain Runa as their puppet, removing her from the Keikain family's control, are being enthusiastically spread by Keikain family members.**" And: "the Koizumi administration is targeting the Keika Group over this Moonlight Fund."

**Revelations**
- Washington sought, and discussed in the open, authority to use nuclear weapons against Iraq. Runa's Chapter 5 reasoning was not a private fantasy; it was one side of a public American debate.
- The war is won in a month and the peace fails at once — with Kurdish secession and an Iranian occupation, an outcome *worse* than the real one.
- Terror attacks in London and Spain have already happened in this world, earlier than in ours.
- The Shinjuku plot joined the domestic far left, cults, gangsters, **veterans of the Second February 26 Incident** and ex-Northern soldiers — and was betrayed from inside.
- Karafuto is Japan's internal low-wage zone, and Keika now issues the identity documents of its migrant workers.
- The old zaibatsu are dissolving themselves under Takenaga's accounting; Iwazaki is winding up its holding company.
- The press says openly that the prosecutors went after Tachibana on the government's instruction, and that the government's target is the Moonlight Fund.
- Members of her own family are briefing the press that Runa is her managers' puppet.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the congressional-approval material draws on Aida Hirotsugu's book on Congress and the Gulf War and Yoshizaki Tatsuhiko's *America no ronri* (2003), with the "Nayirah testimony" in the background; "in reality, the London attacks took place in 2005, the Spain attacks in 2004. In this world, the state of the Iraq War results in numerous terrorist acts of revenge"; the digital boom's products were really made in China and Southeast Asia and "didn't boost Japanese employment rates much."
- **Real:** the electronics industry's mass layoffs of 2001–03 (Matsushita, Toshiba, Fujitsu, Sony among them); the yen's peak near ¥80 in April 1995; the "three sacred treasures" of the digital age, a 2003 catchphrase; mark-to-market accounting and the unwinding of cross-shareholdings; the attack on India's parliament on 13 December 2001; Congress's Iraq war resolution of October 2002; the fall of Baghdad in April 2003; Iraqi Kurdistan's autonomy and Turkey's fear of it; Iranian influence in the Shia south; IC transit cards (JR East's Suica, 2001); public-fund injections and the nationalisation of weak banks in 2003; the "Nayirah" testimony of 1990 — a false atrocity story that helped win Congress's vote for the Gulf War.
- **Altered:** the authorisation covers nuclear use (the real resolution did not); the Kurds declare independence and Iran sends troops (neither happened); unemployment at 4% (really about 5.5%) and a Nikkei at 15,000 (really near 8,000) — this world's economy is visibly healthier; public money goes to *all* megabanks (in reality the 2003 rescue centred on Resona, with Ashikaga nationalised that November); the electronics layoffs are smaller than the real ones; an Iwazaki holding company exists to be dissolved (the real zaibatsu holding companies were abolished in 1946–47).
- **Plausible (my identifications):** Matsuyuki = Matsushita, Shibaura = Toshiba, Somy = Sony, Furukawa Telecoms = Fujitsu, Shiyo = Sanyo, Teisei and Sougou = Seibu and Sogo (earlier volumes); "**Futaki Shiromizu**" is the same bank as Futaki-Yodoyabashi under another disguise — *Shiromizu* is an alternative reading of *Hakusui*, the name of the Sumitomo group's presidents' club (a naming inconsistency in the text); Chishima Prefecture is the Kuril Islands.
- **Fictional:** the Shinjuku plot; K-Cards as civil identity; the magazine.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The chapter is the answer to Chapter 5's open question, and it is not comforting.** Runa argued that only terror would prevent a quagmire; the adults chose the humane war. The news shows that war producing a broken Iraq, an Iranian army in the south, attacks in Europe and a plot against Tokyo. The author does not thereby vindicate her — nothing shows the nuclear option would have worked — but he denies the reader the easy conclusion that being stopped made things turn out well.
- *Strongly supported:* **The "symbol" general is the public face of her private logic.** "An example to anyone thinking of attacking us again" is the exchange-rate argument of Vol. 4 Ch. 11 and Ch. 5, stated as doctrine. The glossary's nod to the Nayirah testimony marks the author's view of such cases: wars are sold with stories.
- *Strongly supported:* **Karafuto is the series' substitute for China.** The real hollowing-out sent Japanese factories to the continent; here they go to a reunified, impoverished north whose people are "second-class citizens." It keeps the jobs inside the border and creates an internal colony — the social base of the film's villain (Ch. 3), of the Karafuto party, and of the ex-Northern soldiers in the Shinjuku plot.
- *Strongly supported:* **The K-Card is the most quietly alarming thing in the volume.** A private conglomerate becomes the issuer of identity and bank accounts for a stateless-in-practice labour force, with "precise statistics" as the stated benefit. Karin Viola called the group's data centres "gold mines" (Vol. 4 Ch. 6). This is the infrastructure of a state, held by a twelve-year-old's company — and a ready-made charge sheet.
- *Strongly supported:* **The magazine lists every fault line the downfall could run along:** a railway whose land could turn bad; a bank being pushed to list; a fund with no legal relationship to the group that it effectively leads; a guardian marked by prosecutors; a family leaking against its own daughter; Romanov claimants; a hostile prime minister. Nothing in it is false.
- *Plausible:* **The Shinjuku network is Chapter 1's "ghost of antiquity" with its mask off.** Veterans of the Second February 26 Incident appear among the bombers of the project Runa and Iwasawa built. The respectable nationalists who wanted her as an icon and the men who meant to blow up her railway share a genealogy. The "anonymous tips from within" are unexplained. *(Speculative: Tachibana's hand, given his promise in Ch. 1 to start "removing" those in her way.)*
- *Plausible:* Part One of the magazine series — "Iwazaki's Obsessive Betting on Karafuto" — hints that the Iwazaki group, the Keikains' in-laws and the nationalists' preferred heir to Keika (Vol. 4 Ch. 12), is itself over-extended in the north.

**Connections:** Ch. 1 (the magazine article that prompted the maid reorganisation; the movement; the Geofront arrests), Ch. 2 (the congressional report), Ch. 3 (Kanbe's broadcast; the film's "second-class citizens"), Ch. 4 (the pharma sale; Tenman's question; public funds and megabanks), Ch. 5 (her logic; "ended within a month"); Vol. 4 Ch. 6 (reorganisation; data centres), Ch. 9 (the raid on Tachibana) and Ch. 10 (the timetable to war; the Karafuto talks); Vol. 3 Ch. 6–7 (the nuclear plot) and Ch. 11 (the sweep; a second "imperial police"); Vol. 2 (the megabank mergers; mark-to-market).

## Chapter 7 — The Lady's Supporting Roles

**Narrative position:** Third person, three vignettes and an appended document, late 2002 to spring 2003 (the last part after Runa has entered junior high). Runa appears for one burst of laughter. The chapter builds the cast that will surround her from now on — and shows whose payroll each of them is on. *(Read in full for the second edition; the first edition sampled it.)*

**What happens**

*Operation Vorpal Bunny*
- A windowless room in the US embassy: the ambassador, the CIA's East Asia chief, an analyst, **Eva Charon** and **Yulia Molotova**. Eva is there because Angela has moved to Keika Holdings — a promotion the agency "both welcomed… and was troubled by."
- The American assessment: the Keika Group "expanded rapidly through acquisitions, and their inner workings haven't stabilized… We don't know who they have on the inside or who's infiltrated them." **Tachibana "has real control"** and is assembling its inner structure "under the surveillance of the U.S. and Russia… to be more accurate, he's trying to protect Keikain Runa." He "will pick Keikain Runa over the Keika Group."

Tachibana, Ichijou and Toudou are ruled out (families; and a failure "would enrage the young lady"); Karin Viola will bring Americans in anyway. **Target: Okazaki Yuuichi** — "the man who led the young lady into actions that greatly harmed our country, and a key person who can't be removed in the face of our wars… He even knew that her reckless behavior was in preparation for a war in Iraq."

The Americans are careful: they remember that Runa and Okazaki "worked together to crash the American stock market and make off with tens of billions of dollars."
- It collapses. The hired gangsters try to bill two clients for the same job; Chinatown finds out; the brawl draws a patrol car instead of a rescuer. At Kagachō police station sit Yulia, Irina, a laughing Ryuu Suzune, a grinning Okazaki — and Eva with **Anisha Egorova** (Runa's maid, here on the Russian side), summoned by **Maefuji** "from the foreign affairs section… the man in charge of a certain young lady": "It's your job, and I can't tell you to walk away from it, but could you please try to hide it a little better?"

It's Yulia-chan and Irina-chan, right? Now that this has happened, you won't betray me, will you?"
- Runa hears the cover story and howls: "Get to Okazaki with a honeytrap?!… That's so stupid!" "That was as far as she treaded into the issue."
- Yulia, Irina and Ryuu Suzune become Runa's **associates**. Suzune's own orders — "to win Okazaki over ever since he got famous in Hong Kong. That was in 2000" — had already succeeded: "I mastered the chicken recipe he tried in Macau."

*The train to Yūbari*
- A commentator-professor's frame: "One reason poverty was so frightening was that it removed someone's ability to see their options." Rescuing people before they fall is cheaper but breeds resentment; those raised in poverty "might not even understand that they're impoverished at all."
- **Yūbari**, the debt-laden Hokkaido coal town, has had its debts taken on by Keika and by Iwazaki (whose mine it was). It is being resettled with people from Karafuto — the former northern elite with their assets, ex-soldiers "paid for shedding blood," refugees. Keika negotiated for arrivals to receive **family registers** there. Works: melons sold exclusively through Teisei; methane-fired power and incinerators; a garbage train from Sapporo; a dam; a **Kitakaba Security training facility** for 3,000 workers and dependants. The mines may reopen as a training site for Karafuto's dangerous, ultra-low-wage pits.
- On a local train, five girls in Imperial Gakushuukan uniforms meet: **Kushunnai Nanami**, **Enbuchi Yuna**, **Nozuki Misaki**, **Rudaka Miu**, **Akibe Riko**. They are orphans from Hokkaido and Karafuto; Kitakaba Security is "the company that had purchased them." "If we do our jobs right, everyone in the orphanage will get to eat delicious meals."
- Roles: Nanami is leader — "I was picked because I looked the most like her out of all the candidates… if push comes to shove, you'll have to impersonate my lady"; Yuna, guard; Misaki, adviser; Miu and Riko, maids. A sixth, **Glasya Marsheva**, misses the stop and is found at Tomakomai.
- Why they exist: "Keikain Runa had decided to take in orphans from Hokkaido and Karafuto because **she realized she had no faction of her own**, despite being adopted into the main Keikain family. Tachibana Ryuuji and Toudou Nagayoshi had really bent over backward to put together this group."
- They will live in Tokyo, be registered in Yūbari, and "conduct themselves as Japanese citizens from Hokkaido." Irina and Yulia, who plainly dislike each other, and Ryuu Suzune meet them at the station.

*Hotaru*
- **Kaihouin Hotaru**'s day, now in junior high: meditation at dawn in a Den-en-chōfu flat (family in Nara); an attendant who has only recently begun to perceive her; school with Asuka, who does the talking.
- The class cleans its own room. The librarian **Takamiya Haruka** insisted: "Keeping things orderly is part of a good education." Runa leads it: "CEO Ichijou at our company still cleans the executive suite toilet himself. I've learned you can't hold a position like that unless you can clean that way." Angela and Karin Viola, "from Western society with its strict class system," fought Ichijou over it.
- Only four people can see Hotaru when she hides: an occult-club senior, Asuka, Kanna Mizuki, and Takamiya (inside the library).

Her power has been "dwindling" — "Asuka's father was struggling in the election, and Keikain Runa was stuck in a political dilemma. If she were a real zashiki-warashi, those things probably never would have happened." She does not understand "the raging current known as 'the times' that was washing it away."

- Takamiya, "the witch of the library," lends her a book and tells her: "I know what you'll become, but the only thing you can ever be is 'you.'" Hotaru reads it "over and over, her tears never ceasing." "The witch had broken the curse."
- Next morning: "**Good morning, Asuka-chan, Runa-chan.**" — "Huh…?" — "For real…?" "Her life as a human had only just begun."

*Interstitial document — "[Let's Celebrate] Keika Railway Part 57 [Etchujima Branch Line Now Open]"*

The network now: KYOSHO Rapid Railway, the new Etchūjima line, the Kagawa Railroad group with the **Shikoku Shinkansen** (Shin-Sakaide–Okayama–Himeji–Shin-Kobe–Shin-Osaka), the Kuzuryū River line, night trains. The chapter asks which of them get to rewrite the part. Okazaki does (from target to creditor). Hotaru does (from spirit to girl). The orphans have not yet been asked.
- *Strongly supported:* **Hotaru's story is Runa's, told in folklore.** A girl with an uncanny gift that makes others prosper; a gift now being "washed away" by "the times"; fear of becoming ordinary; and the discovery that "being normal meant a fun life." Koizumi and the president planned exactly this for Runa — that she "become a normal girl" (Vol. 4 Ch. 10). Hotaru gets there by her own choice, through a friend's mandarins and a book, and the chapter treats it as a liberation. The librarian's line is not in the book; it is her gloss.
- *Strongly supported:* **The associates expose the feudal core of Runa's benevolence.** She shelters refugees, funds an orphanage, gives a dead town work — and in the same motion acquires children whose meals depend on serving her, one of whom exists to be shot in her place. The narration's verb is "purchased." The poverty lecture that opens the section applies to them: they cannot see options they were never shown.
- *Strongly supported:* **Yūbari plus the K-Card (Ch. 6) is state-building.** Registers, identity cards, bank accounts, a trained armed force, a labour reserve and a transport network for a displaced population — assembled by a company. This is what Koizumi meant about a girl "in control of a nation" (Vol. 4 Ch. 10), and what Ono feared as a second "imperial police" (Vol. 3 Ch. 11). The novel presents it as kindness, and it is; it is also the material of the downfall sentence in Vol. 3 Ch. 2 — structures whose top does not control what is done in its name.
- *Strongly supported:* **The spy farce has a serious ledger.** Three services had people beside Okazaki before anyone moved; the Americans consider "obstruct or eliminate" routine language; the only restraint on them is fear of an eleven-year-old's revenge. Maefuji — the policeman who arrested her in the game — is now the official who cleans up around her.
- *Plausible:* Runa's laugh is the "pretending not to notice" the Americans describe. She accepts three foreign plants into her inner circle the same night; her safety lies in everyone's agents being known to everyone.
- *Plausible:* Ichijou's toilet and the Western executives' objection is the volume's culture clash in one image — the same line that divides the subprime scene (Ch. 4): whether rank excuses you from the dirty work or obliges you to do it. It also rhymes with "a job for adults": Koizumi's claim was that adults clean up.
- *Plausible:* "lunakeikain" on the forum is the chapter's comic proof that she cannot stop taking part — and of how easily "the stock market doesn't appreciate them" could be read later as an insider's comment.
- *Speculative:* a body double chosen for resemblance is a device that rarely goes unused.

## Chapter 8 — The Villainess Meets the Fortune Teller

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV, March–May 2003: graduation from the elementary division, a visit to a family of fortune-tellers, and the first weeks of junior high. The longest first-person chapter after Ch. 2, and the volume's richest for the game plot: it introduces a game character whose route conceals how the original Runa financed herself, gives a dated prophecy, and ends with one conversation each with the three boys. *(Read in full for the second edition; the first edition's summary of it was thin.)*

**What happens**

*Graduation*
- All four of the Quartet have beaten the school's record marks; Runa is fourth because of the autumn she was ill. Eiichi is class representative: "A win's a win. I'll always be proud when I can beat you, Runa." Her private thought: "I wonder what the real Keikain Runa would've done in this moment?"
- She asks what each wants to beat her at. **Yuujirou:** "I want to build a relationship with you like the one you have with my father" — which means inheriting his father's seat. His brother, in the upper house "thanks to you," may move to Hokkaido, where the ruling party is losing ground; his brothers-in-law want the base. "You're overqualified to work with a city or prefectural politician."
- **Mitsuya:** "My goal isn't about Keikain. I want to outdo Teia" — on equal study hours. He has had offers from Silicon Valley and still means to enter the bureaucracy.
- **Eiichi**, taking her hand in front of the class: "**I can't do better than you right now, and I want you on my team. So, Runa, will you marry me?**" — "You're so stupid, Eiichi-kun!"

*Badges and associates*
- The academy doubles at each stage: 100 pupils, then 200, then 400. **Gold badges** (pupils since elementary: nobility, zaibatsu, political families); **silver badges** (entrants at junior high, mostly the gold badges' retainers, wearing their patron's crest); **bronze badges** (high-school scholarship students, who may accept a crest). "It was frankly impressive how the powerful people in our country had formed a system to handpick excellent personnel and place them under their protection."
- The Keikains have **bought ten endorsement slots** from other noble houses. She meets nine "prospective classmates": "**These people were to become my pawns.**" Tachibana "purchased outstanding children from Karafuto orphanages… with no one but me to rely on in life, they'd put their lives on the line." "I was already infecting them with my power as if it were a poison."
- Her reading of her own class: "Organizations in Japan functioned by separating authority from real power. We members of the privileged class were displayed like decorative flowers in a solarium… only expected to pair up with someone who held true power and pass our blood on." The nobility is losing value because it "failed to share that blood with the leaders of the next IT bubble." She herself is "an extremely rare product with premiums attached."
- A twelfth slot was surrendered "due to a relationship with the previous family head," and transferred to the Katsuki family for **Kanna Mizuki**, adopted daughter of **Kanna Sera** — a name Runa knows from the game.

*The Kanna house, Jinbōchō*
- **What the game showed:** Mizuki is "a love-crazed girl who had a new boyfriend each month," a barometer of student opinion on the scholarship students' reform, with a hidden mechanic (her affinity rises with each route cleared). She never opposes Runa and leaves to study abroad in the second term of the final year — after which "the school conflict came to a head." The background books say **the three boys "conspired to force her to go abroad."** Runa's inference: she was the Keikain family's private adviser.
- **Who the Kannas are:** an all-female house of diviners founded in one generation by Kanna Sera, mistress of Runa's grandfather Hikomaro, with whom she "reigned over Japan's dark side." Angela's file: "She's a spy. A honeypot." The "daughters" are scouted orphans. With Hikomaro dead and patrons ruined by the bubble, the house is in a succession fight; **Mizuki is the heir.**
- **The debt:** Kanna Sera "told the Keikains they'd be targeted during the **Second February 26 Incident**" — "a coup d'etat by the imperial police and self-defense force that had never occurred in my past life." Hikomaro escaped it on her warning.
- Mizuki looks fully adult at twelve. Raised in an orphanage and abused there; "her hardships were said to be the source of her talents." Her first question: "how old are you, Keikain-san?" To Runa: "I only need to focus on my own life, but you're responsible for hundreds of thousands of Keika Group employees."
- **The theory.** The fee is "market price" — "a wager on fate itself." Fortune-telling is "closer to a curse… **A curse that sets the future in stone.** Having your fortune told is the same as losing two things — your future, and your possibilities." Told that path A hides a pitfall, you avoid it — and never find the money beyond it; told of A and B, you never look for C.
- **The deck** has twenty-three cards: the major arcana plus the blank spare, which "represents something one can't see, or even that one doesn't want to see."
- **The stake.** Runa hands over her silver Keikain crest badge: "I'll bet my future. You can use that however you want, and I'll do the same with the fortune you tell me."
- **The spread** (distant past → distant future, about six months each way):
  1. **The Emperor, reversed** — "did you experience a loss of power?"
  2. **The Moon, upright** — "quite a bit of emotional damage."
  3. **The Star, reversed** — hope and ideals lost, "or perhaps that the situation has become more realistic."
  4. **The Wheel of Fortune, reversed** — "an unpleasant event will soon occur."
  5. **The Hermit, reversed** — "you're going to draw attention in a bad way."
- Runa: "I know what it is… **The snap election in the House of Representatives.**" — "You win. I believe in this future entirely."

*The vision*
- At tea with Kanna Sera ("I used him, and he used me"), Runa slips into "a scene from the future" — the game's unseen farewell:
  - Game-Runa: "**I'm engaged to a Teia family member now**, and Katsuki-san is bugging me to get my affairs in order."
  - "**Now that I've got your connections, money does come in much more easily. I'll get the Keikain family back on our feet.**"
  - Mizuki: "once you marry Teia, you'll get your happy ending. But are you sure?" — "I bet my life on the revival of the Keikain family, and now it's finally within my reach."
  - "I was more talking about Takanashi-san." — "Who cares if he has a mistress or two? That's what happens when two families join."
  - "May your life be full of good fortune."
- Her questions on waking: the downfall followed this farewell at once, so "why did things seem so peaceful?… which part of my plan went wrong?" And, since the family's bad debts needed ¥45–50 billion: "**Who would help a failing young noblewoman like me raise more than fifty billion yen? And why?**"
- Kanna Sera asks to see "what's in your pocket." It is the **Cintamani stone** from Fushimi Inari — which Runa had not brought. "It will lead you where you need to go."
- Runa's verdict on Mizuki: "I'm not sure I'll keep her close to me, but I think we could become good friends."

*Junior high*
- **The household reshuffled:** Tachibana returns as butler after leaving Keika Railway; Angela goes to New York; **Tokitou Aki** becomes secretary over Eva and Ichijou Erika; Saitou Keiko and Katsura Naomi are co-head maids, Tachibana Yuka apprentice; Eva, Kitagumo Ryouko and Anisha are assistant maids under Nagamori Kaori. Runa has "faith in… the trustworthy CIA and ex-KGB agents" — "if I overlooked the information and connections the agents were passing back."
- To Yuka: "think of at least one thing you're interested in for yourself. After all, we both have long lives ahead of us. Most likely." And an order: stop refusing boys out of loyalty.
- **Class 1-A:** Runa, the three boys, Katsuki Shiori, Kanna Mizuki, Tachibana Yuka, Rudaka Miu. The other associates and friends are spread across B–F. Mizuki wears no Keikain badge. Shiori glares at her; Yuka is cold: "there must be bad blood between the three."
- Small things: the boys are now taller; Akiko "crushed" Yuujirou at kendo; Runa will sing in the Teia Philharmonic's *Carmen*; the school's sexual culture is lax ("there'd been no shortage of scandals"); every club wants her and she joins none — "I don't like being tied down," and talent no longer beats hours ("the ten-thousand-hours problem"). The gold badges eat in a private salon; she must show her face there or be ostracised. Shiori chooses the "go-home club."

*Three conversations*
- **Mitsuya, in the library**, reading a fantasy epic: "Do you think we'd make the same choices if we were this story's main characters?" Runa: "I don't think so. **Power always captivates those who have it.**… Once power grows too much, you can't control it. And, of course, the antagonist might always steal it all from you." She has prepared for the fall — a correspondence degree at Kanbe's college; funds dispersed in Swiss accounts. "I'd stared into that abyss, and the adults in my life had pulled me back." The story is won, she notes, not by hero or dark lord but by "a pathetic, obsessed man."
  - Mitsuya: "**You're not alone. I'd definitely save you before the power overcame you.**… Stumble all you like, and I'll come rescue you." After he leaves: "**Liar. You didn't save me at all…** In this world, would Mitsuya-kun come to my rescue? Or take part in condemning me?"
- **Yuujirou, at the café**, sorting photographs of prospective brides. Each girl's family — contractors, civil engineers, co-operative executives with relatives in local assemblies — would keep his father's seat warm until he is twenty-five. "The Koizumi administration is cutting down on public works… Some families even sacrifice their daughters for financing. It can't go on like this." Runa's own marriage offers (Japanese, Russian, European) are binned by Tachibana unread. She recalls that in the game Yuujirou hid his feelings until the heroine, **Takanashi Mizuho**, broke through.
- **Eiichi, after school.** The Teia family is leaving the dissolving Futaki zaibatsu and forming a **Teia Group** from companies whose main bank is the failing **Gowa Osan**. He refused to let the group take TIG Backup Systems (the four friends hold 50%). "Are you buying Gowa Osan, Runa?" Her answer: the government has decreed "Japan can only have four megabanks" — counting postal savings and Norinchukin — so six commercial banks (Keika, Imperial Iwazaki, Futaki-Yodoyabashi, Honami, Gowa Osan, Karafuto) "plus two more would have to turn into four."
  - Eiichi: "Marrying you would really have a lot of benefits, wouldn't it?" Runa: "I wonder if my marriage will have any kind of love in it at all?" — "**Do you really need that?**" — "Eiichi-kun, you're so stupid!"

**Revelations**
- **The game's Runa had a financier.** She was engaged to a Teia, rebuilding the family with money that came through the Kanna network, and believed herself about to win — tolerating the heroine as her fiancé's "mistress." Her downfall came immediately after Mizuki's departure, which the three boys engineered. The game's story of a jealous villainess undone by reforming classmates hid a money trail.
- **The Second February 26 Incident** is defined: a coup by the imperial police and the Self-Defense Forces, aimed in part at the Keikains, survived by Hikomaro thanks to Kanna Sera.
- A dated prophecy: within about six months, an unpleasant turn and unwanted public attention. Runa identifies it with the coming general election.
- The Cintamani stone moves on its own and is recognised as real.
- Eiichi has proposed — as a merger. He has also said he does not think love is necessary.
- Runa privately calls her associates pawns and her influence a poison, expects a short life as a real possibility ("Most likely"), and has an escape fund.
- In the game, Mitsuya did not save her.
- The state intends to force the megabanks down to four.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the "ten-thousand-hour rule" is from Malcolm Gladwell's *Outliers* (a 2008 book — knowledge from her first life); the novel is ***The Lord of the Rings***, the "pathetic man" is Gollum ("his obsession, and Bilbo's feelings when setting him into motion, decided the fate of the world"), and *The Two Towers* opened in Japan in February 2003; the mahjong manga is *Akagi*, the "Washizu" arc; "During Koizumi's reformation, rural civil engineers took a devastating blow"; the "single district phenomenon" and independent voters; the companies joining the Teia Group are the **Midori-kai**, "a corporate group that was like a get-together spot for Sanwa Bank"; Norinchukin Bank.
- **Real:** the 22 major arcana and their conventional readings; cold reading; hereditary Diet seats and "keeping the seat warm"; public works as rural Japan's employer and the construction lobby's place in LDP politics; "lending umbrellas when the weather was good and taking them back when it rained"; UFJ (Sanwa plus Tokai), whose troubles in 2003–04 ended in its absorption by Mitsubishi Tokyo; the general election of 9 November 2003.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The chapter title uses "villainess," not "lady."** Every other chapter of the volume says "the Lady." The fortune-teller meets the game's character — and the vision shows that character from inside: cool, dynastic, sure of her "happy ending." For the first time the reader sees that the original Runa was not a petty bully but a girl who "bet my life on the revival of the Keikain family." The present Runa has been living her predecessor's project at a thousand times the scale.
- *Strongly supported:* **Mizuki's theory of fortune-telling is a critique of the heroine's method.** Knowledge of the future closes doors: you avoid path A's pitfall and lose what lay past it; knowing A and B, you never find C. Runa has run her whole second life on foreknowledge — and has just refused, in Ch. 4, to take the path she knows. The series' title promises a girl who knows the crash is coming; this chapter says that such knowledge is a curse that "sets the future in stone."
- *Strongly supported:* **The spread is the volume in five cards.** Emperor reversed (the defeat by Koizumi), Moon (the breakdown), Star reversed (the present "moratorium" — ideals lost, or realism gained), Wheel reversed (the election), Hermit reversed (exposure). The reversed Hermit is the precise opposite of what the adults arranged for her: withdrawal without notice.
- *Strongly supported:* **She stakes her crest.** The silver badge is the token a patron gives a retainer. By handing it to a girl who refused Keikain protection, Runa reverses the relation: she places a bet with someone who owes her nothing. Her verdict — "good friends," not a retainer — is the only relationship in the chapter she does not describe as ownership.
- *Strongly supported:* **The three conversations are three offers, and each misses.** Mitsuya offers rescue — and she remembers he failed to give it. Yuujirou shows her the marriage market from the inside and does not speak. Eiichi offers marriage twice, as a team-up and as banking strategy. The pact of Vol. 3 Ch. 13 (earn your own money, then confess) is visibly straining: each boy is now competing with the others *through* her.
- *Strongly supported:* **"Do you really need that?" is the game's engagement, re-formed.** In the vision, game-Runa is engaged to Teia for the family's sake and indifferent to love. Eiichi now proposes exactly that bargain, and it is the present Runa who wants more. The roles have crossed.
- *Strongly supported:* **The *Lord of the Rings* reading is her self-portrait after Iraq.** The Ring cannot be used well; its bearer ends afraid of everything; the world is saved by an accident of pity. "The adults in my life had pulled me back" casts Koizumi and the rest as the Fellowship. Her own forecast — "something I didn't account for would've pulled the rug out" — matches the downfall sentence of Vol. 3.
- *Plausible:* **The ¥50 billion question points at Chapter 9.** Someone financed a ruined duke's daughter on a scale no fortune-teller's clients could. The Kannas' business is secrets and sex among the powerful; Kanna Sera is called a spy. The financier's identity and motive are the mystery the vision plants.
- *Plausible:* **The Second February 26 Incident now touches every thread** — the Keikains were its targets, Hikomaro its survivor, the Kannas his informants, and its veterans are still active (Ch. 1, Ch. 6). Runa's house was on the rebels' enemies list a generation ago. Earlier volumes described it as an imperial-police revolt against nobility and zaibatsu, set off by "that author" (Vol. 3 Prologue) and borrowed from Oshii Mamoru's *Kerberos* stories; this chapter adds that the Self-Defense Forces took part and that the Keikains were named. Its slogans — dissolve the zaibatsu, break the privileged classes — are the programme Koizumi is now carrying out by law.
- *Plausible:* The stone's reappearance, on the day Hotaru's story closes (Ch. 7), keeps the supernatural layer alive at the moment the "lucky child" protection is said to be fading: the fox's token is the one piece of luck that is hers rather than borrowed.
- *Speculative:* The bad blood between Shiori, Yuka and Mizuki may be an inheritance from Hikomaro's household — wife's line, retainer's line and mistress's line — carried into a classroom.

**Connections:** Vol. 3 Ch. 10 (Fushimi Inari; the Cintamani stone; the fox bride whose groom is hidden) and Ch. 13 (the pact); Vol. 4 Ch. 5 (the bought election; "the queen with her head on the chopping block… defeated by Takanashi Mizuho"), Ch. 9 (the dream of the game's reform vote; Mitsuya's "good intentions") and Ch. 11 (Eiichi: "I don't want to give you up"; marriage offers from Europe); Vol. 3 Ch. 6 (the nightmare: "even if I have to sell my soul to the devil"); this volume's Ch. 2 (the flower beds in the game; "would I become an adult at all?"), Ch. 3 (the three endings; "sold your soul to the devil"), Ch. 4 (megabanks; Gowa Osan), Ch. 7 (the associates; Hotaru) and Ch. 9 (the game's backers).

## Chapter 9 — The Queen Bee's Tea Party

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV, spring 2003, one afternoon: her first faction tea party in junior high. Five visitors bring five political messages, and between them she reconstructs — at last — what the game's Keikain Runa was doing when she fell. The volume's final chapter and its answer to the question the series began with. *(Re-read in full for the second edition. The first edition had the tax-haven revelation and the last line; it missed most of what leads up to them.)*

**What happens**

*Before the guests*
- "Queen bee" is "the American term for a female student at the top of the social ladder." A tea party says "I'm the queen bee, and I dare any of you to complain!" Runa is reluctant: a show of strength will only call anti-Keikain factions into being. Her adviser Nozuki Misaki: "You draw lots of attention, so you need to ensure you have a large enough group to destroy opposing factions once they form."
- Kushunnai Nanami, the body double, sleeps holding the porcelain doll Runa gave her.
- Runa's own description of her associates: "temporary workers sent to me with strings attached by the superpowers America and Russia."
- **Ryuu Suzune's briefing.** Washington and Moscow place people beside her because their influence is unofficial. "The Japanese government, which could exert its power legally, sent its agents to the Keikain family, my school, and the Keika Group instead." "No one's brave enough to send you people while you're Prime Minister Koizumi's enemy."
  - "Being attached to you is a gamble, my lady… you're extremely secretive, and you try to keep a small group of elites around you. On top of that, you can only control people from behind the scenes, since you aren't a legal adult yet. It's easier to corner someone like that if you have manpower."
  - "**All they have to do is corner you at this moment, right when you want to act. By the time you're an adult, the prime minister will already have left his seat. You can't get around that.**"
  - Her community funds the opposition, and thinks little of it: "My grandfather told me they have the same eyes as the Red Guards." "We don't plan things in terms of five or ten years. We make plans for the next fifty to one hundred years."
- Runa on the election: "I think I'll pour money into Izumikawa's faction and leave it at that."
- A thought stops her: "Try to remember the faces of Keikain Runa's followers in the game. Were any of them my current associates?!"

*Five guests*
- **Asuka** — a favour: help her father, who may lose his seat. The "**987 rule**" for an opposition win: hold 90% of your own base, take 80% of independents, keep the incumbent under 70% of his. Ehime's issue is "environmental and water" — a water-transfer scheme, the Ikata nuclear plant and the Shikoku Shinkansen are being attacked as a "three-point plan for environmental destruction." Koizumi's public-works cuts please urban independents and hollow out the regions. She only wants fundraiser tickets bought. Runa: "We're friends, right? Send the tickets my way."
- **Kaoru** — a message from the **Courtiers**, the academy's clique of old nobility: join us. "The people up top want you to leave Izumikawa's faction, ally yourself with the opposition, and call for a coalition government. You and the Iwazaki zaibatsu could control Karafuto," carry a no-confidence motion and form a government — to save noble privilege. Runa: "the Taira clan seeking help from the Minamoto."
  - Asked as a friend, Kaoru reverses: "If you aren't giving them any money, I think the opposition must be hopeless… We are nobles. We also both belong to zaibatsu. **Don't you see that you're being set up as the heroine on the guillotine?**"
  - Runa: "I know. **That's exactly why I can't get rid of that prime minister.**" Against Koizumi she can "pretend to be a good loser and even have compromises put in place"; allied to the opposition, she and Kaoru "would either be completely overthrown, or need to purge their party entirely. It would be a bloodbath."
  - Kaoru: "I bet the Imperial Court was abuzz with talk like this during Heishoukoku's rise."
  - In passing: the abolition bill followed "after I was forced to use my own special privileges."
- **Lydia** — a test: "Do you honestly believe you can force Prime Minister Koizumi out in the next election?" — "No. Nor do I care to try. Even if we managed to take over, we'd still be the minority. The office workers would chase us out."
  - Their shared analysis: Japan's bureaucrats behave like a socialist state's apparatchiks; the opposition's permanent staff — activists, urban campaigners, defectors' secretaries — are divided, mid-purge, and want above all "the ability to regulate things." The 1990s coalition fell the same way.
  - Runa's question, "a missing link in this world's history": "**how far the former Northern Japan administration has eaten into the opposition party?**" Lydia: "**Workers of the world, unite.**"
  - The North seeded the South with informants, choosing "those who couldn't do much on their own, since that made them easier to control. **My father was one of these incompetents.**" Lydia: "They'll survive even if the country dies. Their positions mean they may even lead this country someday. But do you think my father and I would follow those people after controlling and condescending to them?" Her family "picked the right time to sell out their country"; an opposition victory might destroy them.
  - Runa: "couldn't you also say I'd never forgive the former Northern Japanese government for driving my father to his death?" Both keep smiling.
  - Lydia's joke: her old country found its helpers among "people who love historical dramas. Especially TV shows about shoguns and vice-shoguns who punish evildoers." Runa: "a good way to find idiots with blind faith in justice."
- **Mio**, now treasurer of the elementary council and a queen bee herself — "the oil that made… the whole academy run smoothly." With Lydia above and Mio below, the independent faction has three generations.
- **Kanna Mizuki**, stopped at the door by Nanami for not wearing the Keikain badge. Runa: "Welcome to my tea party, Kanna Mizuki-san."
- The count: sixty-nine girls, fifty-nine of them from her own year of a hundred.

*The reconstruction*
- Who was *not* in the game: Asuka, Hotaru, Kaoru (without Nakamaro alive there was no link), Tachibana Yuka, Mio, Lydia. Who *was*: a girl with Ryuu Suzune's hairstyle; a "fierce, dark" background figure like Glasya. "**The game version of me had definitely been approached by a Chinese faction.**" Her game faction "developed based on how nobles related to the issue of Karafuto."
- "In the game, I'd probably have gone along with the nobles' plan and opposed Koizumi." Then: "Karafuto was struggling with the inefficiencies of socialism, so what's going to happen when they deal with bad debts in real life?!"
- "Was I ruined because I went against the Koizumi administration? No… If my downfall was in 2008, the economic downturn must've done me in. I was actively anti-Koizumi in the game, so **why didn't he stop me…?!**"
- **The vision.** A smaller tea party; the game's Runa in the centre, "a desperate look on my face": "This is my declaration! As the Keikain Dukedom's heir, and one of the nobles carrying this country on their shoulders, Karafuto's state of poverty… **So I'm going to turn Karafuto into a special economic zone serving as a tax haven and law haven…**"
- "I was acting as heir to the Keikain Dukedom in the game. **I'd become the adult Prime Minister Koizumi kept me from becoming when he scolded me.**"
- "**I'd finally found it. The last piece of the puzzle that laid out my downfall. Money laundering.** Karafuto had been chosen as the location, and the declining Keikain Runa was chosen as the face in implementing the plan. I'd even used my special privileges as a noble to create a law haven."
- The boys' roles: "Teia Eiichi was selected as a potential collaborator from the financial world, Izumikawa Yuujirou because he knew politicians, and Gotou Mitsuya because his father had a handle on tax issues."
- The end: "I was ruined because the plan fell apart… No, that's not it. The global financial crisis occurring at the same time had probably caused that final scene." The girl at the airport "probably couldn't have flown anywhere from there. I'd rather have been 'sunk' by being trafficked into a red-light district bathhouse than sunk in the real ocean, although the latter was likelier."
- "Had Takanashi Mizuho, the protagonist, known all that…? Probably not."
- Hotaru is holding her hand. Kanna Mizuki, watching, waves and leaves.
- Runa moves her lips without sound: "**Poor Keikain Runa.**"

*Cast list and afterword*
- The cast list supplies: the game's title, ***Love Where the Cherry Blossom Falls***; Angela as "former CIA agent"; Anisha Egorova, "former KGB"; **Kitagumo Ryouko, "former Northern Japan government spy"**; Eva Charon, "transferred from CIA"; Kanna Mizuki, "heir to the Kanna family of fortune tellers/high-class prostitutes"; Lydia as "daughter of Karafuto noble Marquess Shisuka"; Governor **Iwasawa Makoto**, "author"; Professor **Kanbe Souji**; Maefuji as director of foreign affairs in the Public Safety Bureau.
- **The author's afterword:** the volume runs "from summer 2002 to roughly spring 2003." He came from the "alternate military history" genre, in which America is "the final boss," and watched Japanese feeling toward the United States change online. "This story covers the time period when that anti-American sentiment finally erupted" — over bad debts ("Wall Street had hurt us… simply because they could") and Iraq. "The question 'Will we help America in the Iraq War?' is such a major turning point in this book." The alternative was France's road, "diplomatic independence," which "**Ishihara Shintaro-shi, Tokyo's prefectural governor at the time, advocated**." "The real-life equivalent of the question they face would be '**Why did the people of that time period choose Koizumi Junichiro over Ishihara Shintaro?**'… I remember feeling that Ishihara… became lost in the maze of literary history instead of focusing on postwar history specifically." He adds that America "didn't forget" Japan's help and repaid it in Operation Tomodachi (2011).

**Revelations**
- **How the villainess fell.** The game's Runa, acting as heir to a ruined dukedom, fronted a scheme to make Karafuto a tax and legal haven — in substance a money-laundering zone — using noble immunity as its legal shield, with Chinese and former-Northern money behind her and the three boys recruited for their fathers' access to finance, politics and tax. It collapsed with the 2008 crisis. Her probable end was not exile but death.
- **The school story was a cover.** The heroine "probably" never knew. The scholarship students' "reform" and the boys' betrayal, as the game told them, were the visible surface of a financial and political collapse.
- **This answers Chapter 8.** The ¥50 billion came from people who needed a noble face and a haven; the Kanna network was the introduction.
- **Koizumi's scolding had a counterfactual.** In the game nobody stopped her; she became "the adult" early and was used. In this world the same prime minister removed her before anyone could use her.
- The old nobility now wants the present Runa to play the same role: break with Izumikawa, front an opposition–Iwazaki–Karafuto coalition, save their privileges. She recognises it as "the guillotine."
- The opposition party is penetrated by the former North's informant network. Her father Otsumaro was one of its instruments, and it drove him to his death.
- The Japanese state watches her through her family, school and companies; the foreign powers through her staff. Her opponents' strategy is to keep her cornered until Koizumi's term runs out.
- She will fund Izumikawa's faction and stay out of the election.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the Red Guards and Hong Kong's émigré memory; Koizumi's real practice of choosing ministers himself while letting factions fill the junior posts; fundraiser tickets; **Heishoukoku is Taira no Kiyomori**; "the opposition party's 1990s coalition government" is the **Hosokawa administration**; "self-criticism" refers to the United Red Army; East Germany's tens of thousands of informants in the West; the *Communist Manifesto*; the period dramas are *The Unfettered Shogun* (*Abarenbō Shōgun*) and *Mito Kōmon* — "they never solve the root of the problem whatsoever"; "law haven" defined as a jurisdiction beyond extradition.
- **Confirmed by the afterword:** Governor Iwasawa stands for **Ishihara Shintarō**; the volume's question is why Japan chose Koizumi's road over his.
- **Real:** the Hosokawa coalition's collapse in April 1994 after the abortive "national welfare tax" and the prime minister's loan scandal; the LDP's return in coalition with the Socialists; Nagano's "no more dams" governor, removed by a no-confidence vote in 2002 (and re-elected); the Ikata nuclear plant in Ehime; the Shimanami sea route (opened 1999); the LDP presidency's two-term limit, under which Koizumi left in September 2006; the collapse of the Taira within a few years of Kiyomori's death, and the passing of power from court to warriors.
- **Altered / in-world:** a divided Japan whose northern successor party sits in the Diet; a Shikoku Shinkansen; nobles with a Privy Council to defend.
- **Fictional:** the Karafuto haven scheme; the academy's cliques; the game.
- **Echo (my reading):** a peerage-run offshore zone on a northern island, laundering for continental money, is the dark twin of the "Offshore Town Act" debated in Vol. 3 — the same instrument, privately captured.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The first-edition reading stands, and is now fully grounded: "the scolding was a rescue."** The game's Runa was "the adult Prime Minister Koizumi kept me from becoming." Volume 4 staged his intervention as her defeat; Volume 5's last page shows the timeline in which it never happened. The series' title promised a girl who would dodge the crash by building an empire; its fifth volume says she was saved by being told no.
- *Strongly supported:* **The villainess was a victim, and the heroine an innocent bystander.** "Chosen as the face." The game's condemnation scene punished a girl for a scheme designed by adults who then vanished. This is what Volume 4 foreshadowed in general terms — "the 'villainess' known as Keikain Runa was also a sacrifice, taken down by Japanese culture and its systems" — now given its mechanism. Her last words are not triumph over a bad ending but pity for the girl who lived it.
- *Strongly supported:* **Everything that ruined the game's Runa is present in this world, larger.** A noble face, a Karafuto constituency, Chinese and Russian patrons, immunity, three boys with useful fathers — and now a bank, a railway, identity cards, an armed company, a settlement town and a Swiss treasury (Chs. 4–7). The nobles' proposal is the game's plot offered again. The difference is not her power; it is that she can recognise the guillotine, and that her friends tell her the truth.
- *Strongly supported:* **Her reason for sparing Koizumi is the volume's political thesis.** A skilled enemy who compromises is safer than allies who cannot govern. It is also the author's answer to his own afterword question: Japan chose the actor who could manage the American alliance and the independents over the writer with a grievance. Runa makes the same choice, three times in one afternoon.
- *Strongly supported:* **Lydia's "historical dramas" line is the sharpest sentence in the book about Runa herself.** The spies recruited people who love stories in which a hidden lord punishes the wicked. That is the plot of Runa's own film (Ch. 3), the structure of her bank rescues, and what she tried to do in Iraq. The glossary adds: such stories "never solve the root of the problem."
- *Strongly supported:* **The tea party is a census of the changed timeline.** Six friends who did not exist for the game's Runa — a politician's daughter, a household spirit, an in-law, a maid, a "little sister," a Karafuto senior — attend; the game's entourage (the Chinese girl, the dark background figure) is present too, now as known quantities. Hotaru's hand brings her back from the vision. The original had followers; this one has people who touch her.
- *Plausible:* **Suzune's timetable explains the adults' whole strategy since Volume 4.** Nobody needs to defeat Runa; they need to hold her until 2006. Tachibana's "stopgap… until she reaches adulthood" (Ch. 1) and Koizumi's glass slipper (Vol. 4 Ch. 10) are the same clock.
- *Plausible:* **Otsumaro is rehabilitated a little further.** In Volume 1 he was the disgraced father who dealt with the North. Here he is one of thousands of "incompetents" the North selected because they could be controlled, and his daughter speaks of his death as something done to him. The slur "young lady from Sakata" (Ch. 1) lands differently.
- *Plausible:* **Kanna Mizuki's wave.** She is the one person who has seen the same future from the other side — in the game she was the financier's introduction and was removed just before the end. That she comes without the badge, watches the vision happen and leaves suggests she knows what Runa just saw.
- *Speculative:* "Why didn't he stop me?" has more than one answer the series could still give: in the game Koizumi had already broken the nobles (Vol. 4 Ch. 9's dream) and had no reason to save one; or the scheme began after he left office in 2006; or someone wanted it to run.

**Connections:** Vol. 1 Prologue (the game's ending; September 2008) and the Otsumaro backstory; Vol. 3 Ch. 10 (the game's last image: Runa alone at Kansai Airport), Ch. 2 (the downfall sentence) and Part II §7 (the Offshore Town Act); Vol. 4 Ch. 5 ("a sacrifice, taken down by Japanese culture and its systems"; the cliques), Ch. 9 (the dream of the reform vote; Koizumi's "you'll end up betrayed"; the abolition bill), Ch. 10 ("We can't allow Karafuto's independence"), Ch. 12 (the nobles, Iwazaki and a "Heisei Restoration"); this volume's Ch. 1 (the movement; Iwasawa and Tsurui), Ch. 2 (Lydia; Kanbe's Hosokawa and the opposition's triangle; "would I become an adult at all?"), Ch. 3 (the newscaster; the three endings), Ch. 4 (Iwasawa's question), Ch. 6 (Karafuto's economy; the Karafuto party), Ch. 7 (the associates; Hotaru) and Ch. 8 (the vision of the farewell; the prophecy of the election). *Back to Vol. 2 (added after its reread):* Ch. 6 is this chapter's first draft — Kaoru, on the Courtiers' behalf, offers "support in getting approval if you ever wanted to create an independent state" in Karafuto; Runa works out that the game's Runa fell alone because her house was in decline and she was spent on a marriage; and she first suspects she was "offer[ed] up as a sacrifice." Asuka's "half your district plus one" (Ch. 6) is the seed of the 987 rule.

Two patterns are worth holding on to.

**The volume is symmetrical around Chapter 5.** Chapters 1–4 look outward and backward (what the adults did with her; what she will not do). Chapter 5 says what she was actually stopped from. Chapters 6–9 look at what replaces her — in the world (6), in her household (7), and in the script (8–9).

**Information is distributed against her.** The reader learns in Chapter 1 about a nationalist movement that wants her as its icon; she is told a softened version in Chapter 4. The reader learns in Chapter 7 that Washington considers Tachibana the real controller and Okazaki the target; she gets a cover story and laughs. This is Volume 3's "curated child" (that map's Part II §10) as a standing condition. The exception is the game: there she is the only one who knows anything, and Chapters 8–9 are the first time in five volumes that she investigates it like a case.

## §2. "The scolding was a rescue" — the chain of evidence

The first edition of this map proposed that Koizumi's intervention in Volume 4 saved Runa from her original fate. With the whole volume read, the argument can be laid out link by link.

1. **What Koizumi did (Vol. 4 Ch. 9–10):** stripped her of proxies, turned her own people, moved to abolish noble immunity, and told her "you simply aren't qualified." With the president he agreed that the adults would "stain our hands with blood" and that she must "become Cassandra."
2. **What she was doing (Vol. 5 Ch. 5):** pressing a plan under which America would answer terrorism with the destruction of a city — "You people haven't done anything to me, but I want millions of you to die so that I can build a better future with my country."
3. **What the price of continuing would have been (Ch. 2):** in Kanbe's words, "Drive your father and brother into retirement and inherit the dukedom yourself… Be the one to bury the people you love."
4. **What the game's Runa did (Ch. 8–9):** exactly that. With her brother dead and the family ruined she acted "as the Keikain Dukedom's heir," took money from people who needed a noble face, used her immunity to shelter a laundering zone in Karafuto, and was destroyed when the 2008 crisis broke the scheme. "I'd become the adult Prime Minister Koizumi kept me from becoming when he scolded me."
5. **What is being offered to her again (Ch. 1, 4, 9):** the same role — by the nationalists, by Iwasawa's "friends," by the old nobility through Kaoru. She declines each time and gives her reason: "That's exactly why I can't get rid of that prime minister."

So the claim is *strongly supported* — with two qualifications the text itself supplies. First, the rescue was not disinterested: Koizumi took the returns on her strategy (Vol. 4 Ch. 10), and by Ichijou's account he had a material quarrel with her oil trade (Ch. 4). Second, the rescue is conditional on time: her opponents need only "corner you at this moment… By the time you're an adult, the prime minister will already have left his seat" (Ch. 9). What saved her at twelve does not protect her at eighteen.

## §3. What Runa wanted in Iraq — the ethics, stated plainly

The volume asks the reader to hold three things together.

**Her reasoning was coherent.** She knows from her first life that the "light" war produced years of insurgency. She reasons as a creditor does: a larger loss now against a stream of losses later. "It was a conflict over the going rate for a human life." Deterrence by terror is a real strategic doctrine with a long history; the novel names it after Nobunaga's annihilation of a religious league.

**It was monstrous, and she is the one who says so.** Okazaki's word is "monster"; hers is the sentence about millions of strangers. What broke her was not squeamishness but recognition — "you realized you were capable of something so disgusting." The series has trained the reader for four volumes to admire her ledger. Chapter 5 shows where an unbounded ledger goes.

**The alternative did not go well.** Chapter 6 gives the humane war its results: Iraq in three pieces, Iran in the south, Kurdish secession, attacks in Europe ahead of our timeline's dates. Her verdict — "This world is so foolish and so kind" — refuses to rank the two.

An economist's way to say what went wrong in her thinking: she treated the problem as a **cost–benefit calculation with a single decision-maker**, when it was a problem of **who has standing to decide**. A cost–benefit test can only compare outcomes; it cannot tell you who is entitled to impose the costs. Koizumi's objection was never that her arithmetic was off. It was that a person who answers to no electorate, and cannot be tried, was assigning deaths. The president puts the same point as "the difference between a company and a nation" (Ch. 2). A firm may write off a bad asset. A state that writes off people has to answer for it — to voters, to law, to "posterity," the word that haunted her in Volume 4. That is why the abolition of noble immunity runs alongside the Iraq story: both are about whether power can be exercised by someone who cannot be held to account.

*Where the novel is weakest here:* it never has an Iraqi or Afghan voice. *(Revised 9 October 2026: earlier this note also called the openly debated nuclear option (Ch. 6) the least believable thing in the book. Measured against our history it is; inside this world it follows from the missing demonstration — see §3a.)* The moral argument is conducted entirely among people at a great distance from the dead — which is, to be fair, the author's point about television ("someone else's war," Ch. 3).

## §3a. Necessary context: a world that has never seen the bomb used on a city *(added 9 October 2026)*

*Added at a reader's suggestion; the textual basis is below.*

**The fact.** In this world no nuclear weapon has ever been used on a city. The Allies lost at Normandy, Germany fought on, and Japan's surrender was negotiated (Vol. 1 Ch. 2). There was no Hiroshima and no Nagasaki. Runa says so when she reads the Washington paper in Vol. 4 Ch. 11. The planners want to bomb the cities, wreck the railways, then use "nuclear attacks to set an example. Ah, it's like the Empire of Japan in 1945. They were apparently going to reenact the history that never happened in this world here in Iraq." The Cold War standoff that ended the Manchurian War rested on a Soviet test and the fear of "an all-out nuclear war" (Vol. 3 Ch. 3). Okazaki's line in Ch. 5 — the Soviet leader "mercilessly nuked both friend and foe and stopped us" — may mean battlefield use. If so, it was on a battlefield, by a state that no longer exists, in a war the West won, and it never became the world's image of what the bomb does. Either way, **what this world lacks is not nuclear use but a demonstration.**

**Why it matters.** In our world deterrence rests on a picture everyone has seen: one bomb, one city. That picture also produced what political scientists call the "nuclear taboo," the sense that use is unthinkable. This world has neither, and the series follows the consequences:

- **Planners believe an example still has to be made.** The Vol. 4 Ch. 11 paper calls for nuclear strikes "to set an example." Eva adds an "exchange rate": the murder of one American "repaid a hundred or a thousand times over." Runa's own reasoning in Ch. 5 is the same: "If they established the rate of human life as millions of people to a few terrorists, the resulting fear would last long enough to suppress any foolish backlash." She calls it "a mere variation on the Cold War's mutually assured destruction doctrine" and adds: "That was possible in this world."
- **Afghanistan becomes the substitute demonstration.** Its "war of annihilation, in which chemical and biological weapons had been used… was starting to function as a tool of fear" (Ch. 5). Chemical and biological weapons stand in for the city that was never destroyed.
- **Use is discussable in the open.** Eva: "We want to [nuke Afghanistan], but we have nothing to fire at" (Vol. 3 Ch. 9). The Pentagon's leadership backs the extermination paper (Vol. 4 Ch. 11). Congress fights over a request that would authorise nuclear use against Iraq (Ch. 6).
- **The terrorists want to make the demonstration themselves.** The 9/11 of this world is a nuclear plot: "Nuclear weapons held by nations were used as threats, but terrorist groups used them to get results" (Vol. 3 Ch. 6).
- *Plausible (my inference):* **Japan has no atomic-bomb memory either.** No *hibakusha* and no Hiroshima peace movement may help explain a Japan that is "more of a 'normal country'," with offensive forces and no reluctance to "use armed might in the name of national interest" (Vol. 2 Chs. 6, 8).

**How this changes the reading.**
- **The series' focus on weapons of mass destruction is consistent worldbuilding, not melodrama.** Earlier versions of this map (§3 and §12) called the openly debated nuclear option the least believable thing in five volumes. Inside this world, it is what you would expect.
- **Runa's Fallujah plan is the same logic.** She wanted to be the one who supplied the demonstration this world never had. Koizumi's "a child is trying to help kill people" (Vol. 4 Ch. 9) took her out of the room where that first use was being argued for, before any of it could carry her name.
- **"This world is so foolish and so kind"** (Ch. 5) covers this too. The example was not made in Iraq. The world stays without its Hiroshima, and its fear stays unformed. The open question is who will try to make the demonstration next.

## §4. Subprime — the scene as an economics lesson

Chapter 4's centrepiece is the best-informed finance scene in the series. It helps to separate what Angela describes into its real components, and then to mark where the novel simplifies.

**(a) The loan.** A mortgage to a borrower with weak credit, little or no down payment, a low initial payment and a scheduled jump. The real products were mostly "2/28" and "3/27" adjustable-rate loans — two or three years at a fixed "teaser" rate, then a reset. The novel gives four years and a fifth-year step; the logic is the same. *The loan only works if the house can be refinanced or sold at a higher price before the reset.* It is a bet on house prices in which the lender holds the collateral and the borrower holds the hope.

**(b) Why the money was there.** After the dot-com crash and the 2001 attacks, the Federal Reserve cut its policy rate from 6.5% to 1% by mid-2003 and held it there for a year. Cheap money looks for yield. Japan, at zero, was a second source: borrow yen, lend dollars — the "carry trade." The novel is right about all of this and right to call it "wartime" money.

**(c) Ichijou's objection.** "An 8 percent annual interest rate on a mortgage with no down payment?! That's insanity!" The narration glosses 8% as "an eight percent chance that the ten million yen won't come back." That is a teaching simplification. A lending rate compensates for *expected loss* — the probability of default multiplied by the share of the loan you fail to recover — plus funding cost and profit. With a house as collateral the recovery is not zero. But the instinct is sound: a high rate is a statement about risk, and a banker who lived through Japan's bubble hears it.

**(d) Angela's fix, and the novel's biggest simplification.** She says: mix prime with subprime and the average rate — and risk — comes down. Real securitisation did not average; it **tranched**. Thousands of loans go into a pool. The pool's income is paid out in order of seniority: the top slice is paid first and takes losses last; the bottom slice takes the first losses. Because of that ordering, roughly three-quarters or more of a pool of subprime loans could be sold as AAA. The lower slices were then pooled again into new securities (CDOs) and sliced again, producing more AAA paper out of what had been BBB.
- **The hidden assumption** was that defaults in Florida and defaults in Nevada were largely independent, so a senior slice would be safe unless something close to everything went wrong at once.
- **Ichijou's reply is the correct critique**: "like combining a glass of wine with a glass of mud; neither will be drinkable." In Japan in 1991 land fell *everywhere together*. When losses are correlated, pooling does not diversify them; it only hides who is holding them. This one line is the 2008 post-mortem in miniature.

**(e) The ratings.** The agencies were paid by the issuers and used models with little data on a nationwide fall. The novel notes that their ratings "were useless during the IT bubble's collapse and the Russian currency crisis" and that buyers trusted them anyway.

**(f) The insurance.** "I'll have insurance agencies guarantee all the securities." The glossary names the instrument — the credit-default swap — and the firm, AIG, which had sold protection on mountains of senior mortgage paper and was rescued by the US government in September 2008. (The glossary's wording is loose: AIG's problem was not premiums but the collateral and payouts it owed as the insured paper fell.) Insurance moves a risk; it does not remove it. If the insurer cannot pay, the insured is exposed after all — "counterparty risk."

**(g) "Let's walk away with only the commissions."** This is the *originate-to-distribute* model: make the loan, sell it, keep the fee. Angela believes it leaves Keika with no risk. **Here the novel is shrewder than its character.** The firms that failed in 2008 were precisely the ones who thought this: they were still holding unsold loans in the pipeline, senior slices they had kept because they looked safe, and promises to buy back defective loans. Lehman Brothers, Bear Stearns and Merrill Lynch were fee-takers who turned out to be warehouses. Ichijou's face is the right response.

**(h) The carry.** Borrow at 0.25% in yen, lend at 4.5% in dollars. The narrator mentions hedging the exchange risk and still counts the whole spread as profit. A textbook would object: if you fully hedge the currency, the interest differential between two *safe* rates is competed away (that is what "covered interest parity" means). What is left over is the **credit spread** — the extra yield for bearing default risk. In other words the "tremendous profit" is not free money from Japan's zero rates; it is payment for the very risk Angela says she is not taking.

**(i) Angela's three layers** are what make the scene more than a lecture.
1. *Sell the loans* — $100 billion, $1 billion in fees.
2. *Let Japan's banks copy us and blow up; then buy them.* In our history Japanese banks mostly missed subprime because they were still working off bad loans. In this world Runa cleaned them up early, so they are free to overreach. **Her 1990s rescue has moved the next crisis onto Japan.** This is the series' most honest piece of alternate-history economics: curing one balance-sheet problem without changing incentives sets up the next. Economists call the general pattern moral hazard; the novel shows it without the word.
3. *My government wants this.* Angela has "orders… to teach financial systems in Japan a bit of Wall Street logic," and the listing of Keika Holdings will let foreign capital in through a "third-party allotment" — new shares issued to a chosen investor, diluting the family. Runa remembers "a conspiracy theory that… Wall Street set up Japanese financial institutions to take the fall" and says it is one "I couldn't laugh at any longer." The author's afterword names this resentment as the volume's subject.

**(j) "You mean you don't want to short it?"** Angela took for granted that Runa would sell the loans *and* bet on their collapse — the trade that made a handful of real investors famous in 2007–08. Runa's refusal ("I won't be the one who pulls that trigger") is a refusal of both halves. It is also the first time her knowledge of the future has reduced her profit.

**(k) The compromise, judged.** Car loans only; a ¥100 billion consortium; Keika's share capped at ¥30 billion; Japanese cars; cleared with two ministries. Three things can be said for it. Auto loans are short, the collateral is easy to repossess, and losses on them in the real crisis were far milder than on mortgages — so the fig leaf happens to cover the safest corner of the market. A consortium spreads the loss. And tying it to Japanese exports converts a financial bet into industrial policy. Against it: it is still participation, taken because "everyone had to get on board to some extent." The glossary adds that Angela expected car loans to fail first, dragging mortgages down with General Motors in 2009; reality ran the other way.

**(l) The figures.** The glossary puts subprime loans at "nearly two trillion dollars" when Lehman failed, with about 16% non-performing. Commonly cited figures for subprime mortgages outstanding in 2007 are nearer $1.3 trillion, with a higher share in serious delinquency by late 2008; the glossary's number may include near-prime lending. *Treat as approximate.*

## §5. The zaibatsu dissolved by accounting — and what Nakamaro sees

Volume 2's map explained mark-to-market accounting and cross-shareholdings at length (see its Q&A on the subject). Volume 5 shows the endgame.

- **The mechanism (Ch. 3, 6).** Banks and group companies hold each other's shares. Once those shares must be carried at market value, every fall in the market is a hole in capital. To avoid both the hole and the public money that comes with government inspection, groups sell their holdings — which pushes the market down further (the Nikkei "slumped into fifteen-thousand-yen territory") and unties the groups. No occupation authority is needed; an accounting standard does it.
- **The headline:** "Iwazaki has decided to dissolve its parent company." The loose "business groups" some hope to keep are what the real *keiretsu* were.
- **The megabank rule (Ch. 8):** "Japan can only have four megabanks" — counting postal savings and Norinchukin — so the six commercial majors must fall to roughly two or three. In reality the same pressure produced Mitsubishi UFJ, Mizuho and Sumitomo Mitsui by 2006.
- **Keika Holdings' position (Ch. 3, 6):** Kanbe calls it "a kind of government-affiliated bank." Listing it is the government's exit from the bad-debt era and its way of getting market discipline over a bank whose biggest borrower is its own sister company (Keika Railway, with ¥3 trillion of projects). *A professor would call this the heart of the matter:* a bank that lends mainly to its owner's other businesses is a conflict of interest with a banking licence. The magazine is right that the railway is the "time bomb," and the government is right to want outside shareholders. Runa's group is, structurally, exactly the kind of zaibatsu that Takenaga's reforms exist to break.
- **The Moonlight Fund's anomaly.** It supplies the bank's profits, "effectively leads" the group, has no legal relationship with it, and keeps its accounts in Switzerland. Tenman's question (Ch. 4) and the magazine's (Ch. 6) are the same: once the bank lists, what is this thing? A listed bank cannot depend on an undisclosed private treasury — and a private treasury that props up a listed bank is a securities problem waiting to be named.
- **The conglomerate discount (Ch. 7 glossary).** Investors value a sprawling group at less than the sum of its parts because they cannot see inside it. "The stock market doesn't appreciate them," as lunakeikain posts. Keika's strength — everything connects — is, in market terms, a cost.
- **Nakamaro's answer (Ch. 4).** Sell control of the oldest company to a foreign major on the "50.1%" model (control and consolidation for the buyer; name, management and listing kept for the seller — the real Roche–Chugai template); take ¥200 billion in cash; make dilution the pattern for the rest. "The Keika Group is too large for you to carry all on your own. Then again, if you're going to take on the world in battle, those companies are still too small." He is describing the squeeze every national champion faced after 2000: too big for a family, too small for global competition. He chooses to be a rich shareholder in someone else's group. Runa, by temperament, will not. "We'll end up clashing someday."

## §6. The oil trade — why Koizumi "sees you as his enemy"

Ichijou's chart (Ch. 4) is two paragraphs long and repays slow reading.

- **The structure.** After 1998 the Moonlight Fund became "the biggest absorber of dollar-based Russian bonds." Instead of dollars, it takes repayment in **options on Russian crude** at about $20 a barrel. Russia conserves hard currency; the fund holds a right to buy oil cheaply.
- **The pay-off.** With war coming, the market price approaches $40. Each barrel bought at $20 and sold near the market earns $15–20. The fund sells not to Japan but to "the chief consumers of Russian oil connected… by pipeline — the countries of Europe." Profit: "five billion dollars in the first quarter alone."
- **A back-of-envelope check.** $5 billion in a quarter at a $15–20 margin means roughly 250–330 million barrels — on the order of three million barrels a day. Russia was exporting something like four to five million barrels of crude a day at the time *(my figure, from memory — to be checked)*. So the claim that the fund is "involved in… 60 percent of Russian oil" is at least consistent with the profit figure. If so, a private fund owned by a Japanese child is the marketing channel for most of Russia's oil exports.
- **Why this is political.**
  1. *It is a foreign policy.* It ties Japan's energy and Russia's revenue together through her — the "continental" orientation that, as Kanbe says (Ch. 2), is the opposite of Koizumi's.
  2. *It profits from the war and supplies the war's opponents.* The barrels go to the Europeans who are refusing Washington.
  3. *It predates the war.* "You've been doing this for so long, starting even before the war." To a government watching, the Gulf bases, the Saudi railway and the oil book look like one design: someone who knew.
- **The euro note (Ch. 1).** Asked who stands behind the movement courting her, Tachibana shows a euro banknote. On the surface: France and Germany. Underneath, perhaps: in late 2000 Iraq began pricing its oil sales in euros rather than dollars, and a widespread theory of the day held that the war was partly about the dollar's role in the oil trade. The Volume 3 map tracked a "petrodollar thread"; a fund that sells Russian oil to the eurozone sits exactly on it. *(Plausible — the text does not spell it out.)*

## §7. Karafuto as internal colony; Keika as a state that nobody elected

**Karafuto.** The real Japan of 2003 was "hollowing out": factories left for China. The novel keeps them inside the border by inventing a reunified, post-socialist north with wages 20% lower, unemployment above 20%, and a population the mainland calls "second-class citizens." The consequences are worked through with care: mainland unemployment rises as Karafuto's falls (Ch. 6); migrants drive the trucks and trucks go missing (Ch. 7 thread); mine-owners use "extremely cheap laborers in shady operations"; a regional party props up the cabinet; ex-Northern soldiers turn up among both Runa's guards and the Shinjuku bombers. It is the series' most developed piece of social world-building, and it supplies the ground for the game's plot: a poor, semi-foreign territory that the wrong people want to turn into a haven.

**Keika.** Add up what the group now does:

The downfall sentence in Vol. 3 Ch. 2 described a government unable to control what was done in its name. By the end of Volume 5 that is a description of Keikain Runa.

## §8. Koizumi or Ishihara — the author's question, answered inside the story

The afterword asks why Japan chose Koizumi Jun'ichirō over Ishihara Shintarō. The novel answers through four speakers.

- **Kanbe (Ch. 2)** gives the structure. Koizumi's line is cities, small government, America; his enemies' is the regions, public works, the continent. Television rewards drama, and Koizumi is "the greatest 'actor' of our generation." The opposition's triangle — "politicians, mass media, and agitators" — can perform but not govern.
- **Iwasawa (Ch. 1, 4)** gives the feeling. Japan has obeyed and bled for America since the war; the Iraq War is revenge, "not for any moral or just cause"; "If only I'd had your power, this war could've been avoided." He asks what Koizumi has that he lacks, holding a dead friend's novel — and nearly gets "caught up in my friend's anger."
- **The movement (Ch. 1, 6)** gives the lineage: revenge for the Pacific War, land-power geopolitics, the Second February 26 Incident — and, in Chapter 6, veterans of that incident among would-be bombers. The respectable version and the violent version share ancestors. (Volume 3 said the incident was set off by "that author" calling for a Shōwa Restoration; if the friend whose novel Iwasawa holds in Ch. 4 is the same man, the governor's literary friendship and the nobles' living memory of being hunted are one story.)
- **Runa (Ch. 2, 4, 9)** gives the choice. To Kiyomaro: "This era was choosing Prime Minister Koizumi, not me." To Iwasawa: a smile. To Kaoru: "That's exactly why I can't get rid of that prime minister."

Put together, the novel's answer is close to the author's. Ishihara's independence was a *literary* position — pride, grievance, the beautiful gesture; the afterword's phrase is "lost in the maze of literary history." Koizumi's alignment was a *postwar* position — it accepted the alliance as the frame and worked the television age inside it. Runa, who has every reason to resent both America (Ch. 4) and Koizumi (Vol. 4), still judges that a competent opponent who compromises is safer than admirers who would spend her. The author adds one retrospective argument of his own: America "didn't forget" and repaid the debt after the 2011 earthquake.

*A note on balance.* This is the novel's argument, and the map reports it as such. Whether Japan was right to support the Iraq War, and how to weigh Ishihara's nationalism, are contested questions in real Japanese politics; the book takes a side while giving the other a dignified spokesman.

## §9. Four theories of what to do with a prodigy

Chapter 3 and its neighbours stage a debate among people who all see that Runa is extraordinary.

## §11. The game, as now known

Assembled from all five volumes; each line gives its source.

- **Title:** *Love Where the Cherry Blossom Falls* (cast lists).
- **Setting and span:** Imperial Gakushuukan Academy, chiefly the high-school years; "the game had only shown the life of the villainess… until she was eighteen" (V5 Ch. 2). The climax falls in the second term of the final year — autumn 2008 (V5 Ch. 8–9; V1 Prologue).
- **Heroine:** Takanashi Mizuho, a scholarship student, "the embodiment of the average citizen" (V4 Ch. 5). She "probably" never knew what lay behind Runa's fall (V5 Ch. 9).
- **Love interests:** Teia Eiichi, Izumikawa Yuujirou, Gotou Mitsuya. Yuujirou's route turns on the heroine breaking through his reserve (V5 Ch. 8).
- **The original Runa's situation:** family ruined by bad debts; Nakamaro dead (V3 Ch. 6; V5 Ch. 4); "all alone in the first place" (V4 Ch. 6); the ruin hidden until the final year (V5 Ch. 8); acting as heir to the dukedom (V5 Ch. 9).
- **Her project:** "I bet my life on the revival of the Keikain family" (V5 Ch. 8). Engaged to "a Teia family member"; indifferent to her fiancé's attachment to the heroine (V5 Ch. 8).
- **Her money:** through Kanna Mizuki's "connections" (V5 Ch. 8); a Chinese faction approached her (V5 Ch. 9); the family needed ¥45–50 billion.
- **Her scheme:** a declaration that Karafuto would become "a special economic zone serving as a tax haven and law haven," sheltered by noble privilege — money laundering, with her "chosen as the face." The three boys were recruited for finance, politics and tax (V5 Ch. 9).
- **Her public story:** "a former eastern spy, who used the protagonist to get involved in a takeover of Teia Motor Co." (V1 Ch. 9) — the label under which the game presents her.
- **At school:** a class-committee faction leader — the route Lydia is taking now (V5 Ch. 2); "a reformist despite being a noble" who sought compromise on the scholarship students' reform and lost a tied vote broken by her fiancé: "Keikain, both of us are behind the times" (V4 Ch. 9). She obstructed the heroine's flower beds and used the school's contractors for information (V5 Ch. 2).
- **National background in the game:** Koizumi had already broken the nobility (V4 Ch. 9); the game's Runa was "actively anti-Koizumi" (V5 Ch. 9).
- **The fall:** Kanna Mizuki is sent abroad — arranged by the three boys (V5 Ch. 8); the reform comes to a head; the financial crisis breaks the scheme (V5 Ch. 9); Maefuji arrests her (V2 Ch. 4); the last image is Runa alone at Kansai Airport (V3 Ch. 10), from which she "probably couldn't have flown anywhere" (V5 Ch. 9).
- **Who is absent from the game:** Asuka, Hotaru, Kaoru, Tachibana Yuka, Mio, Lydia (V5 Ch. 9); Nakamaro's child (V5 Ch. 4).

**What remains unexplained:** who her financiers were, precisely; why Koizumi's government "didn't stop" her; why the boys removed Mizuki; whether the engagement was to Eiichi or another Teia; and what the heroine's "reform" looked like from the heroine's side.

## §12. Where the volume strains

- **The nuclear debate — revised.** An American administration openly seeking authority for nuclear use against Iraq, with a congressional majority forming for it, is the least plausible thing in five volumes *by our history's standards*. It is coherent in this world, which has never seen a city destroyed by a nuclear weapon (§3a). What remains a strain is that the novel only states this premise once, in passing (Vol. 4 Ch. 11), and never shows how a world without Hiroshima came to think about the bomb.
- **Chapter 3's time-skips.** The film, its release, the Academy Award nomination and a later greenroom scene run a year or more past the rest of the volume, and the narration promises pay-offs ("her final choice"; "a real headache in her life") that lie outside it.
- **The associates.** Children bought from orphanages, one as a body double, are introduced warmly. The narration lets Runa call them "pawns" and her power "a poison," but the scene on the train is played as a meet-cute. The gap between what is described and how it is lit is uncomfortable — possibly by design.
- **Kanna Mizuki's history** (see the content note) is delivered in two sentences as the "source of her talents." The book moves on quickly.
- **A recycled document.** Roughly a third of "Keika Railway Part 57" (Ch. 7) is copied from Volume 4's "Thread #38," including its closing "breaking news" post. Whether the slip is the author's or the edition's, it means the K-Card-as-ID detail cannot be dated from the thread; Chapter 6's news item is the reliable source.
- **Text slips and inconsistencies:** the tower's "six hundred thirty billion yen" (Ch. 4); Nakamaro as "current family head" (Ch. 7); "Futaki Shiromizu Bank" (Ch. 6); Katsuki/Kazuki; "Keika Corp/Corporation"; Churchill's glow-worm remark placed in his boyhood (Ch. 4); the glossary for Chapter 4 printed after Chapter 5; Runa "giving" Mizuki a silver badge (Ch. 9) that she had in fact staked on a reading (Ch. 8); Gladwell's 2008 "ten-thousand-hour rule" cited in 2003 (defensible as first-life knowledge).
- **Carried over from Volume 4:** the report's attribution to "a Jewish think tank" is not repeated here; Chapter 5 re-attributes the logic to Runa herself and Chapter 6 to American generals — which reads as a quiet correction of emphasis, though the earlier line stands in the text.

# Part III — Synthesis

## Alternate-history ledger

**Preserved:** the 2002 midterms (the president's party holds the House and retakes the Senate); the build-up to Iraq through the autumn of 2002 and the invasion on 20 March 2003; Baghdad's fall within weeks; the Franco-German refusal; Britain and Japan alongside Washington; the split between a "light" war plan and its critics; the Federal Reserve's cheap money and the birth of the subprime boom; Japanese electronics makers' mass layoffs; the pharmaceutical merger wave and the 50.1% alliance model; the "Koizumi Theatre," newscaster politics and the urban independent vote; cuts to public works and the squeeze on rural contractors; a general election in the autumn of 2003; *Ragnarok Online*'s Japanese beta, *The Two Towers* in cinemas, the songs of 2001–02.

**Altered:**
- **The case for war rests on intent, and is partly true in-world.** Iraq financed a nuclear-terror plot and tried to buy a Soviet warhead (Vol. 3; Ch. 2, 6). The White House nonetheless knew there were no weapons (Vol. 4 Ch. 3) and built its case on "intention." *(This refines the Volume 3 map's line that the pretext is "an in-world truth.")*
- **No city has ever been destroyed by a nuclear weapon** — no Hiroshima or Nagasaki (Vol. 4 Ch. 11) — which is the necessary context for everything below (Part II §3a).
- **Washington debates nuclear use openly**, and Congress is asked to authorise it (Ch. 6).
- **Afghanistan was a war of annihilation** — chemical and biological weapons, Indian irregulars, the "Nagashima Doctrine" (Ch. 5).
- **Post-war Iraq breaks up at once:** Kurdish independence, an Iranian occupation of the south (Ch. 6).
- **Terror attacks in London and Spain come early** (Ch. 6 glossary).
- **Japan passes its dispatch law before the war** and its forces go to the Gulf (Ch. 3); really July 2003, for reconstruction.
- **Japan's economy is healthier:** unemployment about 4%, the Nikkei about 15,000, bad loans largely cleared — and its banks therefore exposed to subprime (Ch. 4, 6).
- **Mark-to-market arrives in spring 2003** and dissolves the zaibatsu, which still exist; public money goes to all megabanks (Ch. 6).
- **Factories move to Karafuto**, not China (Ch. 6).
- **The 2002 pharma deal** (really Roche–Chugai) is doubled with a fictional one (Arts Nova–Keika-Iwazaki-Hatabe).
- **Keika Electronics Union** (really HP–Compaq, May 2002) launches on 1 September 2002.
- **Yūbari is rescued** years before its real insolvency (2007).
- **A Japanese child wins an Academy Award** for a Japanese action film (Ch. 3).

**Wholly fictional:** the peerage, its immunity and the Privy Council; Karafuto's partition, reunification and "second-class citizens"; the Second February 26 Incident and its veterans; the Karafuto Social Democratic Party; the DAF's make-up; K-Cards as civil ID; the Shinjuku Geofront plot; the Kanna house; the Courtiers and the badge system; the Moonlight Fund's oil book and GLONASS subsidy; the Shikoku Shinkansen; the game.

## Interpretive possibilities

**Strongly supported**
- Koizumi's intervention spared Runa the role that destroyed her original (Part II §2).
- The game's "villainess" was a front for other people's crime, and the heroine did not know.
- Runa herself advocated mass-casualty deterrence, and the novel treats that as the true content of the title word.
- The volume's political argument sides with alignment over "independence," while giving the latter a dignified voice.
- Runa's rescue of Japan's banks has made Japan more, not less, exposed to the next crisis.
- Her group now performs the functions of a state without any of its accountability, and the people closest to her know it.
- Friendship — not money or foresight — is what most distinguishes this Runa from the game's.

**Plausible**
- The series is moving toward the director's third ending in a form she chooses, as Hotaru chose hers.
- The election will expose some part of what has been "erased" — the Iraq planning, the fund, the oil book, or the IDs.
- Nakamaro and Runa are being set up as the two futures of a zaibatsu: cash out, or carry it.
- The nationalist movement, the nobles' coalition scheme and the game's Karafuto haven are one constituency under three names.
- Kanna Mizuki knows more of the original timeline than a game character should.

**Speculative**
- The game's Koizumi let the haven scheme run because it served someone.
- Tachibana was the source of the Geofront tip-off.
- The "luck" that has attended Runa since kindergarten was partly Hotaru's, and its fading is the mechanism of the Wheel's reversal.
- The stone "will lead you where you need to go" — toward the fox's bridegroom.

## On the "What is Runa?" question

Volume 5 adds more to the running question than any volume since the first.

- **Names she is given:** company mascot; "a beautiful doll"; "Your Little Majesty"; "a Cleopatra of the Heisei era"; "my goddess"; "a monster"; "a natural-born actress"; "a twenty-first century Sarah Bernhardt"; "the Hosokawa"; one of "this country's two greatest geniuses"; "the heroine on the guillotine"; "the young lady from Sakata"; "an icon"; a "puppet"; King Midas reborn; "a kind person"; "not an omnipotent, omniscient god."
- **Names she gives herself:** "a villainess… Ah, wait. That's exactly what I am"; "a goddess, after all" (as a joke, to a temple); "decorative flowers in a solarium"; "an extremely rare product with premiums attached"; a "doting aunt"; "a reckless child."
- **What the supernatural layer says:** the fox's jewel is real and follows her; the household spirit who blessed her is becoming a girl; a fortune-teller reads her accurately and tells her that knowing the future is a curse.
- **What the game layer says:** the "real Keikain Runa" was a serious, lonely girl who staked her life on her family and was used. The present Runa thinks of her in the third person — "I wonder what the real Keikain Runa would've done" — and ends by pitying her.
- **What the moral layer says:** she is someone who can reach, by honest reasoning, a plan to kill millions, and who breaks when she sees it. The volume locates her humanity in the breaking.

The director's remark that she "already sees this world as a stage" is the nearest any character comes to the truth of her situation. But the volume's own answer is quieter. Asked what she wants from a fellow twelve-year-old, she says "think of at least one thing you're interested in for yourself" — and adds, of their long lives ahead, "Most likely." Her last question in Chapter 2 is whether she will become an adult at all. After five volumes of what she can do, Volume 5 is about whether she will be allowed, or will allow herself, to become a person rather than a role. Its last three words are spoken by that person, about the role.
