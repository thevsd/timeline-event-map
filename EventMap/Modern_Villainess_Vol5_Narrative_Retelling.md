*This is Volume 5 retold as a story, in reading order, by a reviewer reading it to you. Each piece covers one chapter, or one part of a long chapter. ## Before we begin: The year she sat out

Volume 4 ended with Keikain Runa being told no. The prime minister of Japan looked a girl in her last year of elementary school in the eye, told her "You simply aren't qualified," and took her war away from her. Not long afterwards she stopped eating, and very nearly broke. So the obvious question for Volume 5 is: what does a girl like that do next?

The book's answer is that, for most of a year, she does almost nothing, and the world goes on without her. The story runs from the late summer of 2002 to the spring of 2003, through the months when America prepared for and then fought the war in Iraq. Runa spends them at school. She sits on the student council, loses a kendo match, plays an online game until one in the morning, graduates from elementary school, and holds a tea party. The narration has a word for this, and the author uses it too: a **moratorium**. In his words, "During her moratorium in this story, Keikain Runa takes some time off from center stage. Afterward, the adults are forced to make a decision, whether they want to or not."

You can see the design in the shape of the book. Four of the nine chapters are told by other people: her family's elders, a film director, an American spy desk, and in one case nobody at all, just a stack of news clippings. In two of them she barely appears. And you can see it in the chapter titles, which all speak of "the Lady" (*The Lady's Parting Gift*, *The Lady's Moratorium*, *The Lady Spectates*, *The Lady's Supporting Roles*) right up until the last two, which switch to the game's own words: *The Villainess Meets the Fortune Teller* and *The Queen Bee's Tea Party*. As Runa steps back from the grown-up world, the plot of the otome game she was reborn into (a romance game written for a female audience, in which she is the villainess) quietly steps forward again. By the last page she has worked out how the girl in the game was destroyed.

The author is unusually open about what the book is for. He grew up reading and writing "alternate military history," the what-if war stories that were popular in Japan from the 1980s to the 2000s, in which the United States is "the final boss." Writing them online, he says, let him watch Japanese feelings about America change in real time. "This story covers the time period when that anti-American sentiment finally erupted." Some people felt "that Wall Street had hurt us with bad debts simply because they could"; many asked whether Japan, in backing the Iraq War, was "merely following in America's footsteps." That is why, he says, "the question 'Will we help America in the Iraq War?' is such a major turning point in this book."

He even names the real argument underneath the story. The alternative to following Washington was the road France and Germany took, "a route to diplomatic independence," and the man who argued for it in Japan was Ishihara Shintarō, the novelist who was governor of Tokyo at the time. In the novel he appears, thinly disguised, as Governor Iwasawa. So the author frames the volume's question as a real one: "Why did the people of that time period choose Koizumi Junichiro over Ishihara Shintaro?" His own short answer is that Ishihara "became lost in the maze of literary history instead of focusing on postwar history specifically." Keep that sentence in your pocket. It comes back, in a very literary way, in Chapter 4.

I'll be honest about how I felt reading this volume. It's quieter than Volume 4, and for long stretches it's gentle and funny: a girl who owns a megabank throwing a tantrum over a shooting game, three boys making her stamp paperwork. But underneath, it's the darkest book in the series so far. In Chapter 5, Runa tells us in her own voice what she was really planning for Iraq, and it's far worse than Volume 4 let us believe. Then the last chapter shows us the girl from the game, and we finally understand that being told no may have saved her life.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Moratorium**: a period in which something is put on hold. Psychologists use it for the time when a young person postpones adult commitments; here it means Runa's year away from business and politics.
- **Otome game**: a Japanese romance game written for a female audience, in which the player's heroine wins one of several boys. Runa has been reborn as its villainess.
- **Alternate military history**: what-if war fiction ("What if Japan won World War II?"), a popular genre in Japan from the 1980s to the 2000s.
- **Wall Street**: shorthand for America's big banks and investment firms.
- **Bad debts**: loans that won't be paid back in full. Japan spent the 1990s and early 2000s struggling to write off its banks' bad debts.
- **Diplomatic independence**: a foreign policy that doesn't simply follow America's lead.
- **Governor Iwasawa**: the novel's version of Ishihara Shintarō, governor of Tokyo from 1999 to 2012.

## Chapter 1: A trial she wasn't invited to

The volume opens with a sentence that sounds like a narrator clearing their throat before a fable: "PEOPLE ARE FOOLS. Their inherent foolishness makes them impossible to stop once they set foot down a path. This is the story of one prime example." It's never quite said who the fool is. By the end of the chapter I suspect it's nearly everyone in the room.

The room is in Shirokane, the Tokyo neighbourhood where the main Keikain family lives, and it's full of relatives. Not close ones. These are the branch families and vassal families, the minor noble houses that hang off a great dukedom like smaller branches off a trunk, and they've come to put Runa on trial. She hasn't been told. The book calls it, exactly, her "trial in absentia," and Tachibana, her old butler and the man who runs her railway, privately thinks the whole thing "a complete farce." He only agreed to come because Duke Kiyomaro, Runa's adoptive father, said it would let the family's inner circle "blow off steam."

The loudest voices belong to the Kitayama clan, descendants of the old ducal line from which Runa's grandfather Hikomaro came. Their highest title is an earldom, they have lived for generations on the legal immunity that Japan's nobles still enjoy in this world, and that immunity is about to be abolished. They also hold rights to inherit within the Keikain house. Their demand is simple: "now is the time to place all her rights under the main family's control!" After all, "She's been stopped by both the Japanese and American governments now."

Tachibana's answer is the most practical sentence in the chapter, and I love it because it isn't moral at all. "Say you stripped her of her rights. How would you manage them? And how would you inform her ladyship of your intentions?" Then, in that deep voice that silences rooms: "The main family couldn't manage everything Runa built." Her brother Nakamaro backs him up. Runa owns four of Japan's biggest companies, a bank, a railway, a trading company and an electronics group, and even she struggles to make their long-serving employees listen. "If they won't listen to her, do you really think they'd listen to us?"

So the relatives try another door. A man from the Iwazaki side of the family, the great industrial clan that both Kiyomaro and Nakamaro married into, asks, "Shouldn't we just leave it all for Iwazaki to handle?" Tachibana has already checked. Iwazaki's bank has told him privately that it doesn't want Runa's bank, and carving up the rest among Iwazaki's own companies would start a war inside Iwazaki. Then Kiyomaro adds something the room didn't expect. The American ambassador has thanked him personally. "The Americans scolded her too, but they apparently don't want to take any power out of her hands." Washington wants her groundwork for Iraq, and wants her in reserve if the war goes badly.

One relative can't resist a sneer and calls Runa "the young lady from Sakata." She was born and raised in Tokyo. The jab is at her late father, Otsumaro, who built his old Far Eastern Group in the port of Sakata in Yamagata, and it draws hard looks from Tachibana, Kiyomaro and Nakamaro all at once. Nakamaro mutters, to no one in particular, "We've grown too large. We can't even voice our true intentions anymore," and the room has the grace to look ashamed.

The verdict, when it comes, is Tachibana's. "We will temporarily manage my lady as a stopgap measure until she reaches adulthood. At that time, we'll take on independent directors from the Iwazaki zaibatsu to keep her in check." In plain words: nobody takes her companies, but when she grows up she'll have watchers on her boards, outside directors from her in-laws' industrial group. The duke closes the session: "I'll continue to leave the responsibility for Runa to Tachibana-kun."

I think this is the villainess story's most famous scene, the public condemnation, held years early and quietly defused. In the game, the villainess is denounced and stripped of everything in front of the whole school. Here the denunciation happens behind closed doors, while she's at school, as theatre stage-managed by the man who loves her, and it collapses on one dull question: who would actually do the work?

Then the real meeting starts, in the duke's office, over coffee Tachibana makes himself. "Are you happy with the way things turned out, Tachibana-kun?" "Everything in this world requires a bit of compromise." He produces a paper, an American investigation into the forces around Runa, and Nakamaro reads it and says, "I see… So the ghost of antiquity has been trying to possess Runa."

The ghost is a political movement. It wants revenge for losing the Pacific War, wants Japan to stop simply following America, and believes in Halford Mackinder's old theory that whoever controls the great land mass of Eurasia controls the world. In this world's history it "indirectly caused" a coup called the Second February 26 Incident, and after the Cold War its people helped Japan take back southern Karafuto (the island we call Sakhalin), then went too far and swallowed the island's Russian north as well, a quarrel between Tokyo and Moscow known ever since as the Northern Karafuto Problem. Now it wants a figurehead, and Runa, with the blood of the Romanovs, Russia's last imperial family, is perfect. Two of the politicians she helped are already in it: Governor Iwasawa of Tokyo and Tsurui, a former senior figure in the ruling party.

Tachibana's first decision is to hide all of this from her. "If she learns of this, she won't be able to act of her own free will anymore." Asked who's really behind the movement, he takes a single banknote out of his wallet and lays it on the desk. It's a euro, the new European currency that went into circulation that year, and the meaning is clear: France and Germany, who are breaking with Washington over Iraq. The narration gives the chapter its ethic in one sentence: "the adults stopping her now had to take the responsibility in her stead—both for the world and for history itself." And it gives Tachibana his old underworld name, the "Hungry Wolf of the Far East," for whom "the job was only just beginning."

His own job, meanwhile, is ending. At the railway's temporary headquarters near Akihabara he hands the presidency to Mikihara Kazuaki, a quiet timetable specialist from one of the big national railways, nicknamed the "line master," who "acted like a mere worker" but can play politics perfectly well. Mikihara's first act is to promote Nagamori Kaori, the hotel concierge who looks after Runa's tower. The official reason is tidying up the group's many overlapping maid organisations after a business magazine ran a story called *The Bloated Keika Group*. "Then what's the real reason?" Nagamori asks. "That would be office politics." He wants an ally in the hotel side, and above all he wants "a constant grasp on the young lady's next moves." Nagamori, with a concierge's smile, accepts, and mentions that the young lady has been complaining it's hard to leave her station on special trains.

The last scene is the one that matters most for the rest of the book. In a private room at the Keika Hotel Shinjuku, Tachibana meets the governor. "You have friends trying to trick my lady into taking a stance against the United States." Iwasawa doesn't deny it. "I'm not senile enough to betray the young lady who made me governor and advanced the huge Shinjuku Shinkansen project. If she picked a fight with the prime minister or the United States, though, I'd happily run to her aid." He is angry that she has been turned into "an icon for American propaganda," and he believes the coming war is revenge for the September attacks, "not for any moral or just cause."

Tachibana tells him calmly that he'll start "removing the politicians who'll most likely get in my lady's way," and that the governor used to be on the list. And Iwasawa says the line I keep coming back to: "If she were an adult… No, if she were a boy, I'd gladly have made life hell for the prime minister." Notice how he corrects himself. Her age is a problem that time will fix. Her sex, in his mind, is not. A knock comes at the door: the governor's next engagement, a newspaper's party attended by exactly the friends they've been discussing, is cancelled. Tachibana arranged it.

"Meanwhile Runa, who knew nothing about any of this, had been in a moratorium in the form of a normal school life."

What stays with me is how much love and how much control are mixed together in this chapter. Tachibana protects her from her relatives, from Iwazaki, from the nationalists. He also decides, for her, what she's allowed to know. In Volume 4 her friend Mitsuya named this exact thing: betrayal born of good intentions. Tachibana's version is the best possible one, done so that her choices stay her own. But it's still a decision about her life made in a room she was never invited into, and from here on, the reader knows something about Runa's position that she doesn't.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Trial in absentia**: a trial held without the accused present.
- **Branch families / vassal families**: lesser noble houses descended from, or sworn to, a great house.
- **Noble immunity**: in this world, Japanese nobles' legal protection from arrest; the Koizumi government is moving to abolish it.
- **Earldom**: the rank of earl (count), below a marquess and above a viscount.
- **Zaibatsu**: a family-controlled business conglomerate. The Iwazaki zaibatsu is one of the greatest.
- **Independent director**: a board member from outside the company, there to monitor management.
- **Stopgap**: a temporary arrangement until something permanent is possible.
- **Mackinder's land power theory**: Halford Mackinder's idea (1904) that control of the Eurasian "heartland" is the key to world power.
- **Karafuto**: the Japanese name for Sakhalin. In this world a Soviet-backed communist state, Northern Japan, ruled the Japanese half during the Cold War, and Japan reunified with it in 1994. The island's Russian north is still disputed.
- **Second February 26 Incident**: an in-world coup attempt, named after the real February 26 Incident of 1936, when young army officers tried to seize power in Tokyo.
- **Romanovs**: Russia's imperial family until 1917. Runa is descended from them.
- **Euro**: the European currency; notes and coins entered circulation on 1 January 2002.
- **Fixer**: someone who arranges deals and solves problems through informal, often shady, channels.
- **Shinkansen**: Japan's high-speed "bullet train" lines.
- **Concierge**: a hotel employee who handles guests' requests.

## Chapter 2, part one: The mascot and the flower beds

On 1 September 2002, Runa's newest company is born. Keika Electronics Union, made by merging Japanese and American computer makers, throws its launch party at Kudanshita Keika Tower, her own building, and the founder isn't allowed into the room. She listens from a service corridor that curves around the banquet hall, where the staff can pass unseen, and hears what the guests really think. "All they did was suck up to whoever was in office at the time." "I thought she might go belly-up, given that Koizumi's administration couldn't stand her."

Her adoptive father, the duke, does the greeting for appearances. "My role was to stay quiet and smile as if I were the company mascot." The prime minister's words from Volume 4 come back to her ("You simply aren't qualified. That's what I'm trying to tell you"), and she admits they were "undeniably true. I'd accomplished so much, but there was still no place for me." Then, a little wistfully: "Well, maybe if I hadn't fainted when I saw those terrorist attacks…"

Her maid Tachibana Yuka, the butler's granddaughter and her own age, catches her eavesdropping and marches her off to eat. "We're all so relieved to see your appetite has returned." Runa sips her grape juice and tries to sound profound: "People die when they don't eat, you see…" "Exactly," says Yuka. "But you couldn't even do that until recently, my lady." It's a joke, and it isn't. Only two or three months earlier, she couldn't keep food down.

Later the new company's chief executive, Karin Viola, comes up to tell her the truth she couldn't tell the guests. "Lip service aside, we'll be fighting a war of attrition for a while." The group is really being kept afloat by one healthy member, Shiyo Electric; the telecoms arm is sliding, and the American computer maker needs another acquisition. "If you can be patient three more years, my lady, I'll be sure to make it successful!" Runa laughs, pulls out her little PHS phone, and gives an order: "I'm ordering you to take over the world with cell phones." Karin turns it into a slogan on the spot: "Take over the world with a cell phone even a young lady can use!" Asked how she's feeling, Runa says the sentence that names the whole volume: "I'm just taking a short break from playing grown-up."

And so she goes to school, where the student council turns out to be Volume 4 again, cut down to a size she can survive.

The first job is a donation drive for the school's alumni. Runa, naturally, asks, "Can't I just make the whole donation?" Eiichi, the car-company heir who is her friend and rival, begs her not to: "Next year's student council will suffer for it." Yuujirou, the politician's son, explains why it matters that everyone chips in: "School spirit and the feelings of camaraderie that come from it form this country's upper classes, after all." And Mitsuya, whose father is a Finance Ministry official, adds the picture I can't get out of my head. The school's old boys are a whole faction in Kasumigaseki, Tokyo's government district, and his father complains that whenever they meet, "all they do is get drunk and sing the school song." So instead of a cheque, the four of them pose for the alumni brochure, write appeal copy full of meaningless bureaucratic phrases, and bow politely to every faction until the money comes in. Eiichi sums up the lesson with a proverb: "'The boughs that bear most hang lowest.' I've found out that learning to humble yourself is good experience—at least, since I started running a company with these guys."

The target came from a girl Runa admires, Shisuka Lydia, a year older and already a leader on the junior high's class committee. She left it in a postscript, which Runa reads as both trust and threat. When the four of them report that they've met it, Lydia gives them a compliment that Runa hears as "both praise and a warning": "You four are an extraordinary Quartet, the likes of which we haven't seen in recent years. You'll definitely make history in this country, if not the entire world." Runa also has an uncomfortable thought about her. In the game, Runa rose through the school's committees. Here it's Lydia who is doing that, and Runa suspects "that Lydia-senpai was taking what would've been my path in the game."

The second job is the budget. The elementary council turns out to be the "sub-subcontractor" of the councils above it, and the narration offers a rule worth remembering: "Organizations existed for the purpose of spending money." Club spending has crept up because "a certain idiot had accidentally qualified for a national tournament," in a sport that is little more than her hobby. The idiot, the narration admits, is Keikain Runa, now known around school as the "Comet of the Track and Field Club." Then comes one of the most honest lines she says in the book. She tells the boys she's going her own way, but privately she admits she has "no real plan for the future. If my life went the way it had in the game, I was sure to be ruined, so I'd been focusing on how to survive instead."

The third job is the one I think matters most. Her friends Asuka and Hotaru, the politician's cheerful daughter and the silent girl who never leaves Asuka's side, want to plant flowers in some empty beds on the border with the junior high. Hotaru whispers into Asuka's ear, and Asuka translates: "It's sad that they're empty." The request to transfer the beds is refused. The boys dig, and Mitsuya finds out why: the junior high's beautification committee pays contractors, generously and in separate fees, to maintain twenty "flower beds" that are really bare dirt ringed with stones. The beds are padding a budget, and a transfer would expose it.

And a memory surfaces. In the game, it was the heroine who tried to bring those same beds back to life, and Runa, as the villainess, who worked behind the scenes against her, using the committee's contractors to collect the school's rubbish "for information." Sitting in the council room, she can feel what that other version of herself might have been thinking.

Her instinct is war. Eiichi starts to ask whether she's legally allowed to go and beat the junior high students up, and she sweetly corrects him: "I can beat them up illegally so long as I don't get caught." Then she goes to the whiteboard and writes the goal: *Recover the rights to the flower beds from the junior high students.* Eiichi looks at it and says the sentence that, to me, is the key to the whole volume: "That's not right, Runa. The goal is to be able to use the flower beds."

Once the goal is flowers rather than victory, the answer is easy. Mitsuya suggests planters on elementary-school land, registered as Yuujirou's science homework, with soil and pots borrowed from the biology department. And why planters? "Because we can move them that way, of course." If the junior high complains, the flowers simply move, and it's the committee, not the children, that has something to hide. In the end the committee approves their very first application anyway, and "you almost wondered what the point of the turmoil was in the first place."

Runa still can't resist one extra precaution, which Eiichi asks her to keep "not too extreme." She meets Lydia in the library, the two of them facing a bookshelf and pretending to read, and speaking Russian in case anyone is listening, and gives her the evidence about the committee as a deterrent, one that would hurt everyone involved if it ever came out. Lydia understands the weapon perfectly. "You'll both be in trouble if it comes out. That's your weapon, right? I really hate that side of you, Keikain-san." "And I love the side of you that doesn't hold back when you talk to me, Lydia-senpai." "…Thank you."

Walking back, Runa realises something about herself. "If I wanted to accomplish something, I needed everyone, not just my own willpower. It was the first time I'd recognized that since being born into this world." Outside, Asuka and Hotaru, in tracksuits, hold up geraniums in every colour they could find, and soon the classroom is full of their scent.

The last job is a small, sad one. "We tried, but it didn't change much," Eiichi admits. They've made things run better, but only because they're good at it, which is "hardly a reformation of the system," and next year's council will slide back. The boys turn it into a seminar on Japan: prime ministers who change every year, bureaucrats who carry the state and start to rot ("Absolute power corrupts absolutely," quotes Mitsuya), and conglomerates too weak after the bubble to fix either. Runa's answer is one of the most grown-up things anyone in the volume does with power: "we should at least make a maintenance manual for our underclassmen." Mitsuya does the spreadsheets, Yuujirou the tape-recorded minutes, and Runa a year-at-a-glance wall calendar like the one she saw at the ruling party's office. Eiichi, the president, is left looking around. "Wait… What am I supposed to do?" "Isn't it obvious? Stamp the papers, please!" When Mio, the little girl who calls Runa her big sister, knocks with a stack of documents, Runa tells her they're "working on a little present for your generation."

What I enjoy most about these episodes is how kindly they teach the lesson Koizumi taught cruelly. She offers to pay for everything, and her friends explain that money from one person damages the institution. She reaches for war, and they remind her the goal was flowers. She reforms a council and learns that personal brilliance isn't reform. Every time, she listens. And the flower beds, I think, are a parable of the Iraq argument. She wanted to "recover the rights." The point was to let things grow.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Keika Electronics Union**: Runa's electronics group, formed from Shiyo Electric, Furukawa Telecoms and the American firm Portercon.
- **War of attrition**: a long, grinding struggle won by whoever can hold out longest.
- **PHS**: the Personal Handy-phone System, the cheap mobile phone Runa carries.
- **Quartet**: the school's name for Runa and the three boys, Eiichi, Yuujirou and Mitsuya.
- **Kasumigaseki**: Tokyo's government district, and shorthand for the central bureaucracy.
- **Class committee / beautification committee**: student bodies that run class business and look after the school grounds.
- **Sub-subcontractor**: a firm that does work handed down through two layers of contractors; the joke name for the elementary council.
- **Collusion**: a secret arrangement to cheat, here between a committee and its contractors.
- **Mutually assured destruction**: a standoff in which either side can ruin the other, so neither strikes.
- **Bubble**: Japan's huge rise in land and share prices in the late 1980s, which burst around 1990–91.

## Chapter 2, part two: A goddess, a lost match and a pair of rabbit ears

"Allow me to tell you a story—this story—about gods."

That's how Runa opens the strangest stretch of her school year, and it begins with a pile of thank-you letters. Tachibana, handling her charitable giving, made "an uncharacteristic mistake" and donated to the Orthodox church nearest her home. Now three branches of the Orthodox Church are competing for her: the Tokyo church, cut off from Moscow since the Revolution; the much bigger church of Karafuto, Japan's reunited northern territory; and the Russian Orthodox Church itself, which has noticed her Romanov blood. Japan's new religions, multiplying in the anxious years since the bubble burst, want her face for their posters too. She looks at it all with tired amusement. "No matter which church I joined, I'd start a fight." And besides, "My body notwithstanding, my soul was probably pure Japanese."

And yet. "Despite everything, I felt myself seeking a god. This country was full of gods." As she approaches the end of elementary school, she feels it's "almost time to make that decision for myself."

So she goes looking, and she takes the photographer Ishikawa Nobumitsu with her, the man who has been photographing her for years, and who took the famous picture of her collapsing in the prime minister's arms on the day of the September attacks. In the wedding chapel of her own hotel, she asks him if he's ever believed in a god. "I have. My god is on the other side of this lens. When I capture a moment from that world, I see it as a god. Gods are both good and bad, but you're a goddess to me." At Meiji Shrine, in the lingering summer heat, he says even gods goof off together, since "This country has eight million of them, after all." Tourists stare at the blonde girl with the maids and the camera and don't recognise her, though she's on television constantly. On the expressway, watching the towers slide past, she asks him what makes television so different from photographs. He doesn't know, but he remembers a line from a film: someone once called TV "a god that does nothing."

Then they come to Kawasaki Daishi, a great Buddhist temple south of Tokyo, and the smell of incense, which she finds she doesn't mind at all. She lights a stick, puts her hands together in the main hall, and Ishikawa, kindly, keeps his distance. Afterwards he asks if she was asking for something.

"No. I'm a goddess, after all. I guess I'm saying 'pardon the intrusion,' if anything."

He laughs. Most people who pray, he tells her, "ask to be saved or to have their sins forgiven. You sure don't hear 'pardon the intrusion' much." And the remark lands somewhere deep in her. *So that's why those with power hold on to religion? Because they're aware of their own sins?* She wonders whether she, too, will cling to a god someday. Then Ishikawa ruffles her hair, keeps shooting through her protests, and makes her a promise.

"That's why I want you to stay like you are now, got it? Even if you lose your way, I'll take lots of pictures of who you used to be so that you can make it back home, my goddess."

The photographs run in a weekly magazine and cause a sensation. Runa says nothing when her classmates ask. "I was shocked to see what my face actually looked like in the photos." She doesn't tell us what she saw. I think that silence is the most touching thing in the chapter. In Volume 4 she recorded a message to her future self, and she broke down watching it. Now someone else has taken on that job, keeping a record of the girl she is now, in case the woman she becomes forgets the way back.

From gods, the chapter turns to swords. Runa reaches the final of a regional kendo tournament, the Japanese sport of fencing with bamboo swords, and her opponent is her classmate Takahashi Akiko, a police chief's daughter. Over lunch, Akiko mentions, not realising what she's saying, when she started: "When I was in kindergarten, the dojo master gave me a bamboo sword as a present." That's six years of training to Runa's few.

Runa's teacher is her maid Kitagumo Ryouko, a former spy for Northern Japan, and she teaches what the book calls "Eastern" kendo, the Cold War version, in which sport was a way for socialist states to win prestige without weapons, and science and information were part of the game. Her maids film every competitor. Runa practises her footwork toward the corners of the ring, not the centre. When Yuujirou asked her why, she told him she was working on "scoring a point and then running away until the time is up."

From the stands she can hear her friends, every word. Yuujirou explains what kendo really means, "a way of living through the sword," and says of her style, a little sadly, "I think most kendo practitioners would hate her style if they found out about it, since it's such a waste." (Runa, under her mask: *I can hear all this…*) Her classmate Katsuki Shiori, from a branch family of the Keikains, says in a voice the narration calls "cold and empty" that the maids won't let her lose: "She's the treasure of the Keikain family, after all." And Shiori reports what Akiko said when she was asked if they could film her training. Akiko just laughed: "But I don't think a picture will tell you much. If you want to see everything I can do, you'll have to be there at the match."

The match is quick and honest. Akiko reads Runa's footwork after a single exchange and jumps back from her first attack, then takes the first point on the head. Runa answers with the counter she secretly rehearsed and strikes Akiko's forearm to level the score. The third point isn't shown at all. The match goes to the judges, and they give it to Akiko.

Afterwards a reporter calls her "a Cleopatra of the Heisei era," the queen of the present age, and Runa gives him a throwaway answer that I think is the truest thing she says all year. "I started practicing with a sword for self-defense, so my priority is just to survive. My opponent, Takahashi-san, uses her sword for martial arts and trains strictly to win." Akiko comes over, beaming, and says, "Let's compete again sometime." "Really? I don't want to. I'll just lose." The look on Akiko's face makes Runa laugh, the photographers catch the moment, and the entertainment pages run it under the headline *Young Lady Turns Down Rematch after Championship Defeat*.

It's funny, and it's a diagnosis. Score once, then run out the clock: that's how she has lived her second life, avoiding the bad ending without ever deciding where she wants to go. And this is the first contest in five volumes that she loses on the page, with every advantage money and surveillance can buy, to a girl who has simply kept training since kindergarten.

The rest of this stretch is pure fun. At five to eleven one night she sets out cookies and grape juice, switches on a top-of-the-line computer, and tries to log into a new online game she has fallen for because of one picture: a little priestess in rabbit ears punching a monster. "Connection to server canceled." The game is still in testing. When it works, she spends hours hunting with strangers for the materials to make her own rabbit ears, sitting her character down to rest while passing players cast healing spells on her, until her maid Aki appears behind her at nearly one in the morning: "Time for bed, my lady." "All right, I'll stop for tonight. But I'm coming back for you, rabbit ears, so wait for me there!" The narration adds, with a wistful sideways look at the future, "At this point in history, online games were still harmonious places." When the servers go down at the worst possible moment, she ends up buying her rabbit ears from another player's shop.

Then she goes shopping for real, in an Akihabara arcade, with Okazaki, her fund manager, lighting a cigarette beside her. Between rounds of a shooting game she can't beat (a helpful fan explains the game's hidden difficulty trick), and of a light-gun game about military satellites, the two of them settle two deals. She'll buy Zugagaga Entertainment, a video game company that has just given up making consoles, for about ¥50 billion: "I'll manage them out of my own pocket for a while." "You sure have lots of pocket change." And, because the game's plot reminded her, they agree to put ¥200 billion through the Moonlight Fund into GLONASS, Russia's broke satellite navigation system. She imagines herself as the final boss of a video game, "howling with laughter at a rocket launch site," with Okazaki as her miniboss. Rumour has it that when Zugagaga's staff cheered at the chance to make hardware again, Angela and Karin went straight to their headquarters to set them straight.

And over lunch one day, the girls each name a song they love, while the school's broadcasting committee plays music through the speakers. Then a summery, ethereal tune comes on that everyone admires and nobody can place. Runa knows it, and smiles very hard. It's famous in Akihabara, fans will one day jokingly call it Japan's "new national anthem," and it comes from "a game that people under eighteen weren't allowed to play." The committee had broadcast someone's personal tape by mistake. Adults who didn't know where the song came from didn't scold anyone, and "those who knew kept their mouths shut."

I like that the chapter lets her be a child here: losing, staying up too late, buying things she shouldn't. But look at what each episode is about. Churches, sects, a photographer and, soon, a president all want her face. Her answer at the temple, "pardon the intrusion," is a refusal to be either worshipped or forgiven. She would rather be a guest.

- **Romanovs**: Russia's imperial family until 1917. Runa is descended from them.
- **Meiji Shrine**: Tokyo's great Shinto shrine to the Emperor Meiji.
- **Kawasaki Daishi**: a large Shingon Buddhist temple in Kawasaki, linked to the monk Kūkai, who was born on Shikoku.
- **Kendo**: Japanese fencing with bamboo swords; points are scored by clean strikes to the head, wrist or torso.
- **Dojo**: a hall where a martial art is taught.
- **"Eastern" kendo**: the book's name for a win-at-all-costs style shaped by Cold War sport.
- **Heisei era**: Japan's era name from 1989 to 2019.
- **Akihabara**: Tokyo's electronics and otaku district.
- **Moonlight Fund**: Runa's private investment fund, the real source of her wealth, run by Okazaki.
- **GLONASS**: Russia's satellite navigation system, its version of GPS.

## Chapter 2, part three: Be the one to bury the people you love

After the flower beds and the rabbit ears, Chapter 2 sits Runa down with two grown men who tell her, in very different ways, what her defeat in Volume 4 actually meant. One is an economics professor. The other is the president of the United States.

She starts with Professor Kanbe, whose seminar she has been attending at his university, and she starts lightly. "I have no desire to fight the prime minister. I'm trying to find a point of compromise between us, but right now, I can't figure out what's going on in his head at all." Kanbe smiles. "It would be dreadful to see this country's two greatest geniuses tear each other down. Let me offer you some advice, since I'm an adult." She pouts at that last word, and he notices, because her age is the whole quarrel.

Then he picks up a marker and turns her patriotism into medieval history. He writes an old phrase on the whiteboard: "Favors and services." It's the bond that held Japan's warrior society together in the Middle Ages. A lord granted his vassal land and protection, and in return the vassal fought for him. Then, in red, Kanbe writes what Runa has been doing instead: "Forcing your services." Picture a builder who turns up uninvited, puts a new roof on your house, and then sends you the bill. That, he says, is a child of a great provincial house "forcing the shogunate to purchase her services before she even comes of age. Don't you think the bakufu and shogun would be amused?"

She's interested now. Which great house is she, then? Kanbe chooses the family that once ran the shogunate from behind the throne. "That said, I'm comparing you to Hosokawa Masamoto, of course." Runa catches the reference at once. "Are you saying I should start another Meio Incident, Professor?" In 1493, Hosokawa Masamoto deposed the shogun and installed a puppet of his own. Kanbe doesn't finish the story, but anyone who knows it will feel a chill. Masamoto had no children of his own, adopted several heirs, and in 1507 was murdered by his own retainers in the quarrel over which of them would succeed him. I suspect that's not an accident in a book whose first chapter was a family council about Runa's inheritance.

Kanbe's point is practical. If she wanted a coup, "you should've done it in spring of 2002." Now there's nobody left to put forward. "The prime minister has eliminated any potential puppet who could stand center stage in your place while you're still a minor." When Runa asks, half offended, what exactly he thinks of her, she catches herself mid-protest: *He really made me sound like a villainess… Ah, wait. That's exactly what I am.*

Then he says something nobody in her life has said aloud: "A mere child would never get involved in the Iraq situation." Her maid Ichijou Erika, sitting in, is startled. Runa wipes the expression off her face. He knows. America's war preparations, he says, have "helped ease the pain of the IT bubble bursting," and two of the most senior men in the cabinet "were completely shocked that you predicted the war in Iraq and prepared your investments accordingly." So he states the real issue in one sentence: "Should one approve of a child who hasn't even come of age holding up the enemy's head on the battlefield?"

Erika, who has a gift for saying exactly the wrong thing at exactly the right moment, asks, "So it would've been fine if she came of age first…?" And Kanbe claps his hands. "Exactly! That's the root of the entire issue! Keikain-kun, everything would be solved if you came of age and became an adult." Adulthood in Japan begins at twenty. Killing outside of war is a crime, so would she be tried as an adult? Runa starts to say she could always fall back on her noble immunity, and stops, because Koizumi is already abolishing it. "The prime minister isn't bullying you," Kanbe says. "If you're going to insist on special rights, then you have to shoulder that responsibility. If you want to be seen as an adult, you must act like an adult."

And he tells her the one road Koizumi has left open. It's the most chilling advice in the volume, and he gives it gently.

"Drive your father and brother into retirement and inherit the dukedom yourself. Once you have the dukedom, take control of the Privy Council if you want to stand on equal footing with the prime minister. Be the one to bury the people you love. If you do all that, I think the prime minister will be willing to listen to you."

Before she can answer, he draws two triangles. The ruling party governs through one: "politicians, bureaucrats, and financiers." The opposition, freshly regrouped as the Democratic Allied Fellowship, runs on another: "politicians, mass media, and agitators." Activists stir up an issue, television spreads it, and politicians wave it at the government. It's a machine built by television, and its members are, as he puts it, "actors and the Diet as their stage." Runa teases him that the ruling party performs just as much, and he agrees, and names its star. "Prime Minister Koizumi. Just as you say, he's currently the greatest 'actor' of our generation."

Then he lays out the policy map. Koizumi's line is to focus on the cities, cut public spending and stay close to America. His great enemies inside his own party, the Hashizume faction, stand for the opposite: money for the countryside, more public works, and closer ties with continental Asia. "Exactly. Keikain-kun, those are the same policies you've adopted." And she sees it. Her empire began in Sakata, grew through Hokkaido, and lives on regional railways, department stores and supermarkets. And then there's her blood. Her hand goes to her blonde hair: "I could never escape the curse of the continent either." Her quarrel with Koizumi isn't personal. She has been financing the old Japan he exists to dismantle.

Television, Kanbe warns, is waiting for the showdown, and "the public will just consume you as a form of entertainment." Her reply comes instantly. "You're telling me to abandon my father and brother, all for that fiction? I'll have to refuse."

He's satisfied, and then he does something unexpected. He stops talking about power and talks about her. "Power is a solitary thing. Or, rather, you can't make use of power unless you're solitary." To dance with the prime minister she'd have to give up not only her family "but also your friends and partners." And then: "Keikain-kun, you should think more about your own well-being. Let me be blunt. Your goal should be to find your own happiness, not this country's." It's hard, she admits, to answer when an adult speaks to you with a face like that. "I'd truly met some wonderful people, but I knew that I was constantly betraying them." He goes on: "A single genius isn't enough to save us." Two genius actors, one stage. "Keikain-kun, you've debuted too early." Is she still ready to step up? "I'm not going to do that. I think you've forgotten, Professor, but I'm still in elementary school."

That night she goes to the family manor and tells her adoptive father what the professor said: that to fight Koizumi she should push him and her brother aside, and that she refused. The duke's face shows neither shock nor anger, only relief. "Runa, have you finally had enough…?" And she gives the answer that closes Volume 4's war for good: "I haven't had enough, but I don't want to go against the times." Her own verdict follows: "This era was choosing Prime Minister Koizumi, not me."

The president's call comes in November. Runa has backed his party in the American midterm elections, "in part as an apology for the American IT bubble bursting," and it has kept the House, won the Senate and taken about thirty governorships. "Thank you, Your Little Majesty," he says, using her American nickname. He's kind. Koizumi isn't a bad guy, he tells her; she simply couldn't handle such a weight, and isn't old enough for it yet. And Runa asks him the question she has been carrying since the summer: "I was thinking about death like some vague concept. What I couldn't handle was the fact that my actions would kill hundreds of thousands, even millions, of people. Mr. President, how can you shoulder the weight of that burden?" "Well, that's exactly why I repent to God."

Then she sees what the adults have done with her. The report on the September attacks that went to Congress argues the case for war in a new way: not that Iraq *has* weapons of mass destruction, but that it *intended* to get them. In this world Iraq really did try to buy a nuclear weapon from the former Soviet Union, and the plots she helped stop, a train hijacking in Denver and a failed attack in Tokyo, are presented as its attempts to get nuclear material, with the September attacks as the diversion. And Runa, the girl who helped prevent nuclear terrorism and fainted at the news of the attacks, in a photograph the whole world saw, is the campaign's poster girl. "The adults wouldn't let me be stained with blood; it seemed they'd chosen to treat me like a beautiful doll instead." They have "chipped away at my power while leaving my assets and my status as a symbol untouched."

The president tells her why. "If you show children a wicked future, you've failed as an adult." And then, with the honesty of a man who has made his choice: "Your Little Majesty, when I occupy the chair I'm sitting in as I call you, I'm prepared to sacrifice hundreds, thousands, maybe even tens of thousands of lives from other countries if it means saving a single American. Despite your nickname, you're not in charge of a country. If that changes someday, remember what I'm saying to you. I'm talking about the difference between a company and a nation."

She hears two things in it. He is willing to accept a massacre as the lesser evil. And, if the world asked it of her, she might be allowed to rule a country one day. Then a third thought comes, from her first life, and it has no answer: *If countries' rulers must protect their citizens at all costs, like the president says, why did I die in my past life?* She thanks him, and hopes she'll understand before she grows up.

The chapter ends with her alone with the thought. The game only showed the villainess's life until she was eighteen. "For the first time, I had to think about my own future."

"What kind of adult was I going to become?"

"And, before that, would I become an adult at all?"

I find those two lines almost unbearably sad, and I think they're the real end of Volume 4. Every adult in this chapter sees a role for her: the professor describes the road to a dukedom, television wants a duel, the president imagines a throne someday. The only one who offers her a life is the professor, in one sentence, and the only one who is simply glad she has stopped is her father. But look at the price Kanbe names, "be the one to bury the people you love." Hold on to it. In the last chapter of this book, we'll meet the girl who paid it.

## Chapter 3: The monster on the silver screen

Chapter 3 begins at a food stall on the edge of the city, late at night, with a famous film director drunk and furious. "I KEEP SAYING IT—I want them to let me use her in a movie! How dare those sponsors tell me no?!"

His name is Shirosaki Kouji, a director with prizes from abroad, and his drinking companion is the idol scout who once tried to make Runa a pop star. Shirosaki discovered her watching a rerun of *Imperial Bodyguards*, the late-night police drama her group backs and sometimes appears in, and he has seen what nobody else has. "Everyone's got tunnel vision over her looks and singing voice, but those are just the start of things! The girl is a natural-born actress." She has spent her whole life, he says, playing the part of a noblewoman "without ever once breaking character. She already sees this world as a stage."

He has no idea how right he is. She really has known since birth that she's living inside a script.

"She's a monster," he goes on. "She won't resemble anyone else, nor will anyone influence her. It's my duty—the movie industry's duty—to preserve her on the silver screen!" Money won't tempt her, and she doesn't need connections. The only way in is the planned *Imperial Bodyguards* film, so he'll direct that, a job he'd normally despise. "What I'd hate more than anything would be someone worse than me having the chance to film her."

Then the narration jumps ahead and tells us how it turns out: the film becomes a worldwide hit, "a historic work of Japanese cinema," and "the Academy Award for Best Supporting Actress would have her name on it someday."

The shoot itself is the funniest thing in the volume. Runa signed on for a cameo, and expects to "just sing and be protected." Shirosaki lies to her that the script isn't finished and invites her to film whatever scenes she'd like, fully intending to rebuild the story around them. So she hands him an airsoft gun, a toy that fires plastic pellets. "Would you mind shooting me with that, Director?" He fires, and she draws her own gun and shoots his pellet out of the air. She can deflect them with a blade too, she adds; the idol scout taught her. *That's right, my lady,* the director thinks. *In movies, monsters can act like monsters.* When he explains that real guns and explosions are a headache under Japanese regulations, she has the obvious answer: "Very well. In that case, let's film in Hollywood. I'll handle the bills!"

Somewhere in the middle of all this, we overhear her secretary Angela, a former CIA officer, being gently rebuked by her old superiors, including a man the book calls the CIA's "Head of Foreign Leadership Analysis." "I'm pleased that you've taught the young lady about the dangers of violence. But I don't remember ever telling you to turn her into a comic book heroine." The reply is cheerfully unrepentant: "Exactly! Thank you so much, Chief! Next time, we'll try doing wire fu!" And from the set we hear Runa: "Over budget?! I haven't even spent a billion yen yet! I'll pay you with my pocket change, so please bring us lots of explosives! I want to blow up a building next!"

At the wrap party, Shirosaki asks the photographer Ishikawa why nobody in her entourage ever stopped the shoot. Ishikawa's answer is simple and, I think, quietly devastating: "At the end of the day, none of them really knew her. That's why they couldn't stop it." Her secretary had let slip that "My lady puts too much of herself into everything she does." Through the lens, they had all watched her grin like an innocent child.

Then Shirosaki explains what he thinks he has done, and it's the chapter's big idea. "If I'd just ignored her, she could've turned into much more than a monster. Maybe even a dictator. But she showed me the extent of her monstrousness. Monsters are monsters because their true nature is unknown, first and foremost. People will eventually get a handle on her mysterious nature and counter it." He clenches his fist. "I took her, I took this era, and I sealed them into the silver screen!" He believes that filming her has made her knowable, and therefore safe. Her group later forbids him to claim any compensation for the awards the film sweeps, but Runa keeps hiring him anyway: "After all, no one in the world can film me better than he can."

Next comes the television industry. At the station that airs *Imperial Bodyguards*, she agrees to appear on a late-night special aboard her own cruise ship. Then the head of programming begs her, yet again, to move the show to prime time. She refuses, as always. Her group pays for the show and buys all its advertising, which gives it freedom; prime time would mean several sponsors, the big advertising agencies and the loss of that control. Then she looks at him and names what he really wants. "You don't just want *Imperial Bodyguards*," she says. "You want me too, isn't that right?" Then, in a sweet childish voice, she mentions that she's "heard" a few of her companies might like to sponsor the station's money-losing late-night slots. He bows. She leaves without looking back.

After she's gone, the station's star newscaster says what he actually wants. "I just want to see what the young lady is hiding behind her mask." He wants a showdown with Koizumi, and he knows why there hasn't been one: "It's all because of Iraq. The U.S. is trying to cool the antagonism between the prime minister and young lady." The narration adds a striking claim, that Koizumi and Runa "were the only two people in Japan who could object to the United States." And then the newscaster gives the speech that made my skin crawl. "The people want entertainment, not facts and real life. What could be more entertaining than someone else's war?" Japan's own Self-Defense Forces are about to deploy to the Gulf, and he still calls it someone else's war. "Besides, you know what people love? The story of Icarus." The boy whose wax wings melted when he flew too close to the sun. "If they fight it out, maybe we can show everyone the moment when one stumbles and loses everything! It'd be even better to stir them up and see both of them sink in the end."

The narration answers him with a prophecy of its own. "The Koizumi Theater's show was going for an extended run. Its villain, the young lady, would stand her ground as opposition, despite being continuously attacked all the while."

Then we get the film, through a breathless review. *Imperial Bodyguards: The Tomboy Lady's Improv Theater* follows bodyguards who discover that the girl they protect "is striking down evil in the name of restoring the world order." They try to stop her, then fight beside her when terrorists target her. There are bullets shot out of the air, a bulletproof umbrella used as a sword, a leap from an exploding building filmed in America, and Runa singing "Micaela's Aria" from *Carmen*. And the review notices the film's social argument. The heroine can do all this because she has noble immunity, so the police can't touch her, and she throws it back at them: "Let's say what I'm doing is evil. After failing to track down all those evildoers I punished, can you really sit there and call yourselves 'just'?" The villain is a young Russian from the annexed north, one of the people the mainland calls "second-class citizens," and he gets the film's most desperate line: "We have nothing! No money, no country, and no pride! So we'll steal everything this country has!" A real private military company staged the gunfights, American forces in Japan play her rescuers, the American president lends his voice to a phone call, and the United States has to raise the film's age rating to stop American children copying her.

Two interviews follow, and they're a masterclass in polite evasion. To Japanese television, after her Academy Award nomination, she says she turned down every film offer because school comes first, and that she did her own stunts because "it's hard to find a child stuntwoman." She's being called "a twenty-first century Sarah Bernhardt," after the great French actress. To the foreign press, every question is political: the Russian throne, the Romanov treasure, Florida, the wars. She bats each one away. "Well, I'm actually just a Japanese person named Keikain Runa." "A Japanese elementary school student controlling American politics? You couldn't even get away with something so silly in a movie." "Why would any grown man with the title 'prime minister' obey whatever silly things a child had to say?" She does admit, carefully, that the Moonlight Fund uses "a private Swiss bank account."

The chapter's real heart is a quiet conversation in a greenroom before another television interview. Shirosaki, for once completely serious, asks her, "What depths do you plan to sink to next?" Then he says something I keep turning over. "Most people in this world will never amount to anything, my lady. They call themselves 'adults' and pretend that it means something. You, however, will definitely become someone whose name is remembered by history."

"Master one thing, and people call you 'great.' Master two things, and people call you 'a genius with a gift from above'. But do you know what people think when you master three things or more?" He pauses, and says it slowly. "They fear you sold your soul to the devil." Runa, who has an empire, a dukedom, the Romanov bloodline, a coloratura voice and now a film career, can only "chuckle sadly" and admit that it isn't an unreasonable explanation.

Then he gives her his warning, looking at her reflection in the mirror instead of at her. "Monsters in movies pretty much always end up the same way," he murmurs. "They either kill people, or they're killed by people. In special cases, a monster turns back into a human. The gimmick that does it is 'love.'" For her, he sees only a few endings. "You'll rule as a queen, or the masses will overthrow you as a dictator…" And then, as a joke: "Or maybe getting married will turn you from a monster into a woman." "That's sexual harassment," she says. Later, half teasing, she asks whether that makes her Audrey Hepburn. "I'm sure you'd have shone in *Roman Holiday* too." That's the film in which a princess escapes her duties for one perfect day in Rome, and then goes back.

The narration lingers on this scene more than any other. Runa "would always be grateful to Director Shirosaki for hinting what her final choice should be," though she has yet to learn that his way of using her in his films "would become a real headache in her life."

The chapter closes on Professor Kanbe as a television commentator, briskly summing up the winter. Japan has pledged to join Britain behind America in Iraq. The Russian-Japanese summit on Karafuto, he says, is really about lining up Moscow on Iraq. Keika Holdings, her bank, is under pressure to list its shares on the stock market. And the Privy Council, "a bastion of fifty nobles," is fighting the abolition of its privileges. Then the anchor cuts to a commercial, promising a story about "the failed terrorist attack on the Shinjuku Geofront."

I think this chapter is the volume's study of Runa as an image, and every adult in it has a theory about what to do with a prodigy. The director films her so the world can understand her. The newscaster wants to film her fall. The CIA watches. Only the photographer just looks. And look at the film they make: it's the war she wasn't allowed to fight, the heroine punishing evil with immunity no one can touch, shot in the very winter her own immunity is being taken away. She can't do it, so she's allowed to act it.

The three endings Shirosaki gives her, queen, overthrown dictator, or a monster made human by love, are flagged by the narrator as pointing to her future. Every one of them already has a sponsor. Kanbe described the first, the newscaster is hoping for the second, and since Volume 4 nearly every grown-up who loves her has been gently suggesting the third.

## Chapter 4, part one: A brother who has plenty

The title of Chapter 4 is *The Lady Spectates*, and it's exact. Runa comes back to work in the winter of 2002–03, and in scene after scene she's asked to approve, veto or forgive things other people have already set in motion. The first is a drug company, and it opens with a casual question over lunch: "COME TO THINK OF IT, what's going on with Father's company merger?"

The lunch itself tells you how far she has come. Her executive lunches used to be humble meals with two men, Tachibana and her banker Ichijou. Now they fill the main hall of her hotel, with directors from five main companies and a few dozen subsidiaries. "Father's company" is the oldest of them, the family's drug maker, Keika-Iwazaki Pharma, which is about to merge with a third firm, Hatabe Pharmaceuticals. And her secretary Angela has heard a rumour: a "megapharma," one of the giant Western drug companies, wants to buy the merged firm.

Over pudding, Angela explains why. The first reason is ordinary: buying a Japanese company is the fastest way into Japan's market. The second is darker. The company supplies medicine to the American forces in Japan and to the Self-Defense Forces, Japan's military, and both are "secretly stocking up on medicine." Runa feels sick. That can only mean Iraq. "Medicine to combat chemical weapons?" "I'll leave that to your imagination." And the third reason, Angela says, is Runa herself. Since she stepped back from the group's leadership, Wall Street looks at it from outside and thinks the drug company "could be cut off" from the rest.

Her brother Nakamaro, who runs the pharma side, comes in with the full story. One of the start-ups he signed contracts with on a trip to New York has produced a drug that's "getting a whole lot of buzz," and "It went too well." The giants want the drug so badly they want the whole company. Two of them are circling. One, Charles & Earhart, plans to offer about ¥1.3 trillion for all three firms, which are worth about ¥1 trillion on the market, and to pay in its own shares rather than cash, once Japan's parliament allows that kind of share swap. If it went hostile instead, it could probably buy a majority for ¥600–800 billion.

Here's why that's possible. The merged company's shares are split 4:3:3 among Keika, Iwazaki and Hatabe. Think of a house owned by three siblings, one with four-tenths and two with three-tenths each. The biggest owner runs the house, but if an outsider persuades the other two to sell, the outsider owns it. That's what "hostile" means: going around management and buying from the other owners.

The other suitor, Arts Nova, wants something gentler, copied from a deal that has just shocked the industry. A European giant called Eflaro has taken 50.1 percent of a Japanese drug maker, Gainai, enough to control it, while promising that Gainai keeps its name, its managers and its listing on the Tokyo Stock Exchange. It's a takeover dressed as a partnership.

Runa does what she always does. She offers money: the Moonlight Fund, her private treasury, can pay for a defence. And her brother says the sentence that turns the scene upside down. "You see, Runa, I'm actually the one eager to take this offer up."

He explains with real patience. Iwazaki's people resent that Keika, the young upstart, holds the reins of a company Iwazaki built up over decades, and the merger with Hatabe was partly their bid for a comeback. A foreign owner would give everyone "a common enemy," and an organisation pulls together when it has one. Then he gets serious. "To be more serious, this future is also your future." Keika Holdings, Keika Railway, Keika Corp, all of it, will need to go public. "The Keika Group is too large for you to carry all on your own. Then again, if you're going to take on the world in battle, those companies are still too small. The government will take action to dismantle the zaibatsu someday." The great family conglomerates are finished. The only question is how to survive their end.

She asks him, half joking and half not, whether he'd lead the whole group in her place. His answer is gentle, and it has a blade in it.

"I have plenty already, Runa. Since you don't have enough yet, we'll end up clashing someday. At the very least, the Keikain family will secure enough funds to last up to your generation and the generation of the child in Sakurako-san's belly, so you can have your fun for now without any worries. I won't get in the way as long as you don't do anything dangerous."

There's a lot in that paragraph, and I didn't take all of it in on first reading, because the last part hides the first. His wife Sakurako is pregnant. Runa is overwhelmed with happiness, and the reason is the most moving thought in the chapter. In the game, Nakamaro died. "Someone who hadn't been alive in the game story was going to bring forth a new life. It was like an affirmation of everything I'd done to this point. I was so happy." Every bank she rescued, every risk she took, comes down to this: a child who would never have existed.

But go back to the first sentence. "I have plenty… Since you don't have enough yet, we'll end up clashing someday." It isn't a threat. It's a diagnosis of appetite, said with love, by the heir to the dukedom about the sister who already owns far more than he does. And he has just announced that there will be another heir after him.

The deal goes Arts Nova's way: it takes 50.1 percent and the company keeps its name and managers. The Keikain family sells half its own stake for ¥200 billion, and the narration tells us that "a certain young lady" means to use that money to prepare things for her unborn nephew: "she'd been turned into a doting aunt."

The second scene is in her tower's huge meeting room, before more than a hundred managers from the trading companies being merged into her new Keika Corp. They have more than ten thousand clients to sort out, and she has bought a building to do it in. Her explanation is pure household wisdom: "To clean up something big, start with a room you aren't using, fill it with all the things that need to be organized, then work on emptying that room." The spare room is Kansai Airport Rinkai Tower, the skyscraper on the reclaimed land opposite Kansai Airport, built in the bubble and left as a monument to bad debt.

Okazaki, her fund manager, handles the questions smoothly. No, they won't touch the airport's own troubled expansion; yes, the tower helps the region's small borrowers indirectly. Then a manager from one of the absorbed companies, a man named Tenman Hashimitsu, asks whether the group's "resource management department" will leave the Kudanshita headquarters. That's the polite name inside the trading company for the Moonlight Fund. Okazaki says it will stay. "In terms of governance," Tenman replies, "it seems like it'd be best to define the resource management department firmly. But I understand. Go ahead."

The room turns to look at Runa. She keeps smiling "like a mask." Inside, she reasons: "If I fired an executive for this level of meddling and provocation, I'd be no different than a dictator." Afterwards Toudou, the old trading-company boss who runs the merger, tells her she should be pleased: "It seems like you've found an ambitious person to succeed me."

I like that small scene a great deal, because it's the first time a salaried manager has pressed, in public, on the oddest thing in her empire. Her group is becoming an ordinary company, with shareholders and governance and people who ask questions. And an ordinary company has no comfortable place for a private treasury owned by a child.

Both scenes are about the same squeeze. Her brother sees it clearly: the group is too big for a family and too small for the world, and he chooses to become a rich shareholder in someone else's company. She, by temperament, never will. And I can't help noticing where she has put her spare room. In the game, the last image of the original Keikain Runa is of her alone at Kansai Airport. The present Runa has just bought the bankrupt tower that looks across at it.

## Chapter 4, part two: A demon whispering in her ear

This is the scene the whole series has been walking toward since its very first page, and it arrives in a polite question. Angela, about to take over the North American business of Runa's securities company, has just explained how she plans to make money. Then she asks:

"My lady, are you familiar with subprime loans?"

To Runa it "sounded like a demon whispering in my ear." The series opened on 15 September 2008, the night Lehman Brothers collapsed and the world's financial system nearly went with it. Subprime loans are the thing that caused that night, and Runa is the only person in the room who knows it. She has to pretend she's never heard the word. "Only I was aware that those were a trap that would lead to destruction."

So let me walk through Angela's pitch the way she does, one step at a time, because the book gets it remarkably right, and because each step is a little worse than the one before.

It starts with the loans themselves. After the September attacks and the dot-com crash, America cut interest rates very low, partly as a wartime measure, and the country is awash with cheap money. In America, Angela says, two things give you status: a car and a house. Banks happily lend for both to "prime" borrowers, people with good credit. Hardly anyone lends to the rest. That's the opportunity. "Subprime" simply means below prime: loans to people with poor credit.

Ichijou, Runa's banker, looks at the numbers and nearly falls off his chair. "An 8 percent annual interest rate on a mortgage with no down payment?! That's insanity!" The narration explains his horror with a banker's rule of thumb. A high interest rate isn't only a price; it's a warning. The lender is charging that much because it expects a lot of people not to pay. It's like the insurance premium for a reckless driver: the size of the premium tells you what the insurer expects to happen.

Angela has an answer. The payments will be kept low for the first four years and then jump in the fifth. By then, if house prices have gone up, the borrower can simply take out a new loan against the more valuable house and pay off the old one. Do you see the catch? The whole thing only works if house prices keep rising. The borrower is betting on it, and so is the lender. Runa, who has heard all this before, notes drily: "During Japan's bubble, I heard that all the time. Well, actually, I'd just been a baby."

The next step is the mixing. To make the loans look safer, Angela says, you mix them with good ones. Combine an 8 percent loan with a 1 percent loan and you get something that looks like two 4.5 percent loans. And Ichijou, who watched Japanese land prices fall everywhere at once in the 1990s, answers with the best single line on the 2008 crisis I've read in a novel: "That's like combining a glass of wine with a glass of mud; neither will be drinkable." Mixing only protects you if the bad loans go bad one at a time. If house prices fall everywhere together, the mixture spoils all at once.

Then comes the walking away. Angela isn't worried, because she doesn't plan to be holding the loans when that happens. "So let's walk away with only the commissions on the loan sales." She'll borrow cheaply in Japan, where interest rates are close to zero, lend in America, bundle the loans with safe assets like government bonds, and sell the bundles to investors around the world as securities. The servicing (collecting the monthly payments) can be sold off too. "We'll collect only the commission and take on no risk."

If you remember Ichijou's pudding lesson from Volume 1, this is the pudding again. Back then he taught a five-year-old Runa what bad debt is by buying her a pudding for ten thousand yen that nobody would buy back for more than a hundred. Angela's plan is to wrap the pudding in beautiful paper, sell it on at the full price, and be gone before anyone opens the box.

"And you're okay with this?!" Runa can't hold it in. Angela is untroubled. "You're a kind person, my lady… However, if both parties agree on these deals, they're proper transactions, even if they're basically a grift." Ichijou mutters, "They're more like a trick played while gambling."

After that, a stamp and a safety net. The bundles will need a stamp of approval, so Angela will ask the ratings agencies, the firms that grade how safe an investment is, to give them their top grade, AAA. The narration notes sourly that those same ratings "were useless during the IT bubble's collapse and the Russian currency crisis," and that people trust them anyway. And in case "the unthinkable happens," she'll buy insurance on the securities. "My lady, insurance is the greatest gamble one can take in life." If the president is re-elected, she says, "these loans will continue to sell until 2008." The target is $100 billion of loans, at 1 percent commission: a billion dollars of profit. "That way, no one can complain when I become CEO of Keika Holdings."

Now the plan's second layer appears. Ichijou asks the question that turns my stomach. Other Japanese banks will see what Keika is doing and copy it, less carefully. What then? Here the novel makes its sharpest point about its own alternate history. In real life, the narration says, Japanese banks mostly stayed out of the subprime boom, because "their hands were full with bad debt disposal." But in this world Runa helped them clean up years early. They're healthy, and free to walk straight into the next disaster. Angela welcomes it. "As always, Keika Holdings just has to take over any financial institution that sustains a fatal blow, right? We can make a megabank that can compete with the rest of the world, just like you used to want, CEO Ichijou." Let the others blow themselves up, then buy what's left.

And then the third. "Tell me, Angela. Is the U.S. government ordering you to do this?" "Good question. The government's given me some orders." She is to "teach financial systems in Japan a bit of Wall Street logic." When Keika Holdings lists its shares on the stock market, it will issue new ones to a chosen foreign investor, opening the door to foreign capital. Runa remembers a conspiracy theory from her first life, that Wall Street set up Japan's banks to take the fall in the bubble. It's "a conspiracy theory I couldn't laugh at any longer."

Then the scene turns, on a misunderstanding. Runa asks whether the bomb going off is part of Angela's orders too. Angela is puzzled. Surely it'll be Wall Street's own fault if Wall Street gets blown up? She doesn't think it'll go off before 2009, "you can probably conduct yourself as an adult by that point," and then she sees the faces in front of her, and asks, in honest disbelief:

"What… You mean you don't want to short it?"

To "short" something is to bet that its price will fall. Angela had assumed the whole plan was a present: sell the loans, then bet against them, and make money on the way up and on the way down. "It's the rebirth of King Midas," the king whose touch turned everything to gold. Runa's anger melts into something sadder. Angela wasn't being cynical. She was being loyal, in the only currency she trusts.

And Runa draws her line. I think it's one of the most important things she says in the series, because she doesn't pretend to be clean. "Having come as far as I have, I've resorted to sinister means of making money. I know I've sent those whose top priority is money to hell." Then: "This plan will cause too much damage, though. I won't be the one who pulls that trigger."

Angela's reply is the saddest line in the chapter. "But investment banks on Wall Street are already on board. The funds you burned while you were playing with fire are particularly eager. Whatever you decide, my lady, that trigger is going to be pulled." The vulture funds Runa ruined when she brought down America's internet bubble are now hungry to win their money back on subprime. Her past has built part of her future.

So she compromises, because she has to. The Moonlight Fund is watched by politicians, by financiers and by the CIA, and this bubble is, as the narration puts it, "the plan of the United States." Everyone has to get on board "to some extent." Angela offers to aim for ¥50 billion of car loans instead of mortgages. Ichijou tightens it further: a consortium, a group of lenders sharing the risk, raising ¥100 billion, with Keika's share capped at ¥30 billion; loans for Japanese cars only; cleared with the trade and foreign ministries; and announced "as a means of supporting the United States." It's a fig leaf, and Runa knows it ("But you'll still keep up with it"), though as fig leaves go, it covers the safest corner of the market.

Then Ichijou, who has been on her side all along, turns to Runa herself. He puts a chart on the wall. "WTI jumped to forty dollars over predictions that war will break out in Iraq, but a certain fund keeps buying cheap twenty-dollar shares from Russia using futures options."

WTI is the American benchmark price of oil. The "certain fund" is hers. Here's how it works. The Moonlight Fund holds a mountain of Russian government bonds, and instead of being repaid in dollars it has taken payment in options on Russian oil: the right to buy crude at about $20 a barrel. With war coming, the market price is near $40. So the fund buys at twenty and sells near forty, not to Japan but to Europe, which gets Russian oil by pipeline. Five billion dollars in the first quarter alone. The fund is said to be involved in 20 percent of the world's crude and 60 percent of Russia's. And Ichijou says it plainly: "Prime Minister Koizumi sees you as his enemy precisely because you've been doing this for so long, starting even before the war."

That one sentence rewrites Volume 4 for me. We were told the quarrel with Koizumi was moral, a child and a war. Ichijou says it's also about money. She has been profiting from the approach of the war, and selling Russian oil to the very Europeans who oppose it.

The scene ends with Angela, suddenly expressionless. "Okay, Boss," she tells Ichijou. "I'll join you on this train of yours." Afterwards Ichijou explains what Runa missed: Angela was competing with Okazaki, "the dealer who brought you into a huge operation that turned out successful. Wouldn't it be more surprising if she weren't jealous of him?"

What stays with me is the shape of the choice. In every earlier crisis, knowing the future made Runa rich. Here, for the first time, it's a reason not to act, and she refuses the trade anyway, knowing it will happen without her. Her virtue is real, and it's partial. She won't pull the trigger, but she'll stand near the gun, because standing too far away has a price too.

- **Short / short-selling**: betting that a price will fall.
- **King Midas**: the mythical king whose touch turned everything to gold, including his food.
- **Vulture funds**: investors who buy distressed assets cheaply, hoping to profit.
- **Consortium**: a group of firms sharing a venture and its risk.
- **CIA**: the Central Intelligence Agency, America's foreign intelligence service.
- **WTI**: West Texas Intermediate, the American benchmark price for crude oil.
- **Option**: the right, but not the duty, to buy or sell something at a fixed price.

**Precision pointers**
- *"An insurance premium for a reckless driver"* → my analogy. The narration says an 8% rate is "an eight percent chance that the ten million yen won't come back"; strictly, a lending rate covers expected loss (chance of default times the share you fail to recover), plus funding cost and profit. (Vol. 5 map, Part II §4c)
- *"Payments low for four years, then a jump"* → the real products were mostly "2/28" and "3/27" loans, with a two- or three-year teaser rate before the reset. (Vol. 5 map, Part II §4a)
- *"You mix them with good ones"* → the novel's biggest simplification. Real securitisation didn't average risk; it sliced pools into layers (tranches) paid in order, so most of a subprime pool could be sold as AAA. Ichijou's wine-and-mud line is the correct critique: when losses are correlated, pooling hides risk instead of spreading it. (Vol. 5 map, Part II §4d)
- *"Gone before anyone opens the box"* → the originate-to-distribute model. The novel is shrewder than Angela: the firms that failed in 2008 were fee-takers left holding unsold loans and "safe" top slices. (Vol. 5 map, Part II §4g)
- *"Borrow cheaply in Japan, lend in America"* → the yen carry trade. If the currency risk is fully hedged, what's left of the profit is mostly payment for credit risk, the very risk Angela says she isn't taking. (Vol. 5 map, Part II §4h)
- *"She'll buy insurance on the securities"* → the glossary names the credit-default swap and AIG, which the US government rescued in September 2008. Insurance moves risk; it doesn't remove it. (Vol. 5 map, Part II §4f)
- *"Japanese banks mostly stayed out"* → the map calls this the series' most honest piece of alternate-history economics: curing one balance-sheet problem without changing incentives set up the next. (Vol. 5 map, Part II §4i)

## Chapter 4, part three: The glowworm's confession

The last scene of Chapter 4 is the quietest, and it's the one I think about most. It takes place in a library.

Runa's charitable foundation, the Choufuu Council, has donated a collection of books to her school, and the library has given them their own shelves, marked with her moon-and-cherry-blossom crest. While the ceremony goes on elsewhere, she slips away among the stacks. "Just being surrounded by the rows of shelves, not a single soul around me, made me feel like I'd been pulled into a different world."

Then the door opens, and Governor Iwasawa of Tokyo, the man who asked to see her alone, greets her with a riddle. "'We are all worms, but I believe that I am a glowworm.' Do you know who said that?" Churchill, he tells her, "when he was just a boy. I believe he was able to become such a great ruler because he took pride in himself." Runa thinks of Churchill's Nobel Prize in Literature, and of the man in front of her, who is also both a writer and a politician. It's not hard to hear what he's saying about himself.

"I had you come here so I could atone."

He's holding a novel by an old friend of his, "a story of politics, money, and women," a strange, beautiful book whose author was taken to court over it. And he confesses what Tachibana tried so hard to keep from her in Chapter 1. "I must confess that my people tried to deceive you and strike back against Prime Minister Koizumi. Tachibana-san already gave them a warning, but if you were a boy, they probably would've demanded action from you. That's just how ugly the United States' actions are right now." There it is again, the line from Chapter 1: if she were a boy.

The war will start soon. In Runa's narration it's not a war of justice but a superpower showing its strength, with Japan having declared it will take part. "If only I'd had your power, this war could've been avoided," the governor says. "I'm very sorry it couldn't be stopped."

Runa shakes her head. "I don't have that much power. Besides…wasn't this inevitable?" She thinks it over, and her thought is one of the most delicate in the book. "Using the word 'destiny' to describe it would be too heavy, and looking at it as kindness from adults was too cruel." The adults stopped her because she was a child. She has no other answer.

Then he asks the question that's been eating at him. "I have a feeling… No, I'm certain that if you had to bet on Prime Minister Koizumi or me, you'd choose the prime minister, wouldn't you? What is it that the prime minister has that I don't? If I possessed that quality, I would've run for prime minister instead of governor…"

He tries to hide it behind a smile, but she can see "the rage deep in his eyes." She knows the answer, but it would be rude to say it aloud, so she simply smiles. His smile turns sad. "I'm sorry. I almost got caught up in my friend's anger just from holding his book. Forget I said anything." And Runa, kindly: "Did you say something?"

Then there's a knock, and the head librarian, Takamiya Haruka, steps in. "Are you done sharing your little secrets, you two?" "You were listening to us, weren't you? You never change, eh?" "If not, I'd recommend not having your secret chats in my castle, Governor." They turn out to be old friends from the literary world, and Runa leaves them to it.

I've read this scene several times, and I think it's the volume's literary key. Remember who Iwasawa is: the author's afterword confirms he stands for Ishihara Shintarō, the novelist who governed Tokyo. Ishihara's real friend and rival was Mishima Yukio, the great novelist who in 1970 called on Japan's army to rise and restore the emperor's power, and then killed himself when it didn't. The event map suggests the book in Iwasawa's hands is Mishima's *After the Banquet*, a novel about a politician, his campaign money and the woman who runs his restaurant, which led to Japan's first privacy lawsuit. It fits every detail the text gives.

And in this world, that anger had consequences. Volume 3 told us that the Second February 26 Incident, the coup that targeted the Keikains, was set off when "that author" talked about a Shōwa Restoration. So picture what's happening among these shelves. An old man who has felt his country bend to America for half a century, holding his dead friend's book, is apologising to the granddaughter of a man his friend's followers tried to kill, for trying to use her against the prime minister. And in the same breath he asks why she still prefers the prime minister.

The author gives his own answer in the afterword: he felt Ishihara "became lost in the maze of literary history instead of focusing on postwar history specifically." This scene acts that sentence out, a governor almost swallowed by a friend's anger "just from holding his book." What does Koizumi have that Iwasawa lacks? Runa never says. My hunch, and I'd put it no higher than that, is the willingness to act and be hated for it, where Iwasawa would rather be right. The book leaves the silence to us, and I think that's the right choice.

- **Governor Iwasawa**: the novel's version of Ishihara Shintarō, novelist and governor of Tokyo.
- **Mishima Yukio**: a major Japanese novelist who died in 1970 after a failed call for a military uprising.

## Chapter 5: So foolish and so kind

Chapter 5 is two pages long, it takes place in one room on one day, and it changes the meaning of the book before it.

The day is 20 March 2003. The room is the vault beneath Kudanshita Keika Tower, where the Moonlight Fund keeps its emergency treasury: more than ten trillion yen in shares and bonds, gold and silver ingots, crates of banknotes holding a hundred million yen each, and jewels and paintings sent as gifts. In the vault's front room, set apart from all that, sits one single hundred million yen. "It was the money I'd once stared at in the manor, when I began the bad debt disposal that led to where I was today. I kept the money there in the treasury so that I'd never forget those times."

That's where she is standing when Okazaki finds her, and she doesn't turn around. "It's started. The war in Iraq." "Very well. The winner's already been decided, after all." Around the world, people who saw the result coming are already positioning for what comes after. "I didn't join them."

Still facing the money, she asks him why he thinks America lost in Vietnam, and his answer is cheerful and chilling. "It's a simple answer. They confused their methods with their objective." If America really feared communism spreading country to country, he says, it should have fought an all-out war in the North, not a defensive one in the South. The Soviet leader, the "eastern general secretary," understood that: "In the Manchurian war, his country mercilessly nuked both friend and foe and stopped us." His country won the Second World War "because they sacrificed tens of millions of lives." Runa thinks, quietly, that the country that leader tried to protect "no longer existed."

Then, half to herself, she murmurs, "I wonder if the United States will make the same choice now." And Okazaki, still smiling, says the thing she has told no one. "Indeed. You've been trying to focus on that point, and now you're here with your wings clipped because you were thinking ahead."

"…How long have you known about that?"

He guessed when she fell ill. She built bases for America in the Gulf, calling them distribution centres, because she foresaw this war. She worked it out logically "while looking at the proxy war in India and Pakistan, hundreds of thousands of people dying, and many, many more becoming refugees. You're a monster for thinking up the plan you did." He says it almost as a joke, but she feels like a murder suspect being questioned by a detective. And then he says something kinder and more devastating.

"You broke down after that. If human lives really meant nothing to you, you wouldn't have felt all that weight. You didn't just break because you were disgusted, did you? You broke because you realized you were capable of something so disgusting."

And then, for the first time in the series, Runa tells us in her own voice what her plan for Iraq actually was. I'll tell it as plainly as she does.

She saw the coming war as "a conflict over the going rate for a human life," a variation on the Cold War doctrine of mutually assured destruction, in which peace was kept by each side's certainty that the other could annihilate it. America was stuck fighting terrorists, an enemy with no country to destroy. The way out was to turn that kind of war back into an ordinary one between states, "and forcibly make it a conventional war. That was possible in this world." In this world, she tells us, it has already happened in Afghanistan. A doctrine of bombing whole areas to kill guerrillas along with everyone around them, the "Nagashima Doctrine," has turned the country into "an irreparable scene of genocide," with chemical and biological weapons used and Indian mercenaries sent in, and the horror of it is already working as "a tool of fear."

What America wanted, "what I'd tried to do by displaying my political strength to the neocons," the hawks in Washington, was revenge as a demonstration. Nuclear strikes, or their equivalent, on Iraq and Afghanistan, killing millions. An exchange rate of "millions of people to a few terrorists," so terrible that nobody would ever dare attack again. Machiavelli's old rule, that it is better for a ruler to be feared than loved. She had even worked out where the bomb would fall. Not Baghdad, which would have to be governed afterwards, but the heart of the Sunni region: "They'll hit the center of the Sunni Triangle, Fallujah."

"That was why I thought I could avoid Iraq's future quagmire through the sacrifice of those millions of lives. And it nearly broke me."

Because she remembered, from her first life, the real Iraq War: a quick victory and then years of insurgency that bled America and its allies. She weighed it the way a banker weighs a loss, one enormous loss now against an endless stream of losses later. And then, in Volume 4, she read a report from Washington and saw her own thinking turned into a plan. She heard what she was really saying:

"You people haven't done anything to me, but I want millions of you to die so that I can build a better future with my country."

"When I realized that was essentially what I was saying, I fell apart. Prime Minister Koizumi saw that I was being a reckless child, scolded me, and forced me out of the situation."

I had to put the book down at that point. For a volume and a half, we had been allowed to believe that Runa was a brilliant girl who overreached, and that the monstrous arithmetic belonged to other people. This chapter takes that away. It isn't the American generals. It's her. And she is the one who says so.

There's one more thing you need to know to read this scene fairly, because the series quietly built it in. In this world, no nuclear weapon has ever been used on a city. There was no Hiroshima and no Nagasaki, because the war ended differently. In our world, everyone has seen the picture of one bomb and one city, and that picture made the bomb almost unthinkable to use. This world has never seen it. So its planners believe an example still has to be made, and Runa's plan was, in effect, to be the one who supplied it.

She still half believes she was right. "I don't actually think they'll use nukes, at least," she says, and Okazaki answers, "That's why Iraq might turn into another Vietnam. People sure are fools." She's not even sure being stopped was good: "I'd predicted that far ahead, put that much thought into it, and been stopped just as I was about to move. I wasn't sure whether that was good or bad."

Then she asks him the question she's afraid of, and her voice shakes a little. "Tell me, are you satisfied with this option for the future?" He was beside her like this on the day of the September attacks too. And he smiles, to cheer her up.

"I am. I get to watch your chosen future from the front row, and now I know you're not an omnipotent, omniscient god."

I think that may be the healthiest thing anyone says to her in the entire volume. Everyone else wants something from her strength: Angela a King Midas, the nationalists an icon, the director a monster. Okazaki is content precisely because she turned out not to be a god.

The war, she reflects, will be judged by history. Because she was a child, the adults made her step back so that she would never face that judgment. "For that, I was grateful. But on the inside, I lamented those adults' stupid actions." And then the line that stands alone on the page:

*This world is so foolish and so kind.*

Foolish, in her reckoning, because the gentle war they chose will cost more lives in the end. Kind, because they would not let a child be its author. She holds both, and the book refuses to tell her which one weighs more.

She asks Okazaki to leave her there a while longer. The war ends in a month, just as she predicted, and the long aftermath begins. The chapter's last sentence is as bleak as anything in the series: "The truth that I'd tried to be involved in that war no longer existed."

I keep coming back to Okazaki's diagnosis of Vietnam, "They confused their methods with their objective," because it cuts at her too. It's the flower beds again. In Chapter 2 she wrote "recover the rights" when the goal was flowers. Here her objective was to spare her country, and her method became millions of dead strangers. An economist might put it like this: she treated the question as a calculation of costs and benefits, when the real question was who had the right to make it. Koizumi's objection was never that her sums were wrong. It was that a child who answers to no voter, and can't be put on trial, was choosing who would die.

## Chapter 6: The year, as the newspapers saw it

Chapter 6 has no narrator and no Runa. It's a stack of clippings: a dozen or so unsigned news items running from the autumn of 2002 into 2003, and then a long magazine feature about her group. Reading it feels like being handed the public record of everything the chapters around it showed from the inside. I'll go through it the way you'd leaf through a scrapbook, pausing on the pages that matter.

The first pages are about jobs. Japanese electronics makers are cutting thousands of workers: six thousand at one company, ten thousand each at two more, eight thousand at the old Furukawa Telecoms. Unemployment is "roughly 4 percent." Runa's new Keika Electronics Union stands out by "temporarily refraining" from layoffs. Then comes a quieter item that turns out to be one of the most important in the book. Since the yen shot up to eighty to the dollar in 1995, factories that would have moved abroad are moving to Karafuto instead, Japan's reunited northern territory, where people will work for 20 percent less. Unemployment there is still over 20 percent, and "no solution to the issues of migrant workers and second-class citizens has emerged yet."

Next, the banks. The government will make companies value what they own at today's market prices, which accountants call mark-to-market accounting. It sounds technical, so here's an everyday way to see it. Imagine you had to value your house each year at what it would actually sell for today, not what you paid for it in the boom. If prices have fallen, your wealth has shrunk on paper overnight. Japan's banks and conglomerates hold huge piles of each other's shares, a habit called cross-shareholding, a bit like a circle of friends who each keep a stack of the others' IOUs. Under the new rule, every fall in the market becomes a hole in their accounts. So, to avoid the holes, and the government inspectors who come with public bail-out money, the great groups start selling the shares off. The selling pushes the market down further, the Nikkei index "slumped into fifteen-thousand-yen territory," and the old ties come undone. One headline says it all: "Iwazaki has decided to dissolve its parent company." No occupying army is needed to break up the zaibatsu. An accounting rule does it.

Then the war. A summit in Geneva between Japan and Russia on the Karafuto question, with the American Secretary of State in the room, turns out to be mostly about lining up support for Iraq: "Japan, Russia, and Great Britain have joined with the United States." Then the item that makes Chapter 5 look less like a private nightmare. The American president has asked Congress for authority for an Iraq offensive "that included authorization for weapons of mass destruction," and it has come out "that the U.S. planned to use WMDs, including nuclear weapons, against Iraq." The inquiry's account of 2001 is laid out: the hijacked planes, the anthrax letters and an attack on India's parliament were all "diversionary measures" for the real plot, a train in Denver to be loaded "with nuclear materials" and a detonator being built in Akihabara. An anonymous general explains the logic: "the Iraq War has to become a symbol through asymmetric war." Runa's private reasoning was one side of a public American debate.

A few pages later, the war is won, and the peace is lost at once. Baghdad has fallen and Iraq has broken into three. Multinational forces hold the Sunni centre and are losing control of it. In the north, the Kurds have declared independence, and neighbouring Turkey is under martial law. In the south, Iran has moved in "to protect inhabitants," and American and Iranian troops now face each other. America's objective, the report says drily, was to topple the regime, "with no intentions of destroying Iraq itself as a nation." In our world, Iraq had a long, bloody insurgency but stayed one country. Here it's worse, much faster.

Then come two items about Runa's own empire, and I found both of them unsettling. The first is the K-Card, her group's new smart card. It combines the cards of her bank and department stores with the transit card of East Japan Imperial Railway, the big Tokyo rail company, and it also works as "personal identification for residents of Tokyo, Hokkaido, Chishima Prefecture, and Karafuto." For undocumented workers from Karafuto it means, for the first time, "a personal ID and bank account." It's a genuine kindness. It's also a private company handing out identity papers, and the item ends: "some express fear that their personal information will become free for the Keika Group to use as they please."

The second is the investigation into the plot against the Shinjuku Geofront, the underground city being built around her Shinjuku bullet-train line. It was planned after the September attacks and stopped in time, "unlike the attacks in London and Spain," which in this world have already happened, years earlier than in ours. The plotters were a strange alliance: "radical leftists, environmentalists, religious extremists, and crime syndicates, with participants in the Second February 26 Incident and former Northern Japanese military members" named as those who would carry it out. The police were alerted by "anonymous tips from within the group." Nobody says who sent them.

The last news items bring things home. Shoppers are back, buying the "three sacred treasures" of the digital age (DVD players, digital cameras and mobile phones), and the new factories in Karafuto lower unemployment there while raising it on the mainland. The opposition is demanding that the Self-Defense Forces come home from Iraq, and some ruling-party members fear for their seats if the troops take casualties. And the cabinet approves public money for "all existing megabanks," with Keika Holdings among those promising to pay it back immediately.

Then comes the magazine, *Economics Monthly*, with the third part of its series on zaibatsu in crisis: "The Bloated Keika Group!" It goes through her empire company by company, and nothing in it is false. The family's original drug company "has essentially sold itself" to foreign capital. Her bank has no internal successor to Ichijou, and Angela is tipped. Her railway is "A Time Bomb for the Keika Group?", with more than ¥3 trillion of projects that would turn bad if land prices fell, which is "a major reason the government insists on Keika Holdings going public." The government, the magazine reports, "reportedly told" the special prosecutors "to set their sights on" Tachibana, and "many feel that the Koizumi administration made an example of him." The electronics group's three-year pause on layoffs "can be interpreted as a declaration that major restructuring will occur three years from now."

And then the section on the Moonlight Fund, headed "The Keika Group's Inner Sanctuary?" A few dozen staff in her tower, assets rumoured at over a hundred billion dollars, owned by Runa, run through "untraceable private Swiss bank accounts," with "no public relationship" to the group it effectively leads. Only four people are said to know its true size: Ichijou, Tachibana, Toudou and Angela. Russia's Romanov claimants want its money returned. "Other rumors claim the Koizumi administration is targeting the Keika Group over this Moonlight Fund." And one sentence, tucked in near the end, that I found genuinely painful: "rumors that the group's present executives set up young Lady Keikain Runa as their puppet, removing her from the Keikain family's control, are being enthusiastically spread by Keikain family members."

Her own relatives are briefing the press against her.

I think this chapter is the answer to Chapter 5's open question, and it isn't a comforting one. Runa argued that only terror would prevent a quagmire. The adults chose the gentler war, and the news shows it producing a broken Iraq, an Iranian army in the south, attacks in Europe and a plot against Tokyo. The author doesn't thereby prove her right; nothing here shows the nuclear option would have worked. But he won't let us enjoy the easy conclusion that being stopped made everything turn out well.

And I think Karafuto is the series' cleverest invention. In our world, Japanese factories fled to China in these years. In this one they go north, to a poor, half-foreign territory inside Japan's own borders, whose people the mainland calls "second-class." The film villain in Chapter 3, the ex-Northern soldiers among the Shinjuku plotters, and now the migrant workers getting their first identity cards from a child's company all come from that one place. Remember it. The last chapter of the book shows why it matters.

## Chapter 7, part one: Operation Vorpal Bunny

The chapter is called *The Lady's Supporting Roles*, and it opens in a windowless conference room in the American embassy in Tokyo, where five people are deciding who will play which part in Runa's life.

Around the table sit the ambassador, the CIA's head of East Asia (flown in for the occasion), an intelligence analyst, and two young women in suits: Eva Charon, a CIA officer who works as one of Runa's maids, and Yulia Molotova. Eva is only here because Angela, the agency's star placement, has been promoted into Runa's bank, a move the CIA "both welcomed… and was troubled by."

The analyst's assessment of Runa's empire is blunt. "The Keika Group has expanded rapidly through acquisitions, and their inner workings haven't stabilized yet. We don't know who they have on the inside or who's infiltrated them." And the man who "has real control over the Keika Group," in Washington's view, isn't Runa. It's Tachibana, her butler, and what he's really doing is "trying to protect Keikain Runa." Eva confirms it: "there's no doubt that he will pick Keikain Runa over the Keika Group."

Then Eva says the most interesting thing anyone says about Runa in the chapter. "The problem is that Keikain Runa-sama, the duke's daughter, hasn't noticed that herself," she says. "Or rather, she's pretended she hasn't noticed it all this time. She is both subject to American and Russian surveillance, and a person to be protected." Through the Iwazaki marriage and Runa's recruits from Karafuto, Eva adds, the old Northern Japan intelligence network and the former KGB have formed a faction inside the group. "Most likely—no, almost certainly—the Russians are up to the same things we are."

So what do you do about it? Yulia, bored: "A honeypot? It's overused, but it's a reliable tactic." A honeypot, or honeytrap, is the oldest trick in espionage: an attractive agent befriends a target and wins his trust. They go through the photographs. Tachibana, Ichijou and Toudou all have wives and children, and a failed attempt on them might "enrage the young lady." That leaves one man.

"Okazaki Yuuichi… He's the man who led the young lady into actions that greatly harmed our country, and a key person who can't be removed in the face of our wars in Afghanistan and Iraq. He even knew that her reckless behavior was in preparation for a war in Iraq. He's someone we need to get our hooks in."

The ambassador never says the ugly words "obstruct" or "eliminate," but the analyst does, about any Russians they find in the way: the team "can use their discretion in obstructing or eliminating them." It's said in the same breath as everything else, as routine.

Their surveillance of Okazaki turns up a problem: other people got there first. In Yokohama's Chinatown he regularly eats at a restaurant run by Hong Kong expatriates, where a girl named Ryuu Suzune, daughter of an influential Chinese family in Karafuto, treats him "like siblings with a big age gap." And at the convenience store he always visits, a Russian "student" named Irina Berosova works only about one day a week, which happens to be the day he comes, and visits the Russian embassy once a month. There's even a private detective, hired by Runa's group to check whether any "pests" are following the people around her. "Do we look like pests?"

The plan they settle on is a classic. A staged feud between the Chinese and Russian mafias in Yokohama; Yulia harassed outside the convenience store; Okazaki riding to the rescue. They're careful, and the reason is lovely: they're afraid of a child. Eva and Yulia remember that Runa and Okazaki "had worked together to crash the American stock market and make off with tens of billions of dollars." For a name, Eva chooses a movie puppet that Runa once laughed at. "How about 'Operation Vorpal Bunny'?" It's the killer rabbit from *Monty Python and the Holy Grail*, the small fluffy thing that turns out to be lethal.

It fails spectacularly. Not long afterwards, Eva and Anisha Egorova, Runa's maid from the Russian side, are summoned to Kagachō police station in Yokohama, to a conference room where Yulia and Irina refuse to look at each other, Ryuu Suzune is cheerfully unbothered, and Okazaki makes no attempt to hide his smile. Then the officer in charge walks in. "I'm Maefuji, from the foreign affairs section," he says. "I'm also considered the man in charge of a certain young lady. Need I explain further?"

He explains anyway, with a policeman's dry relish. Somebody paid a gang to make trouble, and the gang tried to bill two clients for the same job. Chinatown found out, the gangster panicked and ran to the convenience store, and Yulia and Irina were dragged into the planned scene. "What showed up next wasn't a knight on a white horse, but a black-and-white patrol car." The prefectural police are furious, and he's had to come all the way out to Yokohama. "It's your job, and I can't tell you to walk away from it, but could you please try to hide it a little better, at least…?"

Then Okazaki, the target, settles everything. "All right, then. If my lady hears about this, it'll be a big problem. Why don't we say you owe me one, and we'll call it even?" Here's the cover story: it was never a honeytrap at all, but a test of Runa's new companions. "It's Yulia-chan and Irina-chan, right? Now that this has happened, you won't betray me, will you?" He says it smiling, and the two girls tremble.

That night they tell Runa the cover story, and she does exactly what they expected. "Get to Okazaki with a honeytrap?! Ah ha ha ha ha ha ha ha ha ha… That's so stupid!" And the narration adds, very quietly: "That was as far as she treaded into the issue."

The punchline belongs to Ryuu Suzune. Yulia and Irina ask why she's there at all, since she wasn't part of the incident. "That's simple," Suzune replies. "I'd had orders to win Okazaki over ever since he got famous in Hong Kong. That was in 2000." The Chinese network had been working on him for years, long before Washington or Moscow thought of it. Why did he keep going to that restaurant? "I mastered the chicken recipe he tried in Macau and said was delicious." All three of them, the American asset, the Russian asset and the Chinese plant, are now Runa's "associates," the girls who'll go with her to junior high.

I laughed all the way through this, and then I went back and read it again, because there's a serious ledger underneath. Three intelligence services had people beside Okazaki before anyone moved. "Obstruct" and "eliminate" are everyday words in that room. The only thing restraining the Americans is fear of a schoolgirl's revenge. And the policeman sighing at them, Maefuji, is the officer who, in the game, arrests the villainess. Here he cleans up after her.

The chapter's title is the argument. Everyone in this story has been cast by someone else: the CIA casts a rescuer and a damsel, Moscow casts a shop girl, Hong Kong casts a cook. Only Okazaki rewrites his part, turning himself in one evening from target into creditor, with three spies in his debt. And Runa's laugh, I think, is the pretence Eva described, that she hasn't noticed. She accepts three foreign agents into her inner circle on the same night, and her safety lies in the fact that everyone's spies are known to everyone.

**Terms in this piece**
- **CIA**: the Central Intelligence Agency, America's foreign intelligence service.
- **KGB**: the Soviet Union's security and intelligence service.
- **Northern Japan**: in this world, the Soviet-backed communist state that ruled Karafuto (Sakhalin) until 1994.
- **Honeypot / honeytrap**: an espionage tactic in which an attractive agent wins a target's trust.
- **Asset**: an intelligence term for someone who works for, or reports to, a spy agency.
- **Associates**: the girls placed around Runa as companions, maids and guards when she enters junior high.
- **Chinatown, Yokohama**: Japan's largest Chinese district, south of Tokyo.
- **Public Safety**: the Japanese police branch that handles espionage, terrorism and foreign agents.

## Chapter 7, part two: The girls on the train to Yūbari

The second part of Chapter 7 opens with a short lecture on poverty, and I'd ask you to keep it in mind through everything that follows. "One reason poverty was so frightening was that it removed someone's ability to see their options." A professor on television explains that it's cheaper to rescue people before they fall, though voters resent it, and that it's far harder to climb out when poverty is all you've ever known, "because the person might not even understand that they're impoverished at all."

Then the scene moves to Yūbari, in the mountains of Hokkaido, a coal town that sank into debt when its mines closed. In this world two companies have taken that debt on: Runa's group, which owns Hokkaido's development bank, and Iwazaki, whose mine it was. And the town is filling up again with people from Karafuto: the old elite of the fallen northern state, carrying their savings; former soldiers "who'd been paid for shedding blood"; and refugees.

On a slow local train at Shin-Yūbari station, early one afternoon, two girls in the uniform of an elite Tokyo school sit down together. Their conversation is wonderfully flat. "That's a lot of green, huh?" "It sure is." "I hear they turn white in the winter." "Well, this is Hokkaido." A girl in glasses pokes her head over the seat behind them: "Can you please put a little more effort into your chitchat? We'll be seeing a lot of each other, after all."

Their names are Kushunnai Nanami, Enbuchi Yuna and Nozuki Misaki, and the girl in glasses, Misaki, already knows a great deal. The mines may reopen as a training centre for Karafuto miners, who work for wages "unimaginable to most Japanese people" in pits nobody inspects. Keika is paying for three thousand workers and their families, "like us," and Kitakaba Security, the group's security company, is building "a training facility here under the pretext of using it to practice drills." Then the narration gives Kitakaba its full description, in one terrible clause: "the company that had purchased them."

Nanami says, simply, "Well, if we do our jobs right, everyone in the orphanage will get to eat delicious meals." Misaki: "It sounds like they're bringing the orphanage to Yubari too." "Yubari will be our new home."

These are orphans from Hokkaido and Karafuto, and they've been bought.

The narration lays out what Runa's money has done for this town, and it's genuinely remarkable. Keika negotiated with the city so that anyone who arrives can be entered in a family register, Japan's official record of who you are. Without one you can't get a driver's licence, open an account or prove you exist. The town grows famous melons that her department stores will sell exclusively; it burns methane from the old mines to make electricity; there's a plan for a train that collects Sapporo's rubbish, and a new dam. It's a whole economy rebuilt around people nobody else wanted.

Two more girls get on: Rudaka Miu and Akibe Riko. And because five girls from the same circumstances have now met for the same purpose, to serve Keikain Runa, the conversation finally takes off.

"I bet I'll be the leader," Nanami announces. "We only just met and you're already assigning roles, Kushunnai?" says Yuna, the slender, sporty one who will be a guard. And Nanami explains, as if it's the most natural thing in the world: "I was picked because I looked the most like her out of all the candidates." Misaki, the adviser, nods. "Ah. Then it makes sense that you'd be the leader." Yuna gets it too: "So, if push comes to shove, you'll have to impersonate my lady? I get it. You can be the boss." Miu and Riko will be maids. "Well, I'll more be doing odd jobs."

Nanami is the body double. If someone ever tries to kill Runa, Nanami is the one who is supposed to be standing there.

And why do they exist at all? "Keikain Runa had decided to take in orphans from Hokkaido and Karafuto because she realized she had no faction of her own, despite being adopted into the main Keikain family." Tachibana and Toudou "had really bent over backward" to assemble them.

At Yūbari station three more girls are waiting to welcome them, unmistakably not local: Irina, Yulia and Ryuu Suzune, the spies from the last chapter, who now share their new job. "Welcome to Yubari, our second home. I'll show you where you'll live." The orphans will actually live in Tokyo, but their family registers will give an address in Yūbari, and they will "conduct themselves as Japanese citizens from Hokkaido." Someone counts heads and comes up one short. The sixth girl, Glasya Marsheva, didn't bother to wear her uniform for the trip, missed her stop, and is later found wandering around Tomakomai station.

I don't know quite how to feel about this scene, and I don't think the book does either. It's lit like the first episode of a school comedy: the awkward small talk, the bossy one, the clever one in glasses, the one who gets lost. Underneath it, the words are "purchased," "impersonate" and "if we do our jobs right, everyone in the orphanage will get to eat." Go back to the lecture that opened the section. These girls can't see the options they were never shown. They are grateful, and loyal, and their meals depend on it. In the next chapter Runa herself will call them "my pawns," and say her power is like a poison she's already infecting them with. The narration isn't hiding what this is. It's letting us see her kindness and her feudalism as one gesture, made with the same hand.

Then the chapter takes a breath, with an anonymous internet message board: "[Let's Celebrate] Keika Railway Part 57 [Etchujima Branch Line Now Open]." Railway fans swap news about her lines, a new branch line in Tokyo, a bullet train for Shikoku, a freight line in Fukuoka, a VIP platform at Narita Airport, and one poster whose ID is, quite openly, "lunakeikain" answers their questions with suspicious inside knowledge. Asked about the chaos at her new trading company, where an employee complains "We don't even know who our bosses are LMAO," she writes, "As for Corporation or Corp… *looks away quietly*." Asked how it's doing financially, she says it's reporting profits, "No idea where they're making their money, though." (It's her fund.) When someone suggests building another terminal at Narita, she replies "*claps hands* I never thought of that! I'm going to go bring it up now!" Another poster wonders: "Could it be that people working on the project are posting in this thread?" And when someone praises the K-Cards, which now "work as photo IDs in Tokyo, Hokkaido, and Karafuto," she adds, a little ruefully, "The stock market doesn't appreciate them, though…"

It's the volume's comic proof that she cannot stop taking part, even during her moratorium. But put the thread beside the train, and you see how much her group now does. It issues identity cards and arranges family registers. It runs railways, an airline and a parcel service, trains an armed security company in a town it bankrolls, and houses an orphanage. Each piece is kindness or efficiency, and each piece really is that. Added together, they're the infrastructure of a state that nobody elected, owned by a schoolgirl.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Yūbari**: a former coal-mining city in Hokkaido, famous for its melons.
- **Karafuto**: Sakhalin. In this world the Soviet-backed Northern Japan ruled it until 1994, and many of its people now move south looking for work.
- **Family register** (*koseki*): Japan's official household record of births, marriages and citizenship, needed for most official documents.
- **Kitakaba Security**: the Keika Group's security company, which employs guards and armed maids.
- **Body double**: a person chosen to look like someone important and stand in for them, sometimes to draw an attack.
- **Faction**: a group of loyal followers inside a larger organisation.
- **Methane**: a gas released from coal seams, which can be burned for power.
- **Message board thread**: an online discussion, numbered in parts as it grows.
- **K-Card**: the Keika Group's smart card, which in this world also serves as photo ID.

## Chapter 7, part three: Good morning, Asuka-chan, Runa-chan

The last part of Chapter 7 is a single ordinary day in the life of Kaihouin Hotaru, and I think it's the most beautiful thing in the volume.

Hotaru is the silent girl who has been Runa's friend since kindergarten, always half a step behind Asuka, a girl you could lose in an empty room. Her mornings begin at sunrise. She kneels on the floor of her flat in Den-en-chōfu and meditates. "She definitely wasn't falling back asleep, but only she knew that for sure." Her family lives far away in Nara, and she lives here with an attendant, a woman who, back when Hotaru was in kindergarten, couldn't even sense that she was in the room. Lately, though, the woman has started to notice her. She says good morning now, and chats with her over meals.

At the school gate Asuka is waiting. "Good morning, Hotaru-chan!" Asuka does all the talking, and understands every answer from a nod or a shake of the head. Hotaru's grades are neither terrible nor outstanding. She takes the class's after-lunch cleaning very seriously, and so, it turns out, does Runa. When the rich girls suggest leaving it to their attendants, the head librarian, Takamiya Haruka, silences them: "Keeping things orderly is part of a good education." Runa leads the cleaning herself, and tells the class, "What? If you're capable of doing something yourself, you should do it. Isn't it nice to have things clean?" To her inner circle she admits the real reason: "CEO Ichijou at our company still cleans the executive suite toilet himself. I've learned you can't hold a position like that unless you can clean that way, at the very least." (Angela and Karin, the narration adds, once had a real fight with Ichijou about it.)

Hotaru's job today is the rubbish. She hauls the bin down the corridor, and the students she passes jump, because what they see is a garbage can floating along by itself. She'll have to be more careful.

When Hotaru really tries to hide, only four people in the world can see her: a senior in the occult research club, Asuka, the fortune-teller's daughter Kanna Mizuki, and Takamiya the librarian, as long as they're inside her library. Hotaru spends her afternoons reading library books in the club room, walks home with Asuka, sometimes stops for tea, and goes to bed after meditating again.

And every day she asks herself one question. *Just what am I going to become…?*

Hotaru, the narration tells us, was the girl who was "supposed to become a zashiki-warashi," one of the child-spirits of northern Japanese folklore who live in a house and bring it good fortune for as long as they stay. She was saved from that fate, "at the very end," by Asuka's mandarins. But the power is still in her, and it's fading. She can feel herself granting fewer blessings to the people around her. Asuka's father is struggling in his election, and Runa is stuck in a political dilemma. "If she were a real zashiki-warashi, those things probably never would have happened."

"Hotaru was still young. She understood her power, but not the raging current known as 'the times' that was washing it away."

*If I become a real zashiki-warashi…I won't be able to go to school with everyone…*

She's frightened. "The thought of turning from a special person into a normal one filled her with fear. But now she knew that being normal meant a fun life. She just didn't understand it, and she didn't want to."

Then the librarian lends her a book. The glossary tells us it's *The Little Prince*, the story of a small prince from a tiny planet and the fox who teaches him that what is essential is invisible to the eye, and that you become responsible, forever, for what you have tamed. As soon as Hotaru finishes it, she goes out and buys her own copy. And somewhere in that exchange the librarian, whom the students call "the witch of the library," tells her the thing she needed to hear:

"I know what you'll become, but the only thing you can ever be is 'you.' So don't let those thoughts trouble you."

"She read it over and over, her tears never ceasing." The witch, the narration says, had broken the curse.

The next morning, at the school gate, Asuka calls out as she always does, then spots Runa: "Good morning, Hotaru-chan! Ah! Runa-chan, good morning!" "Good morning, Asuka-chan and Hotaru-chan." The two of them are smiling just as they did in kindergarten, and to Hotaru their smiles are like beacons. So the words come out on their own.

"Good morning, Asuka-chan, Runa-chan."

"Huh…?"

"For real…?"

Hotaru smiles at their astonished faces. "Her life as a human had only just begun."

I have to admit that I had a lump in my throat at that line. A girl who could make a garbage can float and a household prosper chooses instead to be seen, to be ordinary, and to say good morning to her friends out loud. And I think the book means us to see Runa in her. Here is a girl with an uncanny gift that makes everyone around her prosper; a gift now being washed away by "the times"; a terror of becoming ordinary; and the slow discovery that ordinary might be a happy life. In Volume 4, Koizumi and the American president planned exactly this for Runa: that someday she would fall in love and "become a normal girl." Hotaru gets there herself, through a friend's mandarins and a borrowed book, and the chapter treats it as a liberation rather than a loss.

It's the clearest picture in the volume of the director's third ending from Chapter 3, the monster who turns back into a human, chosen rather than imposed. But there's a shadow under it too. If Hotaru's blessings have been part of what kept Runa lucky since kindergarten, then that luck is running out.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Zashiki-warashi**: a child-spirit of northern Japanese folklore that lives in a house and brings it prosperity while it stays, and ruin when it leaves.
- **Den-en-chōfu**: a wealthy residential district of Tokyo.
- **Occult research club**: a school club for ghost stories, folklore and the supernatural.
- ***The Little Prince***: Antoine de Saint-Exupéry's 1943 fable about a little prince, a rose and a fox.
- **Senpai**: an older student at the same school.

## Chapter 8, part one: The fortune teller's curse

The cherry blossoms are out, and it's graduation day for the elementary school. Mio, the little girl who calls Runa her big sister, hands her a bouquet: "Congratulations on graduating, Runa-oneesama." Hotaru, Runa notices with surprise, is "in full form and smiling today." All four of the Quartet have beaten the school's record marks, but Runa comes fourth, because her tests in the autumn, when she was ill, dragged her down. So Eiichi is class representative, and he's delighted. "A win's a win. I'll always be proud when I can beat you, Runa." She sets her flowers down on her desk and thinks something she has never quite thought before: *I wonder what the real Keikain Runa would've done in this moment?*

Notice the phrase. The "real" Keikain Runa. This is the chapter in which the game comes back, and its title is the first in the volume to drop "the Lady" and use the game's own word: *The Villainess Meets the Fortune Teller*.

She teases the boys: what do they actually want to beat her at? Yuujirou wants a relationship with her like the one she has with his father, the deputy prime minister. Mitsuya, startlingly, isn't interested in beating her at all: "My goal isn't about Keikain. I want to outdo Teia." And Eiichi closes his eyes, thinks for a moment, takes her hand in front of the entire class, and says, "I can't do better than you right now, and I want you on my team. So, Runa, will you marry me?" She takes a deep breath. "You're so stupid, Eiichi-kun!"

Then the book explains how her new school works, and it's quietly chilling. The academy doubles in size at each stage. Children who have been there since elementary school, the nobles, conglomerate heirs and political families, wear a gold badge. Those who join in junior high wear silver, and most of them are the gold badges' retainers, wearing their patron's family crest beside the school's. In high school come the bronze badges, the scholarship students, who can also be given a powerful family's crest to wear. Runa finds it "frankly impressive how the powerful people in our country had formed a system to handpick excellent personnel and place them under their protection."

The Keikains have bought ten of these endorsements from other noble houses this year, and in a waiting room nine of her future classmates stand and bow to her. She tells us plainly what she thinks they are. "These people were to become my pawns." Tachibana bought them from Karafuto orphanages, reasoning, cynically, that "with no one but me to rely on in life, they'd put their lives on the line to protect me." She smiles and says, "Let's have a nice time together," and thinks: "I was already infecting them with my power as if it were a poison." She knows what she herself is, too: the privileged class are "displayed like decorative flowers in a solarium," bred to pair up with real power, and she has become "an extremely rare product with premiums attached."

But there should have been twelve slots, and one went elsewhere. Because of an old debt to her late grandfather, it was handed to another family for a girl called Kanna Mizuki, adopted daughter of the fortune-teller Kanna Sera. Runa knows the name. It's from the game.

In the game, Kanna Mizuki was "a love-crazed girl who had a new boyfriend each month," a side character with a hidden mechanic: her affection rose with each route the player cleared. She never once opposed the villainess. In the second term of the final year she left to study abroad, and right after that the school conflict came to a head and Runa fell. And the background books said something that makes Runa sit up: the three boys, Eiichi, Yuujirou and Mitsuya, "had conspired to force her to go abroad." Runa suspects that Mizuki was secretly the Keikain family's private adviser. So she walks round to meet her.

The Kanna house is in Jinbōchō, Tokyo's old booksellers' quarter, ten minutes from her tower, and to her disappointment it's an ordinary office building. Inside is something less ordinary. The Kannas are a family of women who tell fortunes for the powerful, and much of their work is sexual. Kanna Sera built the house in a single generation as the mistress of Runa's grandfather Hikomaro; Angela's file on her is three words long: "She's a spy. A honeypot." Together, Runa thinks, she and Hikomaro "reigned over Japan's dark side." Her "daughters" are orphans she scouted, and Mizuki, it turns out, is her heir.

Mizuki is the same age as Runa, but she looks almost grown, and her first question goes straight through Runa's defences. "I beg your pardon, but how old are you, Keikain-san?" It's exactly the right question to ask a girl who has lived one life already. Runa feels she's been hit by "a powerhouse punch" and changes the subject.

Mizuki explains the old debt. Kanna Sera "told the Keikains they'd be targeted during the Second February 26 Incident," which the narration now defines: "a coup d'etat by the imperial police and self-defense force that had never occurred in my past life." Hikomaro escaped it on her warning. Then, in two short sentences, the narration tells us Mizuki's own history. She grew up in an orphanage, where she was abused, before Kanna Sera adopted her, and her hardships "were said to be the source of her talents." The book says no more than that, and nor will I. Runa tells her she has lived a very brave life. Mizuki turns it around at once. "It's nothing compared to yours. I only need to focus on my own life, but you're responsible for hundreds of thousands of Keika Group employees."

Then she offers to read Runa's fortune. The Kannas charge "market price," and when Runa asks what that means, the answer is lovely: "this isn't so much a payment for fortune-telling as a wager on fate itself." And if the fortune is wrong? "You'll be upset with us, but that's all. It's our job to be an outlet for resentments like that." Mizuki lays out the major cards of the tarot deck, twenty-three of them, because the Kannas also use the blank spare card. It "represents something one can't see, or even that one doesn't want to see."

Runa places her bet. She unpins her silver badge with the Keikain crest and hands it over. "As a test, I'll bet my future. You can use that however you want, and I'll do the same with the fortune you tell me." It's the badge a patron gives a retainer, and she has just given it to a girl who refused her family's protection.

As she shuffles, Mizuki says the thing that I think is the key to this whole chapter. "Fortune-telling isn't such a convenient thing. We think of it as closer to a curse than anything." "A curse?" "Yes. A curse that sets the future in stone. Having your fortune told is the same as losing two things—your future, and your possibilities."

She explains with two roads. If you knew road A had a pothole, you'd take road B. "Even if you found money on the ground after getting past the pitfall?" That's the future you lose. And if you're told about A and B, you never go looking for a road C. That's the possibility you lose. Runa claps her hands. She understands. Of course she does. She has lived her whole second life this way, steering around the pitfalls she remembers from the game, and in Chapter 4 she has just refused a road she knows leads to 2008.

Then the cards, five in a row, from about six months ago to about six months ahead.

The Emperor, reversed: "Keikain-san, did you experience a loss of power?" Runa freezes. The Moon, upright: "It seems like you suffered quite a bit of emotional damage." The Star, reversed: hope and ideals lost, "or perhaps that the situation has become more realistic." Runa feels sweat running down her cheek. *It's right! It's completely, entirely accurate!* If Mizuki had brought out a suspicious pot to sell her, she admits, she'd have bought it on the spot. The Wheel of Fortune, reversed: "an unpleasant event will soon occur." And the Hermit, reversed: "you're going to draw attention in a bad way."

Runa knows at once what the last two mean. *I know what it is… The snap election in the House of Representatives.* She leans back, defeated. "You win. I believe in this future entirely."

Afterwards she has tea with Kanna Sera herself, a woman with "a bewitching, mysterious, somehow broken aura," who says of Runa's grandfather, with perfect candour, "I used him, and he used me." The cakes are homemade by the Kanna daughters. And as Runa smiles and hides her nerves, a picture slides into her mind, "like a waking dream," of a scene the game never showed: the day the game's Runa said goodbye to Kanna Mizuki.

In it, Mizuki has been approved to study abroad, and the other Runa, the real one, is calm. "I'm engaged to a Teia family member now, and Katsuki-san is bugging me to get my affairs in order." "Now that I've got your connections, money does come in much more easily. I'll get the Keikain family back on our feet." Mizuki asks whether she's sure. "I am. I bet my life on the revival of the Keikain family, and now it's finally within my reach." Mizuki presses her about the heroine, Takanashi Mizuho, and the girl answers coolly: "It doesn't bother me. Who cares if he has a mistress or two? That's what happens when two families join." And they part with a blessing: "May your life be full of good fortune."

This is the first time we've seen the original Keikain Runa from the inside, and she isn't the petty school bully the game seemed to promise. She's a lonely, serious girl from a ruined family, engaged for money, telling herself that her fiancé's love for another girl doesn't matter, who has bet her life on saving her house and believes she's about to win. And the present Runa, waking from the vision, is shaken by its calm. Her downfall came right after this farewell. "Why did things seem so peaceful…? And actually, if I was so confident I'd come out on top, which part of my plan went wrong?"

Kanna Sera, still smiling, asks to see what's in her pocket. Runa reaches in and finds the Cintamani stone, the wish-granting jewel a fox spirit gave her at Fushimi Inari shrine in Volume 3. "I hadn't brought it with me, of course." "You should take good care of this," Kanna Sera says. "It will lead you where you need to go." Runa jokes that a fox gave it to her in exchange for her inari sushi, and the fortune-teller, who understands perfectly, asks if she can borrow it sometime. "No. Sorry."

Walking home, Runa sums up Mizuki to her maids: "I'm not sure I'll keep her close to me, but I think we could become good friends." Of all the relationships in this chapter, I notice, it's the only one she doesn't describe as ownership.

But one line from the vision won't leave her alone. "Now that I've got your connections, money does come in much more easily." From what she knows of the group's bad debts, she reckons the ruined family in the game would have needed some ¥45–50 billion. So: "Who would help a failing young noblewoman like me raise more than fifty billion yen? And why?" Hold that question. The last chapter answers it.

## Chapter 8, part two: Three boys, three offers

Spring arrives, and so does junior high. "I don't feel any different, though," Runa mutters in the car. Her maid Aki, acting as her guardian for the day, corrects her: "Junior high is the first step toward adulthood." Her household is being reshuffled around her. Tachibana is back as her butler, Angela is off to New York, Aki becomes her secretary, and her maids include, as she puts it with a perfectly straight face, "trustworthy CIA and ex-KGB agents," as long as she overlooks the information they pass back to their old employers.

Beside her sits Tachibana Yuka, now her classmate as well as her maid, stiff with nerves. Runa asks what she's looking forward to in junior high. "Nothing would make me happier than to be useful to you, my lady." It's the perfect answer for a servant and the wrong one for a schoolgirl, and Runa gives her an order that I find very tender. "I appreciate that, but I want you to think of at least one thing you're interested in for yourself. After all, we both have long lives ahead of us. Most likely." That last little phrase, "Most likely," is easy to read past. It's a girl who knows how the game ends, quietly allowing that she may not have a long life at all. Then she tells Yuka not to call her "my lady" at school. Yuka tries, and manages: "Runa-sama."

Runa's class, 1-A, holds the three boys, Yuka, one of the orphan associates, Rudaka Miu, her old classmate Katsuki Shiori and Kanna Mizuki, who isn't wearing the Keikain badge. Runa reads it as a choice: Mizuki wants to keep their relationship as it is. Then Runa feels a chill, turns, and sees Shiori glaring at Mizuki, and Yuka icy beside her. "I realized that there must be bad blood between the three." In the classroom, the boys greet her exactly as they always do. "Morning, Runa." "Good morning, Keikain-san." "You're late, Keikain." She smiles and gives them her first greeting as a junior high student: "Good morning, everyone."

There are small changes. All three boys are taller than her now, which Eiichi finds only fair: "If I can't at least beat you in height, what else am I supposed to beat you at?" Every club wants her, and she joins none. "I don't like being tied down." Besides, she has realised that natural talent no longer beats practice, a lesson from her kendo defeat, and she cites what she calls the "ten-thousand-hours problem," the idea that real mastery takes about ten thousand hours of practice. And then, over the following weeks, she has one conversation with each boy. Each of them, in his own way, makes her an offer, and none of them quite lands.

The first is Mitsuya, in the library at lunchtime, reading a fantasy epic that has just been made into a film. The glossary confirms it's *The Lord of the Rings*. He asks, "Do you think we'd make the same choices if we were this story's main characters?" "I don't think so," she says. "Power always captivates those who have it. When you've got power, the idea of losing it is terrifying." He closes the book and smiles sadly, and she can feel the question behind his eyes: hasn't she already got that power? So she answers it. "Once power grows too much, you can't control it. And, of course, the antagonist might always steal it all from you."

She's thinking, she admits to us, of the ending in which she is exposed and ruined as the villainess, and she tells us something she hasn't said before. She has quietly prepared for it. She's studying for a degree by correspondence at Professor Kanbe's college, so that she could live a modest cultured life afterwards, and she doubts her fund's Swiss account could ever be fully drained, since Ichijou and Tachibana are probably spreading it across several already. "I'd stared into that abyss," she thinks, "and the adults in my life had pulled me back." Aloud she says, "It's scary to lose things and to have enemies. When you possess nothing but fear like that, the world itself starts to terrify you." "And that turns you into a demon king…?" In the story, she notes, the world isn't saved by the hero or the dark lord, but by "a pathetic, obsessed man" who lost the power and kept chasing it.

"Let me ask you, Keikain, what would you have done with that power?" "I think the power would've gotten to me. Then something I didn't account for would've pulled the rug out from—" "I don't know about that," Mitsuya interrupts. Then he looks her right in the eyes and says:

"You're not alone. I'd definitely save you before the power overcame you."

He's so earnest that she laughs, and tells him that if she ever falls, he can come and pull her up. "I will. Stumble all you like, and I'll come rescue you." The bell rings. He goes to the checkout counter with his book, and she watches him, and remembers the game, and whispers to herself:

"Liar. You didn't save me at all…"

*In this world, would Mitsuya-kun come to my rescue? Or take part in condemning me?* "There was no answer to that, so I stood and left the library."

I find that whisper heartbreaking, because it's aimed at a boy who hasn't done anything yet. She loves him as a friend, and she's carrying the memory of a betrayal he doesn't know is in his future.

The second conversation is with Yuujirou, at their usual café, where she finds him frowning over a large pile of photographs of girls. They're prospective brides. His brother may move to Hokkaido, which leaves their father's parliamentary seat in play, and his brothers-in-law are fighting over it. Each girl's family, Yuujirou explains, would put up a father or uncle from a local assembly to keep the seat "warm" until he's old enough to run at twenty-five. Runa leafs through them. "A lot of them are related to general contractors and civil engineers." And Yuujirou, the politician's son, says something unexpectedly bleak: "Rural areas hire more general contractors than anyone. The Koizumi administration is cutting down on public works projects in the name of financial reform, but a lot of places are suffering for that. Some families even sacrifice their daughters for financing. It can't go on like this."

Does she get photographs like these? "Probably. Tachibana throws them out before I can get a look, though." Then she goes quiet, remembering the game. Yuujirou is a boy who hides his feelings, and in his route it's the heroine, Takanashi Mizuho, who breaks through the walls he's built around himself. Runa drinks her grape juice so he won't see what she's thinking. He shows her the marriage market from the inside, and says nothing about himself.

The third is Eiichi, after school, in what she can tell is business mode. His family is leaving its old conglomerate, and the companies around it are forming a new Teia Group, all of them with the same struggling main bank, Gowa Osan. A main bank, in Japan, is something like a company's family doctor: the one bank it relies on above all others, which is supposed to stand by it in hard times. Eiichi has already refused to let the new group take over the computer-backup company the four friends co-own, because he thinks it should stay in her hands. "How honorable of you."

Then he asks, "Are you buying Gowa Osan, Runa?" She explains the government's new rule, and it's the kind of riddle she loves. "Japan can only have four megabanks," but that count includes the post office's savings bank and the farmers' cooperative bank, Norinchukin. So six commercial banks, hers among them, have to shrink to two or three. Half of them must merge.

Eiichi listens, and then says quietly, "Marrying you would really have a lot of benefits, wouldn't it?" That's his second proposal, and this time it's a banking strategy. "I think it would cause just as many headaches too," she replies; she's used to the subject by now. He explains, confidently, that a company feels safer with a main bank behind it. And Runa sighs, half to herself.

"I wonder if my marriage will have any kind of love in it at all?"

Eiichi looks genuinely confused. "Do you really need that?"

There's a silence. *Okay. I see how it is.* She beckons him closer, leans to his ear, and shouts into it: "Eiichi-kun, you're so stupid!"

It's played as a joke, and it's very funny. But put it next to the vision in the first half of this chapter. In the game, the original Runa was engaged to a Teia for her family's sake and told herself love didn't matter, that a mistress or two was "what happens when two families join." Now a Teia boy proposes exactly that bargain, and it's the present Runa who wants more. The roles have crossed.

Three boys, three offers. Mitsuya offers rescue, and she remembers he didn't give it. Yuujirou shows her how marriages are traded, and keeps his own heart to himself. Eiichi offers marriage twice, first as a team-up and then as banking, and never once mentions love. The pact the boys made in Volume 3, to earn their own money first and only then confess, is visibly straining. Each of them is now competing with the others through her.

## Chapter 9: Poor Keikain Runa

"QUEEN BEE." The last chapter opens with the American name for the girl at the top of a school's social ladder, the one every clique forms around. Runa, now in her first term of junior high, is about to hold her first tea party, and the point of a tea party, she explains, is to announce, "I'm the queen bee, and I dare any of you to complain!" She isn't keen. A big show of strength will only call rival factions into being. Her adviser among the orphan girls, Nozuki Misaki, the one in glasses, disagrees. "You draw lots of attention, so you need to ensure you have a large enough group to destroy opposing factions once they form. If you don't, they'll only gang up on you." Runa, who belongs to an online game guild that Misaki runs, asks whether they're bringing guild wars to school. "Are you sure you should ask that, my lady? You already lead plenty of guild battles here in real life."

Before the guests arrive there's a small, lovely detail. Kushunnai Nanami, the orphan chosen as her body double, spends her days off admiring Runa's doll collection, and Runa gave her a porcelain doll as a present. Nanami sleeps with it in her arms. Then Ryuu Suzune, the girl from the Chinese expatriate network, gives Runa a political briefing so cold and clear that it explains the whole strategy of the adults since Volume 4.

America and Russia, Suzune says, place their people beside Runa because their influence over her is unofficial. The Japanese government doesn't need to; it can act legally, so it puts its agents around her family, her school and her companies instead. And Runa is easy to corner. She's secretive, keeps a small circle of elites, and "you can only control people from behind the scenes, since you aren't a legal adult yet." Then the finishing blow: "All they have to do is corner you at this moment, right when you want to act. By the time you're an adult, the prime minister will already have left his seat. You can't get around that, my lady."

Nobody needs to defeat Runa. They only need to hold her until Koizumi's term runs out. She has no answer, so she says what she'll do about the coming election: "I think I'll pour money into Izumikawa's faction and leave it at that." Suzune's community funds the opposition, and doesn't think much of it: "My grandfather told me they have the same eyes as the Red Guards," the young zealots of Mao's Cultural Revolution. "We don't plan things in terms of five or ten years. We make plans for the next fifty to one hundred years." Runa starts to reply, and stops. If the Chinese expatriates are sure she'll still matter in fifty years… She steps into the garden, and a thought hits her hard: *Try to remember the faces of Keikain Runa's followers in the game. Were any of them my current associates?!*

Then the guests come, and each of them brings a political message.

The first are Asuka and Hotaru. Asuka, the politician's daughter, draws Runa into a corner. Could she help her father in the election? He may lose his seat. She explains the opposition's arithmetic, a rule of thumb called the "987 rule": in a one-seat district, the opposition wins if it holds 90 percent of its own voters, takes 80 percent of the independents, and keeps the incumbent under 70 percent of his base. In her home prefecture of Ehime the fight is over "Environmental and water issues": a water project, a nuclear plant and a bullet-train line, all being attacked as a "three-point plan for environmental destruction." Hotaru looks stricken until Runa reassures her that the famous Shikoku pilgrimage, where she was once given mandarins, is safe. In the end Asuka only wants one thing: "I'd be really happy if you just bought lots of tickets for his fundraisers." Runa grins. "We're friends, right? Send the tickets my way."

The second is Asagiri Kaoru, her sister-in-law's younger sister, with two other noble girls. Kaoru belongs to the Courtiers, the academy's clique of old nobility, and she comes with a formal invitation and a plan from "the people up top." Runa should leave Izumikawa's faction, ally with the opposition, and call for a coalition government; with the Iwazaki conglomerate she could control Karafuto, help pass a no-confidence motion and bring down the cabinet, and so save the nobles' privileges. To Runa it sounds like the Taira clan of old going to their enemies the Minamoto for help, and she asks the obvious question: hasn't the opposition always wanted to strip the nobles of their privileges?

Then she asks Kaoru what she thinks, "not as a messenger, but as my friend." And Kaoru drops the message entirely. "If you aren't giving them any money, I think the opposition must be hopeless." Then, quietly: "We are nobles. We also both belong to zaibatsu. Don't you see that you're being set up as the heroine on the guillotine?"

"I know," Runa says. "That's exactly why I can't get rid of that prime minister."

Her reasoning is the political heart of the book. Against Koizumi, she can be a good loser and even win compromises. Allied with the opposition, she and Kaoru would either be overthrown completely or have to purge the opposition's own people. "It would be a bloodbath." A skilled enemy who compromises is safer than allies who can't govern. And Kaoru compares their situation to the court in the days of Taira no Kiyomori, the twelfth-century warlord whose clan fell soon after his death, when the warriors took power from the court for good.

Runa steps onto a balcony to think, and the faces come back to her. A girl in the game wore her hair in a bun exactly like Ryuu Suzune's. A background figure had the same "fierce, dark aura" as Glasya. "The game version of me had definitely been approached by a Chinese faction, at the very least." In the game, she realises, she would probably have gone along with the nobles' plan. And then a terrible thought: "Karafuto was struggling with the inefficiencies of socialism, so what's going to happen when they deal with bad debts in real life?!"

The third guest is Lydia, the senior from Karafuto, and her question is a test. "Do you honestly believe you can force Prime Minister Koizumi out in the next election?" "No. Nor do I care to try. Even if we managed to take over, we'd still be the minority. The office workers would chase us out." The two of them agree that the opposition, the secretaries and activists who do its real work, is divided and in the middle of purging itself, and wants above all the power to regulate. Then Runa asks the question she's been saving, "a missing link in this world's history": how far has the old Northern Japan regime "eaten into the opposition party?"

Lydia smiles and answers with a slogan, as if casting a spell: "Workers of the world, unite."

Runa understands. The communist North, during the Cold War, planted informants all through the South, and chose "those who couldn't do much on their own, since that made them easier to control. My father was one of these incompetents, though I didn't like admitting it." Lydia's own family were Northern officials who sold out their homeland at the right moment, and she can't imagine bowing to the people they once controlled. Runa answers in kind: then surely Lydia can understand that she'll never forgive the Northern government "for driving my father to his death?" The air turns icy. Both girls keep smiling. "Even if we were born in the same country, we probably would've still been friends," Lydia says, and then she tells a joke. How did her old country find people willing to help it in Japan? "They look for people who love historical dramas. Especially TV shows about shoguns and vice-shoguns who punish evildoers. Fans of those never fail to hit the mark." Runa laughs inwardly: *That's a good way to find idiots with blind faith in justice.*

I think that joke is the sharpest sentence in the book about Runa herself. Those dramas are about a hidden lord who appears in disguise and punishes the wicked. That's the plot of her own film in Chapter 3, the shape of her bank rescues, and what she tried to do in Iraq. And the glossary adds a line that stings: such stories "never solve the root of the problem whatsoever."

Then the easy guests arrive. Little Mio, a queen bee in her own right now, the girl the narration calls "the oil that made her class—no, the whole academy—run smoothly." With Lydia above her and Mio below, Runa realises, her independent faction will last at least three generations. The two girls from ordinary families, Akiko and Shizuka, nervous to be at such a grand party. And at the door, a quarrel: Nanami has stopped Kanna Mizuki, because Mizuki isn't wearing the Keikain badge. "Not at all," Runa says. "Welcome to my tea party, Kanna Mizuki-san." Sixty-nine girls come in all, fifty-nine of them from her year of a hundred.

When it's over, watching her associates clear up, Runa does what she's never done before: she treats the game like a case to be solved. Asuka, Hotaru, Kaoru, Yuka, Mio and Lydia were never in it. Her faction in the game had grown out of the nobles' quarrels over Karafuto. And then the question that has haunted her since Volume 4: "Was I ruined because I went against the Koizumi administration? No. That wasn't it. If my downfall was in 2008, the economic downturn must've done me in. I was actively anti-Koizumi in the game, so why didn't he stop me…?!"

Her vision blurs, the way it did at the Kannas', and she sees a smaller tea party, with the other Runa in the middle of it, wearing "a desperate look on my face."

"This is my declaration! As the Keikain Dukedom's heir, and one of the nobles carrying this country on their shoulders, Karafuto's state of poverty…"

*I see. I was acting as heir to the Keikain Dukedom in the game. I'd become the adult Prime Minister Koizumi kept me from becoming when he scolded me.*

"So I'm going to turn Karafuto into a special economic zone serving as a tax haven and law haven…"

"I'd finally found it. The last piece of the puzzle that laid out my downfall. Money laundering."

So this is what happened to the girl in the game. Her brother was dead, her family ruined, and she stood in as heir to the dukedom, exactly the role Kanbe described in Chapter 2: "be the one to bury the people you love." Karafuto was chosen as the place, a poor, half-foreign territory, and the ruined young duchess was chosen as the face. She used her noble immunity to make it a "law haven," a place where the law can't reach. In plain terms: a scheme to clean dirty money, which needed a famous, privileged face. The three boys were brought in for what their fathers could offer: Eiichi for finance, Yuujirou for politics, Mitsuya because his father understood tax. And it collapsed, not because of a school election or a heroine's courage, but because "the global financial crisis occurring at the same time had probably caused that final scene I saw."

The final scene of the game showed Runa alone at an airport. Now she understands that the girl probably never flew anywhere, and that the likelier end was being "sunk in the real ocean." And the heroine, Takanashi Mizuho, the girl the game told us was Runa's nemesis? "Had Takanashi Mizuho, the protagonist, known all that…? Probably not."

When she comes back to herself, someone is holding her hand. It's Hotaru. "Thanks." Hotaru nods and smiles. Across the room Kanna Mizuki, who saw the whole thing happen, simply waves goodbye and leaves. "I'm all right," Runa says. "Let's go back to the others."

And then she almost says something else. Her lips move, and no sound comes out.

*Poor Keikain Runa.*

"They were words no one in the room could hear."

I've read many villainess stories, and I don't think I've ever been so moved by one's last page. For five volumes Runa has been running from the girl in the game, the arrogant duke's daughter who bullied the heroine and got what she deserved. Now she knows the truth. That girl was a child put in front of other people's crime, used as a face and thrown away, while the classmates who condemned her never knew what they were looking at. Volume 4 hinted at it, calling the villainess "a sacrifice." Volume 5 gives us the mechanism, and with it the strongest argument the series has made: the prime minister's scolding was a rescue. In the game nobody stopped her, and she became "the adult" too early, and was used. In this world, the same prime minister removed her before anyone could.

And look at what this tea party has shown us. Everything that destroyed the game's Runa is present in this world, and larger: a noble face, a Karafuto constituency, Chinese and Russian patrons, legal immunity, three boys with useful fathers, and now a bank, a railway, identity cards, an armed company and a Swiss treasury. The nobles have just offered her the game's plot again, through a friend. The difference isn't her power. It's that she can recognise the guillotine when she sees it, and that the people around her tell her the truth. The original Keikain Runa had followers. This one has a friend who holds her hand.

Her last words aren't triumph at escaping a bad ending. They're pity for the girl who didn't.

## Afterwards: What Volume 5 says Runa is

Every volume of this series is quietly trying to answer one question: what is Keikain Runa? Volume 4 asked what she was allowed to be. Volume 5, the year she sat out, asks something gentler and harder. Who is she, once you take the role away?

Start with the names other people give her, because this book is full of them. At her own company's launch she is a mascot, smiling and silent. To the president she is "Your Little Majesty," and to the adults who sold the war with her picture, she knows, "a beautiful doll." A reporter calls her "a Cleopatra of the Heisei era"; the photographer calls her "my goddess"; the film director calls her "a monster," "a natural-born actress," and later warns her that people will fear she "sold your soul to the devil." Fans abroad call her a twenty-first-century Sarah Bernhardt. Professor Kanbe calls her one of "this country's two greatest geniuses" and compares her to a medieval kingmaker. A relative sneers at "the young lady from Sakata." A nationalist movement wants her as its icon; a magazine, briefed by her own family, calls her her managers' puppet. Angela, offering her the 2008 crash as a gift, calls her the rebirth of King Midas, and in the same breath "a kind person." Kaoru, a friend, tells her she's being set up as "the heroine on the guillotine."

Those are the grand names. Now listen to the ones she gives herself, because they're smaller and sadder. "A villainess… Ah, wait. That's exactly what I am." "A goddess, after all," said to a temple as a joke. One of the "decorative flowers in a solarium." "An extremely rare product with premiums attached." "A doting aunt." And, in the vault, remembering the prime minister: "a reckless child."

Then there's what the strange edges of the book say. A fox's jewel turns up in her pocket though she didn't bring it, and a fortune-teller tells her that knowing the future is "a curse that sets the future in stone," the one thing she has lived by her whole second life. And the little household spirit who has been blessing her since kindergarten chooses, this year, to become an ordinary girl, which may mean her luck is running out.

And then the darkest layer, which the book makes us face in Chapter 5. Runa is someone who could reach, step by honest step, a plan to kill millions of strangers for her country's sake, and who broke when she saw herself in it. Okazaki says it gently and exactly: "You broke because you realized you were capable of something so disgusting." I think the volume locates her humanity right there, not in her cleverness or her kindness, but in the breaking.

So who is she? The director came nearest to the truth without knowing it: "She already sees this world as a stage." She does. She has known since birth that she was born into a script, cast as its villain, with an ending already written. And the whole of this volume, for all its politics and money and war, is about the person underneath that role, and whether anyone will let her step out of it.

The book gives her two quiet moments of answer, and both are easy to miss. The first is in a car on her way to junior high, when her maid says that nothing would make her happier than to be useful, and Runa tells her, "I want you to think of at least one thing you're interested in for yourself. After all, we both have long lives ahead of us. Most likely." Hear the last two words. It's a girl who has read the end of her own story, saying she might not get a long life at all, and in the same breath wishing one for her friend.

The second is the last page. She has just seen the other girl, the real Keikain Runa, standing at a smaller tea party with a desperate face, announcing a scheme that other people needed her name for. A girl who buried her family, stood in as heir, bet her life on saving her house, and was spent and thrown away, while everyone around her believed she was simply wicked. The present Runa has been running from that girl for five volumes, as if she were an enemy. Now she sees her clearly for the first time, and what she feels isn't fear or relief.

It's pity.

*Poor Keikain Runa.*

I find that ending almost unbearably tender. For four volumes the story asked whether she could escape the villainess's fate. Volume 5 quietly changes the question. The villainess was never her enemy. She was a girl who wasn't saved. And the reason this Runa might be saved is in the same scene, so small you could miss it: when she comes back from the vision, Hotaru, who has just chosen to become human, is holding her hand.

Her last question in Chapter 2 was whether she would become an adult at all. The book doesn't answer it. But it ends with her lips moving silently, saying three words that no one else can hear, and those words aren't about the role. They're spoken by the person underneath it, grieving for the girl who never got the chance to be one.
