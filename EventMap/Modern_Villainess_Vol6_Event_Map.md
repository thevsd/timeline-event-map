**The author's stated thesis (afterword).** "History is like a large river and always heads in the same direction. However, dramatic historic events can still change its flow." For this story the events are "the 9/11 terrorist attacks, the Iraq War, and the 2008 financial crisis," and "this series depicts Keikain Runa spending years laying the groundwork to prepare for the global financial crisis in order to change history. It's meant to show just what a difficult process changing the flow of history can be." Written in spring 2024 as the Nikkei passed its bubble-era peak, the afterword looks back at 2003 — "an all-time low of 8,000 yen," the year "the ten lost years" entered use, when the author was "a novice worker" — and gives his advice to that younger self: "Buy stocks right now, and hold them forever."

# Part I — Chapter-by-Chapter Map
## Chapter 1 — The Daily Lives of Keika Group Office Workers

**Narrative position:** Third person, no Runa on the page; spring–summer 2003, after the Iraq War's main combat ("now that things are finally wrapping up in Iraq"). Five short scenes — Tokyo, the CEO's office, Angela's New York flat, a Manhattan hotel, Hong Kong — that survey the group from below and from outside. The volume opens the way Volume 4 did (with a banker's view) and Volume 5 did (with the household): by showing what Runa's decisions cost the people who carry them out.

**What happens**

*The financial analytics room (Tokyo)*
- The room that consolidates Keika Holdings' and the group's accounts never turns its lights off. Its staff are the group's under-performers, transferred there "as a restructuring strategy" — "better than being forced to resign," but "the shadowy side of the rapidly expanding Keika Group."
- They cannot close the books because Runa keeps buying: Akamatsu, Teisen Ishii, Teimen, Kanegana Textiles. Kanegana's cosmetics division has been propping up its other divisions and "fudge[d] their numbers" to do it; "the Keika Group is doing exactly what Kanegana does," except that it does not need to fudge.
- The change to current-value and **consolidation accounting** has buried every Japanese company in work. Akamatsu's resource department is "a total can of worms" — and the source of most group profit: "They're the pillar of the Moonlight Fund, and the Moonlight Fund is what supports the rest of the group."
- Office gossip maps the politics: the former Finance Ministry men's terms are ending ("musical chairs"); the factions of Hokkaido Kaitaku, Long-Period Credit and Nihon Credit are waiting for the winner; the next CEO will not come from Far Eastern; Katsura is "way too young"; rumours of a merger with Imperial Iwazaki, because the Keikain house's new hires are mostly Iwazaki-connected — "then why'd they remove Nakamaro-sama as an independent director?"; the government fears Keika becoming an "institutional bank."
- On Angela: promoted to executive director of Keika Securities' North American division, tipped for Keika Holdings' board and as next CEO — and "a puppet for the United States." Background given: she had been Far East fund manager at the "Pacific Global Investment Fund" and had an offer from "Silver Woman" before Tachibana recruited her.
- The **nationalists** resist her rise: "Editors-in-chief at major newspapers joined hands with recipients of the highest orders of merit to get Governor Iwasawa Makoto elected"; their line on the listing is "Are you sure you want European and American hedge funds to play with the Moonlight Fund, Japan's treasure, as they please?"
- **The "Pandora's box":** the Long-Period Credit and Nihon Credit banks "catered to Nagatacho"; most of the box "came from the Hashizume faction." The "conspiracy theory" is that Koizumi's drive against bad debts and general contractors is aimed at it — and would hurt him too.
- The listing will force the group to "release control of the Moonlight Fund… It was built without structure for a reason, so it'll definitely come under attack."
- Deposit insurance is now capped at ¥10 million plus interest; large depositors are moving money to zaibatsu banks, as they did in the Shōwa financial panic.
- An employee bursts in: "She did it again! The young lady just bought Zugagaga Entertainment!" — screams, flying documents.

*The CEO's office*
- Ichijou to Katsura, privately: "Isn't it sad… That being at the height of your glory doesn't matter when the next problem arises." The retinue walking behind them is mocked as a "daimyo's procession."
- **Katsura is moved to Keika Trust Bank's trust administration, "in charge of the young lady's assets."** It looks like a demotion; Katsura is calm — "Tachibana-san's already scolded me once." Ichijou: "of all the people in the world I'd like to punch, you're in second place… That would be Okazaki Yuuichi."
- Why: "Because this company's game of musical chairs is about to get a lot more intense." Had Katsura come from Kaitaku's main faction, or from Far Eastern, he would have been given the Tokyo branch. Katsura guesses that the next CEO is Angela; Ichijou does not deny it.

*Angela in New York*
- Dinner with her daughter **Jennifer**, whom she has raised alone; then a secure call to the CIA's head of foreign leadership analysis. "This face belonged to a Central Intelligence Agency agent."
- Her report: "Thanks to Tachibana Ryuuji-shi's efforts, the coup within the Keika Group won't ignite"; outside forces are interfering; Runa "doesn't seem like she'll fall for their tricks."
- Langley: Asian stability is the priority after Iraq; Koizumi "ended up going all in with us on Iraq. We have to repay him"; the president "didn't want to see our mutual friend the prime minister fight with the young lady."
- **Maefuji's intelligence:** Middle Eastern religious extremists influenced the failed Shinjuku Geofront attack; domestic cells were given the job "to eliminate the young lady"; if foreign forces reactivate them, "they'll use Karafuto as their base."
- Opposition forces have no one to rally around: "I'm worried they may choose the young lady as their figurehead." Angela: Runa "truly won't oppose him this time." Silence on the line.
- Two assignments: take in a **witness-protection informant** who laundered money for the Russian mafia on Wall Street until "a certain young lady went a bit over-the-top and broke the washing machine very publicly"; and watch **Tenmabashi Mitsuru** of Keika Corp, ex-Teisen Ishii, "one of the Japanese businessmen who made quite a fuss in places like London and Jerusalem," with "lots of friends in Washington." Even Okazaki is careful of him.
- Looking at the skyline: "The Twin Towers… They used to be right there…"

*Karin Viola recruits*
- Karin, now CEO of **Keika Electronics Union**, hires her former Portercon colleague **Chloe Meyers**, a House legislative staffer since the lost proxy fight ("our proxy war was nothing more than child's play"), as executive for North America.
- Karin needs someone "who will never betray me": Furukawa and Shiyo board members could unseat her. The business: Southeast Asian factories and West Coast logistics consolidated, middlemen cut, heavy TV advertising — "My boss turned out to be an effective face for the company." Runa hired her in a café over cake, showing her the news of the Portercon buy-out on TV.
- Chloe's price (a team, offices on both coasts and in Tokyo) is granted instantly — a sign of how exposed Karin feels. Her map of the group: Runa; Tachibana (butler), Ichijou (treasurer), Toudou (resources) — and "Okazaki Yuuichi, the manager of the Moonlight Fund. I believe he's a key player." Okazaki is Angela's "mortal enemy," and the man who recruited Karin.

*Okazaki in Hong Kong*
- His Hong Kong triad contact, enriched by betting alongside him during the Furukawa operation, now calls him "Brother"; one of Okazaki's hiding places for the ¥100 billion reward Runa gave him is in their office.
- He wants information on **"Gekkou Investment Firm"** — an imitator ("Gekkou" is Japanese for "moonlight") with top Chinese Communist Party officials working for it and a Shanghai organisation behind it.
- **Ryuu Suzune** walks in, in a qipao: the triad boss's granddaughter, sent to be married to him. "It's not right for two men to be whispering in front of a girl of marriageable age" — said by a junior-high-school student.
- Under a giant poster of Runa on a Sougou store: *My lady, you're really going to shake this world up, aren't you?… what will you show the whole world next?* He shapes his fingers into a gun: "Bang!"

**Revelations**
- The group cannot even count its own money: consolidation accounting is exposing how much depends on the Moonlight Fund and Akamatsu's resource trading.
- The listing of Keika Holdings means losing control of the Moonlight Fund, and the nationalists are using that to fight Angela's succession.
- Nakamaro has been removed as an independent director — a break with the main family the employees can see.
- Angela's family: a daughter, Jennifer, raised alone (the husband vanished in Japan c. 1989 — Vol. 3).
- Domestic terror cells were tasked with killing Runa; Karafuto is the expected base for the next attempt.
- A Chinese imitation of the Moonlight Fund exists, protected by Party officials.
- Okazaki is being married into a Hong Kong triad family through a junior-high-school girl who is also one of Runa's associates.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** consolidation accounting ("made it harder to separate accounts to hide losses — or hide profits"); the US Witness Protection Program.
- **Real, used accurately:** Japan's shift to consolidated, market-value accounting around 2000–03; the deposit-insurance cap of ¥10 million plus interest (the real "payoff" limit, lifted for time deposits in 2002 and for ordinary deposits in 2005); the Shōwa financial panic of 1927, when depositors fled small banks for zaibatsu banks; the real suspicion that the Long-Term Credit Bank and Nippon Credit Bank had political loan books; the real "Hashimoto faction"–construction links.
- **My identifications:** Kanegana Textiles = **Kanebo** (its profitable cosmetics arm carried loss-making textile and other divisions, and its accounts were later found to have been falsified); Teisen Ishii and Teimen = **Nissho Iwai and Nichimen** (merged as Sojitz); Zugagaga Entertainment = **Sega** (as in Vol. 5); "Silver Woman" = Goldman Sachs; Honami and Gowa Osan = Mizuho and UFJ; Portercon = Compaq; Karin Viola = Carly Fiorina. *Plausible:* "Pacific Global Investment Fund" echoes PIMCO (Pacific Investment Management Company). **Uncertain:** whether the witness's "washing machine" is Runa's WorldCom operation (Vol. 4) or another of her operations; whether Tenmabashi has a real model.
- **Altered:** Kanebo's real collapse came in 2004–05; here it is already a Keika purchase in 2003.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The "shopping sprees" have victims inside the group.** The opening joke — accountants screaming at each Runa purchase — sits on top of a real point: the group is now so large that only consolidation can show what it is, and consolidation shows that it is Kanebo with honest books, a holding company carried by one profitable division.
- *Strongly supported:* **Every scene is a succession problem.** Ichijou is preparing his exit and moving Katsura out of the race; Karin is buying loyalty; Angela is the heir and also the CIA's officer; the nationalists want to stop an American running "Japan's treasure"; Okazaki is being married into a triad. The volume's first chapter asks who will hold the group after the founders.
- *Plausible:* **Ichijou's "musical chairs" line ties back to Vol. 2 Ch. 5**, where he refused to hire anyone in order not to start that game. Now it has started anyway.
- *Plausible:* **Okazaki's "Bang!" is ambiguous on purpose.** It is a gambler's salute to the person who gives him the biggest table in the world; it is also a man surrounded by intelligence services pointing a gun at the girl he serves. The volume does not resolve which.
- *Speculative:* **Gekkou ("moonlight") Investment** is a mirror image of the Moonlight Fund — a Chinese state-backed copy of what she built — and may be the volume's foreign rival.

**Connections:** Vol. 2 Ch. 5 (Ichijou's "musical chairs"); Vol. 3 Ch. 4 (Angela's vanished husband); Vol. 4 Chs. 2 and 6 (the Furukawa operation; the exit dates; Karin's recruitment); Vol. 5 Chs. 1 (the family "trial" and the nationalist movement), 3–4 (Angela and the CIA desk), 6 (the Geofront plotters), 7 (Ryuu Suzune, the associates, the US operation against Okazaki).

## Chapter 2 — The Final Chapter of Bad Debt Disposal

**Narrative position:** Runa's POV, April–May 2003 (from the founding of the postal corporation to after Golden Week). The volume's plot engine: a coup against her inside the Keika Group is revealed, traced to Karafuto Bank, and tied to her role in the game. Six scenes: Ichijou in her office; katsudon with the police; the executive luncheon; Tenmabashi's briefing; Fuchigami in hospital; the American embassy.

**What happens**

*The end of the bad-debt era*
- **1 April 2003:** the semi-governmental **Imperial Japan Post** is born, a first step toward Koizumi's postal privatisation. The markets read it as a sign that problem banks will be nationalised or forced into mergers.
- Keika Holdings, "which has basically functioned as a garbage can," is to be listed as the model of bad-debt disposal. Runa: after that, "Who's supposed to save troubled banks?… No one is around to fill that role." No bank has asked Keika for help: absorbing one now would delay the listing, which Koizumi and Takenaga do not want. The lesson, in her own narration: bad debts are now handled "not through tactics that crossed into a gray area but through fundamental principles in accordance with the law."
- "Well, I'm a junior high schooler now too," she remarks, "so I can't get away with being reckless any longer." Ichijou: "The hardest part of gambling is quitting while you're ahead. But now you've got the dealer going out of their way to signal you." Runa: "And leave a casino that'll ban me from entering again, right?"
- **The megabanks.** Gowa Osan will not survive: behind on bad debts and in trouble with the Financial Services Agency for resisting its audit, it will be broken up "as an example." Honami's fate is uncertain. Imperial Iwazaki and Futaki-Yodoyabashi compete to absorb them. Angela had proposed that Keika absorb both and become a world-class megabank; Runa refuses and stays an "outside observer."
- **Honami's computer system:** Keika Electronics Union inherited it, with **TIG Backup Systems — of which Runa is vice-president** — helping develop it. She has withdrawn, which ruined the Honami faction that backed using her system and produced the slogan "We can't go to Keika for help!" as Honami hunts for ¥1 trillion.
- **Karafuto Bank** — the former central bank of the Northern Japanese government, nationalised and loaded with all of Karafuto's banks' bad debts — may be merged with Honami or Gowa Osan and absorbed by the Japan Post Bank. Runa's private certainty: **in the game, Karafuto Bank "had been the name behind that money laundering, and the institution that controlled me."** She asks Ichijou to investigate. "Sadly, that prediction would prove correct."

*Katsudon at the police box*
- At the Kudanshita police box, Runa serves katsudon (beef, matsutake, Hokkaido rice) to Inspector Ono and Director Maefuji, who is still banned from visiting the Keikains. Joke: katsudon is actually banned from interrogation rooms.
- **Runa: the ransom in her Vol. 1 kidnapping came from Karafuto Bank.**
- **Maefuji:** the police already suspect Karafuto is "the new place chosen to launder underground money that popped up when Hong Kong was handed over to China"; restoration money "slip[ped] out of sight," and Karafuto Bank — run by the Karafuto prefectural government and the **Karafuto Reconstruction Agency** — "was the entrance and exit for all that money."
- **The history:** the opposition ruled Japan when the North collapsed. Its prime minister, a noble, won high ratings for unification, then fell over the **"National Welfare Tax,"** which was "essentially a Karafuto restoration tax." The coalition broke up and the present ruling party formed; the next governments left Karafuto's restoration to the zaibatsu, and underground money followed. "Zaibatsu contain nobles, and nobles have diplomatic immunity." Crimes were excused as "acts for the sake of Japan's unification," the prime minister "took the ultimate responsibility, while the government was reduced to a nest of demons." The former prime minister is still protected as a noble.
- Ono: "there's no 'good' and 'evil' when it comes to money… that's the one problem that can't be solved."
- Eva asks whether she will really open "that Pandora's box." Runa: "Yes, I am… I know the prime minister would open it if it was him."

*The executive luncheon*
- The weekly executive lunch has grown to 500 executives. "I believe meeting in person like this and eating a meal together leads to a sense of solidarity."
- Factions on display: in **Keika Railway**, East and West Japan Imperial Railway alumni fight for Tachibana's seat (he becomes a chairman without the right to represent the company); in **Keika Holdings**, open hostility to Angela, with former city bankers plotting to succeed or block her; **Keika Electronics Union**'s Karin networking in English; **Keika Corp** mid-merger and tense. Okazaki eats lunch next to Runa and ignores all of it.
- Introductions: **Mikihara Kazuaki** (East Japan line), Keika Railway's next president; **Mark M. Barnes**, ex-Silver Woman, recruited by Angela for Keika Securities — "you haven't fired anyone big yet" (Runa: "That's not my style"); **Chloe Meyers**.

*Tenmabashi's warning — the coup*
- **Tenmabashi Mitsuru** (Keika Corp, Osaka; ex-Teisen Ishii) stops her at the door: "**My lady, a coup d'état is currently underway within the Keika Group to remove you from power.**" She offers him *bubuzuke* (the Kyoto signal to leave); he eats it and asks for seconds.
- In her private room, with Tachibana, Ichijou, Toudou, Okazaki and Eva: the plot spans Keika Corp, Holdings, Electronics Union and Railway.
- **The dice.** "Do ya want odd or even? You've got lots of chips left to bet. Head home now, and you can still have yourself a triumphant sushi feast." Okazaki decodes it: **the coup targets her not because Koizumi beat her, but because she keeps winning.** "Only a few of 'em… are capricious enough to bet the rest of their lives on your gambles." "If you stay behind and spend all your chips, none of us will get to go home either."
- Tenmabashi's diagnosis: Japanese members are uniting against the foreigners she brought in; East and West fight in Railway; Corp is in musical chairs. "You're payin' the price for not purging those people with your iron fist when you had the chance." Ichijou, a regional banker, is a minority on his own board, protected only as her spokesman and as one of two people with access to the Fund.
- **Why now (Tachibana):** the Diet is debating **injections of public funds** into all megabanks. Public money converted into preferred shares could outvote her stake (glossary), and a hostile board could refuse to return funds Keika does not need. And the plotters will build up **"your pops, the Keikain duke"** to "keep you locked away for good" — the glossary names the practice: *oshikome*, vassals confining a lord.
- **Gekkou Investment Firm:** a mainland "Moonlight Fund knockoff," protected by bribes or big political figures, "imitating the Moonlight Fund's actions to perform their money laundering. You've done too much, little missy" — she regrets "the thirty-five billion dollars I'd recently made off with." What they want is "fame… the Moonlight Fund's reputation as **Her Majesty's Escort Fleet**" (Ichijou: the convoy system, upgraded; "London must be involved").
- **The real target is the yen.** The Iraq war is going badly (Turkey "basically in a civil war" over the Kurds; rumours that the American defence and state secretaries will be replaced); America's twin deficits will force easing and bond issuance, and the yen will rise. Gekkou is raising cash by posing as a Moonlight shell; after the coup it will use her name to attack Japan's currency, with a story everyone will believe — that she is going after the prime minister. The opposition will claim her support, turn Izumikawa's faction, and force Koizumi out or into a dissolution.
- Tachibana completes it: since she refuses to oppose Koizumi, the target has shifted to **the duke**. If rumours say Koizumi was behind the coup, Kiyomaro "will have no choice but to fight against the prime minister to protect Lady Runa."
- Runa names the money behind Gekkou: "**Karafuto Bank.**" Tenmabashi's eyes confirm it.
- "Pretty sad, ain't it? If you'd just been born twenty years earlier, you probably could've climbed to the very top of this country…" — "Sad? I don't want to have to shoulder any more burdens than I already do." She makes him **vice-president of Keika Corp, in charge of stopping the coup.**
- Parting shot: **Barnes was a fund manager at Karafuto Bank's New York branch** before Silver Woman, "up to no good there."

*The timetable*
- An underground economics magazine reports the formation of **Keika Corp Holdings** over Akamatsu, Teisen Ishii, Teimen and Kanegana, with Toudou as president and Tenmabashi as vice-president and chairman of organisational reform. Tenmabashi: "I'm surprised no one's tried to kill me over this."
- His reading of the schedule: the Fund is accessible only to Tachibana and Ichijou; Tachibana has lost his power; so the target is Ichijou. Public-fund votes come to a head in July; a snap election would come in July; so: **a coup at Keika Holdings in June; Kiyomaro then has Izumikawa bring a no-confidence vote; a snap election in July.**
- **The trigger is Keika Railway, not Electronics Union.** "Traps are traps because they're laid where you least expect 'em." The plan is to turn Keika Holdings' loans for the Shinjuku Shinkansen and Shin-Tokiwa line into bad debts — cause to remove Ichijou.
- **The Geofront plot was part of it.** Tenmabashi has "a hunch" about where some of its money came from: "So the plan to remove me has been in the works for quite some time." Tachibana and Eva look away. Tachibana had moved those who "made fools of the Keikains" elsewhere in the group — which is how the plot migrated from the family to the group.
- **Why Karafuto Bank wants her gone:** its bad debts lie outside the Finance Ministry's sight, under the Karafuto Reconstruction Agency and the Cabinet Office — "a matter of jurisdiction." "If the terrorist attacks were intended to turn Keika Railway's funds into bad debt…" — "What're a few terrorist attacks if they'll get spare change out of 'em?"

*Fuchigami's warning*
- At the **Imperial Keikain Memorial Hospital** — built with Keika Pharmaceuticals money on the dukedom's land, where scandal-hit grandees hide behind noble privilege, once a target of the second February 26 rebels — Fuchigami is "lying low." Prosecutors are after him over the small-business foundation and the **Imperial Mobile Phones** shares; Runa suspects Koizumi leaked it. He sits in the Privy Council, which Izumikawa chairs.
- "**Your Little Majesty, where do you intend to become queen?… You shouldn't be in control of a country… International society — history itself — treats actresses like you like you're disposable.**"
- His world view: 9/11 began an "asymmetrical war"; the world will divide into America and everyone else; Japan should take America's side — "I believe it's good that Prime Minister Koizumi is currently leading this country."
- "You're already a superstar of history who can't run away from anyone. Your stage was chosen the moment you were born." Runa: "Maybe I wasn't suited to a role in Russia, but I would be perfectly cast in Karafuto."

*The ferries — an "alarm trap"*
- The US wants to buy Keika's four Tokyo–Hokkaido ferries, two already in the Persian Gulf and two still being built. Runa stages an angry scene at the American embassy while Eva gathers information.
- Tenmabashi: it is an **"alarm trap."** The ships belong to Kitakaba Security, are rented on paper to the Self-Defense Forces in Iraq and used by the multinational force; the SDF and Foreign Ministry wanted to please the Americans. Her reaction revealed two things: how much control she still has (she noticed only because her private luxury cabins were removed and she was billed), and that she is still watching Iraq. Going straight to the embassy looks like "forming connections with the United States to oppose the Japanese government."
- The SDF absorbed the North's army, and most volunteers for Iraq are ex-Northern; the Foreign Ministry is full of impoverished nobles, country factions and absorbed Northern diplomats. "I think it was people on our side who decided to sell your toys before you could play with any more fire" — with "professionals" (spies) helping. Runa: their good intentions are "so incredibly wicked."
- Eva's report on Iraq: Turkey is entering Iraq over the Kurds; Iran will not negotiate; the Secretary of State has lost face; the occupation authority has banned the Ba'ath Party, setting loose "**six hundred thousand armed Ba'ath Party members**." "**There, in the deserts of the Middle East, Vietnam was trying to rear its ugly head.**"

**Revelations**
- A coordinated coup is under way across the whole group, timed to the public-funds vote and a July snap election, using her adoptive father as the figurehead.
- Karafuto Bank links three things: the ransom in her kidnapping, the money behind Gekkou and the coup, and — in the game — the laundering scheme that destroyed her.
- The Shinjuku Geofront plot (Vol. 3) was partly an attack on Keika Railway's balance sheet, part of a long campaign to remove her.
- The adults (Tachibana, Eva) knew about the long-running plot and kept it from her.
- In this world, unification with the North was paid for by a "National Welfare Tax" that brought down the prime minister of the day.
- Runa is vice-president of the boys' company's backup-systems arm.
- She made $35 billion recently — the Iraq-war oil trade (Vol. 5 Ch. 4).

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the "noble prime minister" is **Hosokawa Morihiro** (heir of the Higo-Hosokawa daimyo and the Konoe dukes); public funds become preferred stock or subordinated debt that can carry votes; *oshikome* (*shukun oshikome*), the detention of a lord by his vassals; **George Soros**, "the man who broke the Bank of England"; Teisen Ishii and arms dealing — the **Douglas-Grumman scandal**; the Imperial Mobile Phones scandal = the **NTT Docomo** shares scandal; "politics is about numbers…" = **Tanaka Kakuei**; "blood and iron" = **Bismarck**; the 2003 **Hanshin Tigers** under Hoshino; the "Multinational Provisional Authority" = the **Coalition Provisional Authority**.
- **Real, used accurately:** Japan Post was created as a public corporation on 1 April 2003; Mizuho's 2002 computer failure and its scramble for about ¥1 trillion of new capital in early 2003; UFJ's later conflict with the FSA over inspections; the occupation authority's de-Ba'athification order and disbanding of the Iraqi army (May 2003), widely blamed for the insurgency.
- **Altered:** Hosokawa's real "national welfare tax" (February 1994) was withdrawn within days; here it is enacted to pay for Karafuto. UFJ's real FSA conflict came in 2004 and it merged into Mitsubishi Tokyo in 2005–06; here it is to be broken up in 2003. Turkey did not invade Iraq in 2003; the American defence and state secretaries were not replaced that year; the "six hundred thousand" armed Ba'athists is the novel's figure (the real disbanded army was several hundred thousand; Ba'ath Party members banned from office were far fewer).
- **Fictional:** Karafuto Bank, the Karafuto Reconstruction Agency, Gekkou Investment Firm, the Keikain hospital.
- **My identifications:** Honami = **Mizuho**; Gowa Osan = **UFJ**; Imperial Japan Post = **Japan Post**; "Her Majesty's Escort Fleet" plays on the convoy system (*gosō sendan*, "escort-fleet" method).

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The coup is the casino's verdict.** Volume 4's adults stopped her for moral reasons; here her own employees want her gone for economic ones. Winners make the table nervous; people with millions do not want to watch a girl bet hundreds of billions. It is the most ordinary motive in the series, and the most convincing.
- *Strongly supported:* **The game's ruin is arriving by the same door.** Karafuto Bank was the laundering machine behind the game-Runa's fall (Vol. 5 Ch. 9). Here it funds the coup that would hand her to her father and make her an anti-Koizumi figurehead. She has refused the role; the plotters are trying to cast her anyway.
- *Strongly supported:* **Fuchigami completes the Vol. 2 warning.** In Vol. 2 he told her not to take her grandfather's path. Now he tells her not to be queen of anything: history "treats actresses like you like you're disposable." It is the same counsel Vol. 5's film director gave — queen, dictator or bride — given by someone who knows where the money went.
- *Plausible:* **"If you'd just been born twenty years earlier"** — the old trader means she could have ruled Japan in the bubble era. The book means the opposite: born when she was, she arrived as the era turned against people like her.
- *Plausible:* **The ferries are a small model of the whole coup.** Well-meaning people inside her own group sell her "toys" so she cannot "play with fire" again. The good intention is the method.
- *Strongly supported:* **"Vietnam was trying to rear its ugly head"** is Runa recognising the future she tried to prevent in Vol. 5 Ch. 5. Without the terror she wanted, the occupation is producing the insurgency she remembered.

**Connections:** Vol. 1 Ch. 4 (the kidnapping and its ransom); Vol. 2 Ch. 5 (Fuchigami's warning; the Docomo scandal's real model); Vol. 2 Ch. 6 (the Courtiers' offer of Karafuto); Vol. 3 Chs. 6, 9 and 11 (the Geofront plot; the ferries; the police); Vol. 4 Chs. 6, 9 and 12 (exit dates; Koizumi; "the Ministries"); Vol. 5 Chs. 1, 4, 5 and 9 (the family "trial"; the oil profit; the Iraq plan; the game's Karafuto haven).

## Chapter 3 — The First Time's a Tragedy; The Second Time's a Comedy

**Narrative position:** Runa's first person; spring 2003, shortly after Ch. 2's revelation of the coup timetable. Four set pieces — the main Keikain residence, Professor Kanbe's seminar room, Holland Town in Nagasaki, the TIG Backup Systems office — plus a bonus story and a glossary. The chapter is the volume's philosophical centre: three adults (Nakamaro, Kanbe, Karin) each tell her what an adult is, and a fourth (Mark Barnes) offers her a country.

**What happens**

*The main residence: Michimaro*
- She meets her newborn nephew, **Keikain Michimaro** — "the soul who was never supposed to have been born" (Nakamaro "hadn't been alive in the game story" — Vol. 5 Ch. 4; he had been booked for New York on 11 September 2001). The "-maro" ending marks him as the house's heir.
- **Sakurako** (née Asagiri) was raised by the Asagiri family "for the purpose of strengthening the Iwazaki zaibatsu through a political marriage"; she married Nakamaro because he was "seen as a capable man." The main family is not powerless: **Keika-Iwazaki-Hatabe Pharma** steers itself through its strategic partnership with the megapharma **Arts Nova**.
- Angela's verdict on Nakamaro: "He's a capable man, so long as you ignore the fact that he knows what he lacks." Runa agrees: a second- or third-generation stabiliser, not a founder — and his stepping down is "a major reason the Keika Group could reorganize around me—someone with blood other than the main family's."
- Runa: "There's a plot in place within the Keika Group to remove me from power. Your son is one reason for that, isn't he?" Nakamaro: "That's right… people get uglier when more money is involved."
- Being female is part of the problem: an "outsider man" might someday control her and through her the group.
- **Two sources of the plot.** (1) The Keikain **branch families and successive generations**, who have not given up on restoring the main family's leadership — "there's still talk within the family that Father or I should lead the Keika Group." Runa, a minor, has to "haul" her legal guardians along; Tachibana's step-down as proxy guardian is "a huge demerit." (2) "**The people who do us favors so they can cozy up to the Keikain family**" — "more of a threat than I first thought." If the family accepted their help, they "could look to eliminate threats with their diplomatic immunity—legally or illegally," and "that sort of thing had happened many times before."
- The plots are many and separate, and the plotters justify themselves by believing **she will be safer if the group is taken from her**. Father and brother have used Tachibana to circulate the story of her planned ousting "to determine how safe I was."
- **Father's position:** "He says he won't do anything about this incident. If the government turns out to be influencing the plan to overthrow you, he'll use the Privy Council to protect you." Runa: "the worst strategy I could imagine" — though if her safety is his priority, letting her be removed is logical.
- **A second account of her father's death.** "Father won't let there be a repeat of what happened to my uncle, your birth father… The Fellowship of Constitutional Government's administration in those days was what drove Uncle to commit suicide. He was backed into a corner to escape America's wrath and protect then-Chief Cabinet Secretary Katou. Father and I don't want you to end up like he did."
- Runa, inwardly: "Ah. When you put it like that, Father, it makes me want to go and support the opposition…"

*Professor Kanbe's seminar*
- Kanbe opens with Nobunaga betrayed by Akechi and Caesar by Brutus: "There's a reason behind everything."
- The group's **fatal flaw**: "That I, the leader at the top of the organization, don't have the right to control it?" — "Correct." Her **only official title is Vice President of TIG Backup Systems**; Tachibana, Ichijou and Toudou act on her direction, but "I had no actual power. It was a warped structure."
- To Erika's question: "**there's no official organization called the Moonlight Fund.**" Its staff in Kudanshita Tower "work for Keika Holdings or Keika Corp's resource management departments on paper." Withdrawals of hundreds of billions were made by Tachibana and Ichijou "as my guardians." Kanbe: the structure was meant to protect her from fallout, and it has worked "too effectively." Runa reads the coup as her own over-reacting "defense mechanism" after the defeat by Koizumi.
- The easy solution is a post that controls everything — "Power comes with responsibility. I wouldn't have time to talk to you like this anymore." Kanbe: "Right. We call that 'growing up.'"
- "Just what is an 'adult,' anyway?" Kanbe: the law and society define her as a child; adults will say "Stop babbling and go to school." He tempts her — "you might be able to destroy society and its laws" — then names the result: someone forced to decide and answer for everything. "History calls such people 'dictators.'"
- "It's adults' duty to scold children when they go down the wrong path." — "What about my path was wrong?" — "You, personally, haven't made any wrong choices yet. But I don't know what the future will hold once the poison of power infects you. **It's your position that's wrong.**"
- "You aren't considering your own happiness at all, are you?… you've never once told me your dreams, your future aspirations, or what makes you happy." "You probably have the power to change the world on your own, but you'll have to become a victim of this world to do it… **It's all right for you to seek your own happiness. You're a child, so you can choose the people at your side—it's not for this world, or for history, to decide for you.**"
- His "magic words" for an adult faced with a child's irrationality: "**Give up. We adults have already learned to accept that.**" She shakes her head: to accept them would be to "resign myself to my future ruin." Kanbe: "It's a tough thing to be too much of a genius. I guess it's natural to reject those magic words, since you can see the future." (He lights a cigarette and leaves it unsmoked in the ashtray.)
- At the college report box she meets **Eiichi** (they take the same class); she makes him buy her a parfait. "I couldn't help wanting to become an adult with Eiichi-kun and my friends. That was why I also couldn't help being aware that I was one step behind on the coup."

*Holland Town, Sasebo*
- With Eva and **Mark Barnes**, she views **Holland Town** (Nagasaki): Honami Bank executives trail them, desperate to see ¥200 billion of bad debts disposed of before Honami takes public funds. "It's the real deal." — "Why is it performing so terribly?" — "Because it's the real thing."
- Mark's lesson in the theme-park business: parks do not pay for themselves; they gather people so money can be made elsewhere — railways carry the visitors, and **residential districts** built beside the parks pay back construction. In Sasebo (pop. 250,000) the houses would only become holiday homes.
- The holiday-home district was not sold during the bubble because the president refused — selling it would be "like predicting rice profits while the paddies were still green" (pre-selling before development), to avoid resale trouble. The bank did not object because "**Commercial Bank of Japan** was the one promoting that project at the time, and their **charismatic former president** proposed this theme park."
- Runa: "His charisma had made him untouchable, but that was his fatal flaw… Things could turn out the same way for me if I mess up." Mark: "I believe two hundred billion yen is a small price to pay for that lesson."
- After a bailout the park will struggle (Kyushu tourists stay in Fukuoka or Nagasaki and prefer hot springs such as Ureshino, Takeo, Shimabara), but the **Dutch government** has "a keen eye on" it, its theme is environmental research — useful leverage with the EU amid US–Europe tension, and good patronage for a group "criticized for its political connections."
- Mark: "if you can take it on for fifty or a hundred years, it should become an irreplaceable source of profits for you and the Keikain family." Runa: "**Are you suggesting I turn this place into my own country?**" — Mark does not laugh; she reads in his eyes "a sense of deep darkness."
- She hears Fuchigami: "Your Little Majesty, you shouldn't be in control of a country." "I don't feel like playing queen here. But let's have Keika buy this place for the sake of patronizing culture and get them as far out of the red as possible."
- Eva on Mark: "It's best not to trust him, but as you can see, he's someone you can use."

*TIG Backup Systems: the training guide*
- With her new secretary **Tokitou Aki**, she finds a cameraman filming the office: **Karin Viola** has hired a team to make a company **training guide**, and is having "an American research institution" analyse the footage.
- Karin's case: Japan has no "beginner's training guides in the truest sense" — only "Learn to do the job by watching everyone else" and "steal" techniques. A multinational that bought Portercon cannot run "solely on Japanese logic"; workers in the US and East Asia, and products made in East Asia and Karafuto, need explicit guides. Runa recalls the same problem from her past life.
- Two analysed cases: veterans on a production line quietly correcting a slightly faulty machine (newcomers never told); the tea-serving women whose memory of clients' names and teas explained a division's sales — written into a manual, with their permission. "That's the strength of the United States… We know how to analyze things and put them into training guides. It's because we can do things like that that the United States has successfully incorporated immigrants from so many countries."
- The Furukawa and Shiyo manuals are useless "to any new hire, much less a temporary worker expected to perform right away."
- **Restructuring:** around cell phones, as Runa wants; layoffs "a last resort"; "**Laying off workers after a merger is the worst thing anyone could do.** The company would lose all the techniques and training guides it acquired." She has three years.
- **Quasi-stock options:** one share's worth (≈ ¥3,000) paid to every worker, including irregular employees, on top of bonuses — tying pay to the share price (¥2,500 in a bad year, ¥3,500 in a good one) and making the restructuring "much more intense."
- Runa needles her: stock up through layoffs is popular in America. Karin: "**how could I ever run the company so disgracefully when I know my successors will have to follow in my footsteps?** Besides, my lady, you're someone who started out on the other side of that glass ceiling… People like Angela and me are providing one of the routes you can take in life."
- The finished guide gains a line: "*Lady Keikain Runa, major shareholder. Drinks grape juice exclusively.*"

*Bonus story*
- The boys cannot hold a TIG meeting on camera. Filming all of Keika Electronics Union reveals that smokers are being "secretly sweet-talked" before official meetings, which leads to "a big fuss over meetings and smoking bans."

**Revelations**
- Michimaro exists only in this timeline; his birth makes the main family a dynasty again and is one motive of the coup.
- The coup is plural: branch families who want Kiyomaro or Nakamaro back at the top, and favour-seekers who would use the family's legal privilege "legally or illegally" — some of whom believe removing her protects her.
- Kiyomaro will not intervene unless the government is involved, and then only through the Privy Council.
- **Otsumaro's death, second version:** the then-ruling Fellowship administration drove him to suicide to placate the United States and shield Chief Cabinet Secretary Katou. This fits Vol. 1 Ch. 4, where Katou was revealed as complicit in the cover-up and her father "needed to be sacrificed" to close it. (Vol. 2 Ch. 7 said Tachibana "pushed him to suicide"; Vol. 5 Ch. 9 had Runa conclude he was an instrument of the North's informant network. The accounts are compatible — the party's need, Tachibana's hand, the North's use of him — but no single one is complete.)
- Runa holds no controlling office; the Moonlight Fund has no legal existence; its staff are on other companies' payrolls.
- Keika buys Holland Town; Mark Barnes's motives are opaque even to Eva.
- Karin introduces training manuals, video analysis and all-employee quasi-stock options at Keika Electronics Union.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** "Stop babbling and go to school" as Jeremy Clarkson's reply to Greta Thunberg on Twitter; theme-park finance — Hankyu Railway and Takarazuka, and Tokyo Disneyland recouping its investment by selling residential land; **the charismatic president = Nakayama Sohei of the Industrial Bank of Japan**, the "Kurama tengu of the financial world," whose "two wounds" were **Huis Ten Bosch and Sogo**; Japanese firms of the period turning to temps who could perform at once; American temporary layoffs and rehiring; smoking rooms as secondary meeting rooms.
- **Historically real, used accurately:** Oda Nobunaga's death at Honnō-ji through Akechi Mitsuhide's betrayal (1582); Caesar and Brutus (44 BC); the railway–leisure–housing model of Kobayashi Ichizō's Hankyu; Sasebo's population of roughly a quarter of a million; Ureshino, Takeo and Shimabara as hot-spring areas; the long-standing argument that Japanese workplaces transmit skill by imitation rather than written manuals.
- **My identifications:** Holland Town = **Huis Ten Bosch** (opened 1992 at Sasebo, Nagasaki; Dutch-style canals and steeples; an environmental "eco-city" concept; a villa district); Commercial Bank of Japan = the **Industrial Bank of Japan** (now part of Mizuho — hence Honami's exposure); "rice profits while the paddies were still green" = the Japanese idiom *aota-uri* (selling before the harvest). Arts Nova is fictional (the name suggests Novartis — *uncertain*).
- **Altered:** Huis Ten Bosch in fact filed for court-supervised reorganisation in February 2003 and was taken over by Nomura Principal Finance; here Keika buys it. (See sources at the end of the volume.)
- **Interpretation:** the chapter title adapts Marx's opening of *The Eighteenth Brumaire* ("first as tragedy, then as farce"). The tragedy is the father driven to suicide by politics; the comedy is the daughter's coup, run by relatives and favour-seekers who think they are saving her.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **Kanbe states the series' thesis on adulthood.** Adulthood is responsibility for everything, and a person who has it without being chosen is a dictator; the right of a child is to choose the people at her side. Runa refuses his "magic words" because accepting the world as it is means accepting her own ruin — the series' core conflict in one gesture.
- *Strongly supported:* **Three models of adulthood are set side by side.** Nakamaro (the capable heir who knows his limits), Kanbe (the adult who scolds and consoles), Karin ("people like Angela and me are providing one of the routes you can take in life"). Karin's is the one Runa accepts with thanks: adulthood as leaving a path for successors.
- *Strongly supported:* **Holland Town is a mirror.** Nakayama Sohei's charisma made him untouchable and the bank could not say no; Runa sees herself in that position. Paying ¥200 billion for the lesson connects directly to Kanbe's "It's your position that's wrong."
- *Plausible:* **Mark Barnes's "your own country" is a test, and she passes it with Fuchigami's voice.** It recalls Fuchigami's question in Ch. 2 — "where do you intend to become queen?" — and the Vol. 5 nationalists who wanted her as a symbol.
- *Plausible:* **The second account of Otsumaro's death** shows why Kiyomaro mistrusts the government: the house's experience is that the ruling party will sacrifice a Keikain to the United States. It casts the Privy Council threat as a father's memory, not caprice — and links back to the CoCom affair (Vol. 1) and the US alliance that Koizumi now embodies.
- *Speculative:* **The real Katō Kōichi** was Chief Cabinet Secretary from December 1991 to December 1992. If the in-world chronology places Otsumaro's death then (Runa was born about 1990), the detail is precise; the text does not date it here.

**Connections:** Vol. 1 Ch. 2 (the CoCom scandal and her father's death), Ch. 4 (Katou's complicity in the cover-up); Vol. 2 Ch. 5 (Nakamaro's rebuke; Kiyomaro's adoption) and Ch. 7 (Tachibana's role in the suicide); Vol. 3 Ch. 12 (Kiyomaro declines to use the adoption); Vol. 4 Ch. 6 (Karin's recruitment) and the Portercon purchase; Vol. 5 Ch. 4 (Sakurako's pregnancy; "we'll end up clashing someday"), Ch. 9 (Runa's reading of her father as the North's instrument); this volume Ch. 2 (Fuchigami's "queen" question; the coup timetable; Honami's ¥1 trillion).

## Chapter 4 — The Young Lady's Friends and Their Decisions

**Narrative position:** Seven short scenes, almost all without Runa in the narrator's chair: Yuujirou (third person), Mitsuya (third person), Eiichi (third person), **Machiyoi Sanae** (first person — the volume's first non-Runa first-person narrator), Takahashi Akiko, Katsuki Shiori and Ichijou Erika (third person). Spring–summer 2003, in the shadow of the coup and a likely snap election. Each friend receives a message from the adults around Runa — "keep a little distance," "she should lie low" — and decides what to do with it.

**What happens**

*Yuujirou and Asuka*
- **Kasugano Asuka** "just happens" to meet Yuujirou after club ("a lot of work had been done behind the scenes"). Her question, "Would you like to become a teacher?" uses the honorific *sensei*, as for a Diet member: Yuujirou replies that "the Ehime electoral district would go from my surname to 'Kasugano'" — i.e. he would marry into her family and inherit its seat.
- Her calculation: her father will get no higher than cabinet minister; urban growth and rural depopulation will hand the district to swing voters and younger opposition candidates; there will be a dissolution this year; the two of them can stand at twenty-five, "in the latter half of 2010," about five elections from now. "Only ten years away." A woman running in a rural seat "is a real demerit," and she does not want to after watching her father: her choices are "to become a politician or to become a politician's wife."
- The narration explains the post-1994 system: single-seat districts plus proportional representation shift power to the party centre, which now controls endorsements.
- Yuujirou's family: his eldest brother's transfer to Hokkaido (Councilor Izumikawa) has set off an ugly war over who inherits the support base; Deputy PM Izumikawa refuses to interfere — "If it falls apart here, then that's as far as it goes."
- Asuka: "I'm your escape route, Izumikawa-kun. Please don't ever forget that." She has read his love for Runa and his turmoil over Eiichi's.
- Her gossip: "one of your brothers-in-law is supposedly in touch with someone from the Keika Group," seeking the support that moved the councilor to Hokkaido. Runa, told later, is "greatly" surprised — the contact is not hers.

*Mitsuya and his father*
- His father (**Gotou Mitsutoshi**, the cast list's name), "the star of the accounting field in the renamed Ministry of Finance," meets him at the station in the rain and takes him to a family restaurant for sirloin steak.
- Background: the reorganised ministry is desperate to regain power after the revised Bank of Japan Act and the creation of the Financial Services Agency stripped it of financial authority ("hadn't even been able to keep the characters in its name identical"); Koizumi is drawing ministry officials away from Izumikawa; Mitsuya's friendship with Runa marks his father as **Izumikawa faction**. "Bureaucrats rose to power… based on a lack of demerits, not an excess of merits."
- "My higher-ups told me I need to think about the good of the ministry." He had wanted to be administrative vice-minister like his own father; "my outstanding son is becoming my weakness. I was angry at first, but then I was at a loss." He chooses his son: "I want you to decide what to do with your life."
- "Did you know you've already made a lot more money than I have?" He can still reach assistant vice-minister; "that young lady friend of yours is willing to get a seat ready for me somewhere"; METI sees TIG "as a model for student start-ups."
- Mitsuya quotes "an anime Keikain Runa watched": "**It doesn't matter if some people dance in the rain without umbrellas. That's what it means to be free.**" They walk home without umbrellas; the drizzle becomes a downpour; Mitsuya catches a cold.
- Back at school: "Keikain, about that offer to give my dad a job at one of your companies… I don't want you to do that." — "Hang on! That's the first I'm hearing of this!" Someone has been making offers in her name.

*Eiichi and his father*
- **Teia Shuuichi** speaks to him "as boss and subordinate" (a manner Karin taught the boy). There is trouble in the Keikain family: "adults being busybodies, giving advice like 'Stop babbling and go to school.'"
- "The prime minister reportedly scolded a certain young lady for trying to cross dangerous bridges… **Iraq. It sounds like the young lady drafted plans for all the logistics involved in the war.**" Eiichi is "lost for words."
- "I don't care whether she plays computer games or financial games, but war games would make her a death merchant, and that's not a title any child should hold." He laughs at himself: in a developing-country war called the "**Teia War**," soldiers mounted heavy weapons on Teia pickups.
- Asked what he would do if told to "go to school," he says: "I'd have ignored them." His own story: the young master who became "nothing more than the Teia Group's leader." "Treasure the position you have right now… If it's what you want, I wouldn't mind seeing you go as far as to throw away the Teia name."
- The Teia Group once sat on the edge of the **Futaki zaibatsu**, which cut it loose amid the outcry to dismantle Futaki over bad debts; the family owns only a small share and the group was nearly taken over. "It's your fortress, so as long as you can take responsibility for it, I want you to do as you please." "Teaching children bad habits, then scolding them, is an adult's job."
- Leaving, Eiichi hears his father say, loudly enough to be overheard, "Ah, I need to make that call to MITI…" — the ministry's old name. The meddling came from METI, which has "joined hands" with the expanded Keika Group. Eiichi: "**This is about oil…?**"

*Machiyoi Sanae (first person)*
- A choir member who "had fallen for Keikain Runa's singing voice" since the elementary-school courtyard opera. Runa helps the choir for the autumn competition.
- **Asagiri Kaoru** (now Runa's relative) reports that the Berlin, Moscow and Vienna Philharmonics all performed in Japan this summer because they want her; the Keika Group patronises them as well as the Teia International Philharmonic; reruns of a drama she starred in have lifted a station's ratings.
- Runa: "Still, I should probably study music abroad if I can…" She sings "**Daichi Sanshou**" — beautifully, "but her face always looked sad."
- Sanae: "Our noble families will take control of us someday… I just wonder how far they'll manage to go." She lets slip that Runa's family sends the Courtiers' main families "tokens of appreciation, saying they're payment for joining the Courtiers." Runa, with "hidden anger": "We're children, so we can't comment on the jobs adults do."
- Runa: "**Tell me, Sanae-san, do we have a duty to be happy?**" — "I think so. I mean, we don't want people who know us to see us unhappy, do we?" Runa: "It's so obvious, but I didn't even see it."

*Takahashi Akiko*
- At her father's dojo party (he is a prefectural police director), with **Inspectors Ono Kenichi** and **Natsume Kentarou**. Ono is climbing the ladder after all — now in charge of the Kudanshita police box — because "Tachibana-san bow[ed] his head." Ono and Takahashi graduated in the same year; Takahashi "foisted Maefuji on" Ono; Natsume calls Maefuji "senpai." Natsume lost a kendo bout to Akiko, who "even beat the young lady."
- The talk, "pretending to be drunk," is meant for Runa's ears: the Geofront attack proved Japan is a terrorist target; Runa is "friends with the American president"; "the Keikain family wants her to lie low"; "Tachibana-san's stepping down, and there's been a lot of chaos over picking his successor. ## Chapter 5 — The Young Lady's Daily Life and the Shark Movie

**Narrative position:** Runa's first person, with one stretch of third person (the Hollywood audition) and an in-world film review. Comic interlude in five pieces: the Teisei swimsuit shoot, the perfume episode, the four "sisters'" scents, a bicycle ride to Tokyo Bay, and the shark movie. The last piece is a **flash-forward**: she flies to America "later… to attend the Academy Awards," wins Best Supporting Actress, and shoots the shark film the next day; the reference to "a fantasy movie that was a big hit that year" (*The Lord of the Rings*) and the war "with Iraq going on" place it around early 2004, beyond this volume's main timeline.

**What happens**

*The swimsuit shoot*
- At the request of **Teisei Department Stores'** director of sales (sales "exploded" once she modelled for the children's department; Teisei is "in a sticky situation"), she poses at a Keika Hotel pool for **Ishikawa-sensei**, the photographer, in a white bikini, pareo and oversized straw hat — with **Yulia Molotova** (her guard, drafted in) and **Kanna Mizuki** (offered up by Yulia). Ishikawa: "Okay, peel off for this one." They refuse.
- Mizuki on her many boyfriends: "fortune tellers can't date" — a fortune teller who loves a man cannot read his fate objectively; fortune tellers "stand beside those who held power, taking a jester-like role… the ones who admonished the powerful"; the Kanna women stay single and the talented are chosen to succeed, "raised as if they're grafted plants." "It's wrong to be bound by one man. Being bound by many is better." The Kanna are also "famous as a clan of high-class prostitutes." Her master is **Kanna Sera**, Hikomaro's mistress, who warned him before the **second February 26 Incident** and saved his life.
- Runa notes this is "something that had never come to light in the original game," and that in the game Mizuki "was always alone."
- The poster is stolen off walls as soon as it goes up; Ishikawa's photo book sells out; the campaign is "a massive success."

*The perfume*
- Teisei makes her a signature scent for its fragrance exhibition (Keiko insists a lady must "pay attention to things other than just their appearances"). Eiichi: it "smells like the grape juice you're always drinking." Yuujirou: "some kind of herbal scent." Mitsuya: "It smells like soap." She stress-eats garlic steak and spaghetti.
- Keiko then places the perfume bottle on the table, **seal intact**: she overslept after late-night gaming and never put it on. Two-hour lecture. Next day she wears a mask to hide the garlic; the boys think she has a cold; her associates stare "with very cold eyes."

*The four sisters' scents*
- In Kudanshita Tower, Runa's blend is revealed: **clary sage (top), attar (middle), musk (base)**. Mio, Asuka and Hotaru choose theirs and share one note each: Asuka — marine, mandarin, lilac; Hotaru — white sandalwood, mandarin, clary sage (her own scent would be "the aroma of incense"); Mio — clary sage, lilac, white sandalwood ("I wish all my big sisters and I could be connected by a scent…").
- Worn together, the four scents make "a dreadful smell" in the car; they wash them off. Mio: "If I ever get a boyfriend, I'm going to wear this perfume." The three elder sisters' replies are notably cold; Hotaru's grin "contained no such warmth."

*The bicycle to the sea*
- She rides the bicycle she won on a TV show from Kudanshita to **Takeshiba Pier** (7 km) with associates **Enbuchi Yuna** and **Glasya Marsheva** on road bikes, in racing gear with helmet and pads — "a lovely new trend." Traffic lights, parked cars and no-cycling pavements make it a two-hour trip. Passers-by recognise "the young lady who won an Academy Award" and the *Imperial Bodyguards* star.
- At sunset over the Rainbow Bridge she sees houseboats. **World-building:** houseboats reappeared in the bubble; they now house residents of former Northern Japan, and Tokyo wants them gone as "hotbeds for criminal activity." The government is converting tankers into floating "**ark cities**," partly "to screen high-risk individuals"; one is being built off **Kisarazu**; the **Shinjuku Geofront City** will house up to 100,000 people underground. Glasya: "People from Karafuto, like me, see a bicycle as their first asset in life." Former Northerners mostly do manual work on the coasts. The country has absorbed "twenty-four million former Northern Japanese citizens"; public safety "was improving, but it wasn't necessarily good."
- They go home by car: six cars had been tailing them, including a HiAce for the bikes. Eva: "Even this is on the conservative side. I think you'll understand that the next time you visit the United States."

*Samurai Ghost Shark vs. Swimsuit Noblewoman*
- Third person: a studio on the edge of Hollywood making late-night zombie and shark films; actresses auditioning for "shark bait." A Cadillac arrives with **Secret Service** guards; the girl who "had just won Best Supporting Actress at the previous night's Academy Awards" walks in: "Is this the audition for the shark-bait role?"
- Her secretary hands out a case of used ten-dollar bills to pay everyone's travel costs ("People would suspect they were counterfeits" if they were hundreds) — a lesson in the "American class system."
- The studio president had offered her the role only as cover for using a look-alike; she calls each excuse (no sponsors → "my company can find you sponsors"; no crew → she phones her director, "He won the Academy Award for Best Director this year"). Half a day of shooting, a week of editing.
- **The in-world review:** official budget $243 (swimsuit, food, petrol); director and star waived fees ("An appearance fee? I don't need spare change like that"); shot on the private California beach where she was staying; credits list only Director and Lead actress; the soundtrack is public-domain classical music and her own singing. Plot: ghosts of World War II soldiers fuse with a shark; a noblewoman on her private beach is hunted; the camera takes the shark's point of view; she cowers, investigates, and finally hunts it; her single line — "**That's it! I should put some clothes on!**" — is the scene that made audiences spit out their drinks; the climax is a swordfight in a sailor uniform. "Monsters are monsters because they don't speak. In that sense, the girl herself is another monster." "In the end, it's us fleeing from her."
- Aftermath: over 50 million American viewers; prime-time broadcast; Emmy and Golden Globe talk; **the president choked on a pretzel while watching and the nuclear button passed to "that hard-liner vice president for a few minutes"**; aides stopped him: "If you press it because of this movie, we'll get a Golden Raspberry Award."
- **Context given:** "As the situation in Iraq deteriorated, debate about the use of nuclear weapons was heating up rapidly in the United States. The world was learning from Afghanistan that they needed to inflict more fear than was inflicted, or else their people and land would be the next ones turned to scorched earth."
- The DVD came with an "image video" the director made her shoot; it was nearly classed as pornography (US) and sold in adult sections of used-DVD shops in Japan. Police (an inspector from the Kudanshita box) joked about arresting her and gave up because of her noble privilege.
- Imitations: *Last Shark Samurai*, *Shark Noblewoman*, *Noblewoman vs. Mushroom Man*… Buyers duped by them complain to consumer centres.
- Offers she refuses: the heroine of a spy series (love scene); a senator who preserves the republic in a space opera ("Viceroy was the real goal"); an elf in the prequel to a fantasy hit.
- The director: "If you constantly make art, your magnum opus will always be something in the future… the second they realize their magnum opus is in the past, they start going downhill."
- They appear on a talk show hosted by "the walking encyclopedia of the Japanese TV world."

**Revelations**
- Runa wins the Academy Award for Best Supporting Actress (the Vol. 5 flash-forward fulfilled), and is protected by the US Secret Service in America.
- The Kanna fortune tellers' code: celibacy, many lovers, a jester's licence to admonish the powerful; Kanna Sera saved Hikomaro in the second February 26 Incident.
- Former Northern Japanese are housed on boats and in planned "ark cities"; integration is a policing project.
- In this world, a US vice president briefly held nuclear authority during the Iraq war, and American debate over using nuclear weapons is live.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** bicycle prices and job options; *It Came From the Late, Late, Late Show* (credited to "Suzaku Games, 1997"); **George W. Bush choking on a pretzel** while watching football in January 2002; the Emmy TV-movie category requiring a prime-time airing; **Reagan's 1985 surgery**, when presidential powers passed to Vice President Bush for about eight hours; "image video" (non-nude erotica, a gateway for "junior idols"); the spy series = James Bond; the space opera = *Star Wars*; the fantasy = *The Lord of the Rings*; the TV programme = ***Tetsuko no Heya*** (Kuroyanagi Tetsuko).

**Deeper significance**

In a world that has never seen a real nuclear demonstration, the comedy is about how thin the restraint is.

It is the villainess's arc rendered as a B movie — and the shark is a fusion of dead World War II soldiers, the past that comes for her.
- *Plausible:* **The director's advice to keep one's magnum opus in the future** is advice against nostalgia — a counterweight to Kanbe's "Give up."
- *Plausible:* **The ark cities show the cost of unification** at street level, the same 24 million who made the Karafuto underworld and the Geofront plotters. The Kisarazu "ark city" is the tanker city of the Offshore Town Act (Vol. 3), now described from below.
- *Speculative:* **Mio's perfume and the three sisters' cold replies** set up a later romance plot for Mio.

**Connections:** Vol. 2 Ch. 3 and Ch. 8 (Teisei; "Little Queen"); Vol. 3 (Kanna Mizuki; the dollhouse sisters; the Offshore Town Act and the Kisarazu tanker city); Vol. 5 Ch. 3 (*Imperial Bodyguards*; the nomination; the director), Ch. 6 (American nuclear-authorisation debate), Ch. 8 (the second February 26 Incident); the Vol. 5 §3a "missing demonstration" context; this volume Ch. 6 (Narita) and Ch. 1 (Angela's CIA desk on Karafuto).

## Chapter 6 — Failed Terrorism Outside Narita Airport

**Narrative position:** Alternates Runa's first person (the train and the platform) with third-person cut-aways — the Kudanshita police box, the Cabinet Office's Crisis Management Center, the US embassy — and a closing television special. One long day, probably early summer 2003 (Diet dissolution is still a "scenario"). The volume's action set piece, built like a film: Runa notices twice that she has "filmed a movie scene just like this."

**What happens**

*Departure*
- Angela is flying home from Wall Street. Eva steers Runa away from the car (traffic) and the helicopter (wind) to **her personal train** — a three-car **253 series** waiting on Kudanshita's siding ("detention line") — at Angela's request, so Eva can be "interviewed" about whether Runa can be left in her care.
- The trip doubles as the household's "very first evacuation drill." The associates — **Kushunnai Nanami, Enbuchi Yuna, Irina Berosova, Glasya Marsheva, Yulia Molotova** — carry non-lethal sonic weapons; **Tachibana Yuka, Nozuki Misaki, Rudaka Miu, Akibe Riko, Ryuu Suzune** carry riot shields; maid uniforms are bulletproof. **Kitagumo Ryouko** carries a real gun; special forces under former general **Nakajima Atsushi** (head of Kudanshita Tower security) guard the route; **Inspector Natsume Kentarou** and Metropolitan Police Security Bureau officers come along.
- Suzune: "it might help your subordinates if you acted selfishly from time to time, like a normal young noblewoman would." Runa: "I'm being selfish! Yes! Tell them I'm coming through!" Ryouko: "…Taking advantage…" Runa: "It's kind of exciting, isn't it?… like going on a dangerous picnic."
- Tozai Line, then the Sobu Main Line; about an hour; a special VIP platform at Narita. The doors do not open: gunfire has been reported at the airport perimeter.

*What is actually going on (Maefuji to Ono; Anisha to the Crisis Management Center)*
- Natsume: the police raided a warehouse near the airport on a tip and met armed resistance; the guns are Russian-made, from the former Northern Japanese army's stocks, entering via Karafuto; "This country's involvement in the Iraq War could have something to do with it too." Runa asks whether human trafficking and drugs are part of it — "There's no reason the mafia wouldn't get involved."
- **Plot one (Islamic extremists):** American intelligence reported a plan to storm Kudanshita Tower and kill Runa — "the young lady who campaigned as if she was an idol for the American president" — "to force this country out of the Iraq War." The extremists, Russian citizens, entered illegally via Karafuto. Runa was to be evacuated to Hokkaido (New Chitose via Narita, then the Keika gated community at **Yubari**; **Sakata** was the reserve). Five were arrested in an urban **houseboat** hideout.
- **Plot two (Russian mafia):** the extremists bought weapons from the Russian mafia, which sold them out "at the eleventh hour" in a **plea bargain**; in revenge, the extremists leaked the mafia's own plot — to **assassinate Angela Sullivan** on landing at Narita, because she is "a North American surveillance worker with access to the Moonlight Fund" and had been investigating money laundering in Karafuto. Angela's jet diverted to Haneda; she fled to the US embassy.
- The warehouse is an armoury and drug store where the Russian mafia trades weapons for narcotics with the **Chinese mafia**. Drugs from the **Golden Triangle** flow out through Hokkaido and Karafuto and into Europe by Siberian railway — in short supply because Afghanistan, once a large exporter, lost its production "in the genocide." "Karafuto was handling the money laundering for all those business deals." The Russian mafia, built up since the Soviet fall with footholds in Britain and Japan, is "said to be behind up to a third of global money laundering"; its Kanto branch is made of ex-soldiers who fought the yakuza in Hokkaido and Karafuto.
- Maefuji to Ono: send the young lady to the main Keikain estate — "The prime minister and American president are worried about her safety too." Tachibana and Nakajima, listening on speaker, send units to guard the main residence; Tachibana promises Ono expensive liquor. Ono: "This is going to be a long day…"
- **Anisha Egorova** (ex-KGB maid with "unofficial connections to the Russian government," who helped Izumikawa plan the escape) briefs the Crisis Management Center: the affair was "nothing more than a commonplace internal miscommunication" — the deal was struck at the top, so the gunmen at the bottom were never told. Six extremists came by Siberian railway; five were caught. **One is unaccounted for.** "So who planted the bomb in the airport?"

*The platform*
- Every channel covers the siege. Commentators argue over police firepower ("Unlike in the Nagano incident…"), a special anti-terror unit, and the spectre of "the old imperial police" and the **second February 26 Incident**. Under the **revised Security Industry Act**, private security firms in Tokyo and Chiba are placed under police command.
- A newsflash: a radical Islamic group claims the attack; "Their target is Lady Keikain Runa, the noblewoman who incited the war"; she is "stopped at Narita Airport." Natsume: "Who the hell leaked this?!" Misaki traces it: European royal-watching paparazzi tracked her schedule; a **train-fan thread online** spotted the special train leaving Kudanshita; European media bought the story, and Japanese stations used that to break the media blackout. "That star's safety wasn't too important."
- Reporters and frightened travellers rush the VIP platform. A **coin locker explodes** inside the perimeter (no casualties); the perimeter collapses.
- Eiichi phones: he is at Narita to meet staff from TIG's East Coast office; he has booked her a hotel and has a bulletproof car.
- Runa decides: "Please allow any evacuees to enter. The rest of us will escape to a hotel in Narita… Tell the reporters we'll have a press conference in half an hour." Her reasoning: this will be treated as terrorism; the opposition will use it against Koizumi's Iraq policy; pushing back civilians in front of cameras would hand the coup "the ultimate weapon." "Inspector Natsume. Eva. I believe in all of you. So please allow me to do my job." Natsume agrees; evacuees are let onto the platform but not the train and searched.

*The Crisis Management Center*
- Deputy PM **Izumikawa** (minister for crisis management) chairs; **Koizumi** enters and sits to one side. Reports: the five houseboat terrorists in custody "under suicide watch"; police short of men to search lockers and bins; the **First Airborne Brigade (Narashino)** on standby.
- Koizumi: "Sounds like you need more people, eh? I'll give the orders, then." — **the JSDF is ordered out on a public-security operation**, which requires Diet approval. "However things started, multiple countries see this as terrorism. Ours can't fall behind in the war on terror. I'll take responsibility for any fallout." Izumikawa drafts the compromises.

*The grenade*
- Eva: the JSDF will free the police to secure the terminals; tanks — **Type 90s** from the Tsuchiura base — are coming "as a show of power"; the mafia "won't give their lives."
- Eiichi's bulletproof car is let up to the platform entrance. Runa walks out into flashes, envy ("why does some kid get all those guards to herself?!") and questions about her US ties.
- At the top of the stairs Eiichi is wrestling **a girl about their age** to the ground — the sixth terrorist — who is trying to pull a grenade pin. Runa shouts "Grenade!", slips past Akibe Riko, leaps off reporters' backs and pins the girl's wrist; the pin stays in. The girl tries to bite her tongue.
- "Why do you have to do things right out of movies, Runa?" — "Why did you imitate me when you haven't even starred in a movie, Eiichi-kun?" — "I just wanted to try it. I looked cool, didn't I?" *Ba-dump.* "Dummy!" They are evacuated to a Narita hotel and scolded thoroughly.

*The US embassy*
- Angela, swearing, faces **Mark M. Barnes**: "You've got some nerve endangering my lady like that." Mark: "I see you've become nothing more than one of her servants." Angela: "Don't forget, you got saved by a plea deal."
- **Mark is the witness of Ch. 1:** he lost in "the speculative battle" around the tens of billions Runa "wiped out on Wall Street," then changed his name, identity and face (plastic surgery) and sought the agency's protection. He was Karafuto Bank's Wall Street trader and ran the system he now exposes.
- **"Anastasia":** a secret account at a private Swiss bank, opened by Karafuto Bank on the orders of the Northern Japanese government, nominally to hold **the Romanov family treasure**, to be released "when a legitimate heir to the Romanov family emerged" — in reality a laundering system and the Russian mafia's "piggy bank." Karafuto Bank lent against it to Russian oligarchs with mafia ties; the oligarchs, fearing a purge by the Russian president, took up "the cause of reviving the Romanovs"; survivors of the Northern government, fearing Karafuto independence, joined; the Karafuto Reconstruction Agency and prefectural officials looked away. "A system that left everyone happy, at least in the beginning."
- The **Russian financial crisis** and the fall of the oligarchs broke it — "That's how the true treasure—the Moonlight Fund—started getting attention." Laundered on Wall Street: European arms profits, human trafficking, South American drugs. Karafuto Bank, nationalised at reunification, could cover deficits with tax money and call it bad-debt disposal. **"Of the tens of billions of dollars the young lady erased, about fifteen billion were debts related to Karafuto Bank."**
- Angela's summary: oligarch losses in 1998 → bad debts quietly absorbed by the Reconstruction Agency and prefecture → the American IT crash → losses that cannot be hidden under Koizumi's bad-debt drive. "But what if the Romanov family treasure actually existed?… that treasure's owner would be the young lady with the Romanov family's blood in her veins."
- Mark: only a few groups could send a killer after a CIA officer, and the Russian president is on bad terms with the mafia. "You know the young lady makes her moves with dollars and yen. So why don't you consider that other currency?" Iraq had been trying to sell oil for **euros**. "**These are European investment funds… the young lady's games and the war in Iraq are entirely to decide what the hard currency of the twenty-first century will be. The witches in Europe have been writing this entire script.**"

*The television special*

## §3. Consolidation accounting — why the Moonlight Fund had to take a shape

*An economics note.* Japan's "accounting big bang" of 1999–2003 moved listed companies to **consolidated** statements as the primary accounts and to **market (current) value** for securities. The novel compresses this into one spring and makes it a plot engine.

- **What consolidation does.** It adds up a parent and everything it controls, eliminating the inter-company items. The glossary's gloss is exact: it "made it harder to separate accounts to hide losses — or hide profits." Before it, a group could park losses (or gains) in unconsolidated affiliates.
- **Why it hurts her.** The Moonlight Fund was a **deliberately unstructured** pool: no legal entity, staff on Keika Holdings' and Keika Corp's payrolls, withdrawals made by her guardians (Ch. 3). That design protected her — a scandal in the Fund could not be pinned on a company she did not legally run — and it kept control personal. Consolidation makes an off-book pool untenable for a company about to list (Ch. 1: listing "means releasing control"). So the Fund must become something: first the plan to put it under Keika Holdings' head office (Tenmabashi, Ch. 8), then **Keika Resource Development**, merged with Nichika under Keika Corp (Ch. 9).
- **Why it hurts others.** The same rules expose the real estate held by Gowa Osan's borrowers (Fuchiku, Tsukihi, Imperial Golf — "victims of the current value accounting introduced this spring," Ch. 8) and force banks and insurers to sell shares to cover paper losses, deepening the Narita sell-off. This is the real mechanism of 2001–03 Japan: market-value rules turned a slow decline into forced selling.
- **Who benefits.** Transparency helps whoever has nothing to hide and cash in hand. Kanegana (Kanebo) "fudged" to carry its other divisions; Keika is "doing exactly what Kanegana does," except it does not need to fudge (Ch. 1). Honest consolidation therefore reveals Keika as a holding company carried by one profitable division — the Moonlight Fund and Akamatsu's resource trading — which is precisely why METI and the plotters aim at that division.

The volume's quiet economic thesis: **in a world of consolidated, market-value accounts, informal power has to become formal or be taken away.**

## §4. The last act of the bad-debt era — banks, call money and the Keika Rules

*An economics note.* Chapters 2 and 8 replay, with names changed and dates moved, Japan's 2003 banking endgame (Takenaka's October 2002 Financial Revival Program, Resona's rescue in May 2003, Mizuho's ¥1 trillion capital drive, UFJ's 2004 crisis and merger).

1. **The interbank market as the aorta.** A bank fails when it cannot roll its overnight funding, not when it runs out of assets. Gowa Osan and Honami are locked out of the **call market** because lenders fear default; the "Japan premium" (glossary: at most about 1%) was the real 1990s version. "Companies fail on Mondays" is the same logic for firms.
2. **Runa's first instinct: lender of last resort.** A ¥1 trillion **committed credit line** (a facility legalised for Japanese banks by the 1999 Specified Commitment Line Contract Act) announces that Keika stands behind Gowa Osan. Angela's veto is political and reputational: a private group acting as the central bank wrecks its own listing, embarrasses the finance minister and looks like a vulture snatching a bank from Iwazaki and Futaki.
3. **Ichijou's alternative: Bagehot without collateral.** Walter Bagehot's rule for crises is to lend freely, at a penalty rate, against good collateral. Ichijou offers to take **any** call money Gowa Osan sells, at **+1.5%** — a penalty rate, in the Japanese register of "unofficially, we refuse," yet an open door. What is missing is the collateral: these are **unsecured** calls. That is why the next step matters.
4. **The risk is sold on.** Runa offers the ¥200 billion of unsecured calls to Imperial Iwazaki — "Throw in a shrimp, and fish out a sea bream" — then shows Iwazaki the documents that make the rescue *his* interest: his bank guaranteed Nichika's loans through Karafuto Bank. Imperial Iwazaki rescues Gowa Osan under the **Keika Rules**, which let a rescuer send bad debts to the **Resolution and Collection Corporation**. Keika ends with no exposure; Iwazaki gets a bank and a way to dump his Karafuto risk; the RCC transfer forces the losses into the open.
5. **Precedent as policy.** "Japan was a 'company' that relied on precedent." The Keika Rules were invented in Vol. 1 as a private–public workout protocol for Keika's own rescues. By Vol. 6 they are a public good: anyone with money can use them, and the FSA cannot refuse one zaibatsu what it allowed another. This is how informal private rules become institutions — and how her old invention becomes the tool that opens "Pandora's box" without her hand on the lid.
6. **Conformity and stigma.** Runa's sarcastic proposal that Keika also request ¥500 billion of public capital and repay it the same day describes a real equilibrium. In 1998–99 the big banks applied for public funds together so that no single bank's application would signal weakness — a **pooling equilibrium** in the economist's sense: when taking help is a signal, the strong take it too, and the signal disappears.
7. **Honami goes to Teia.** In reality Mizuho raised about ¥1 trillion in early 2003 from "close business partners and overseas investors" (Japan Times, 22 January 2003). The novel concentrates that into one buyer, Teia (Toyota), giving the carmaker a main bank and an insurance arm — the real logic of captive auto finance — and giving Eiichi his "trillion yen."

## §5. The yen trade — reflexivity, the real intervention, and the novel's devices

*An economics note.* Chapters 8 and 9 describe a two-phase operation.

**Phase one (bait).** From about ¥120 to the dollar, Keika buys roughly $20 billion of yen, averaging ¥113, and lets the market know it is Keika. Speculators read the move as a political signal — Keika avenging Narita on Koizumi — and pile in. Keika sells at ¥110 on the snap-election news for about $500 million. Arithmetic: (113 − 110)/110 ≈ 2.7% of $20 billion ≈ $545 million before costs — consistent with the text.

**Phase two (trap).** The funds, now long yen and short dollars, expect a Keika–government war and an Izumikawa defection. Koizumi's televised bow and handshake remove the war; the election removes the defection. The yen falls 2, then 3, then 5 yen over three days; Runa buys "one trillion" of dollar assets in the form of US war-bond "options," spreads the rumour that Japan is buying a trillion dollars of Treasuries, and the Bank of Japan enters on 1 August. The funds must buy back dollars at a loss, paying carry while they wait.

**Why it works — reflexivity.** George Soros (named in Ch. 2's glossary) called it reflexivity: market beliefs change the fundamentals they are about. Runa's reputation is the fundamental. By signalling a war she knows will not happen, she makes the market price it, then lets reality (Koizumi, the election) correct the price. Her edge is not foreknowledge here but **control of a signal** — and Koizumi's theatre (Ch. 8) is part of her trade whether he knows it or not.

**What is real.** Japan's monetary authorities really did intervene on a record scale: **about ¥35 trillion from January 2003 to March 2004** — some ¥15 trillion in January–March 2004 alone — and the proceeds went largely into US Treasuries, financing American deficits during the Iraq war (Ito, 2004). The real rate was held at about ¥116–120 through mid-2003, broke toward ¥110 after the September 2003 G7 meeting, and was defended near ¥105 at year-end. The novel moves the yen's surge into the summer, makes the speculators' defeat crisper, and gives a private actor the role the Ministry of Finance played.

**The novel's devices (flagged, not corrected).**
- *"Bond options" that skip congressional approval.* Individual Treasury auctions do not need congressional deliberation (only the overall debt limit does), and there is a real when-issued market for forward trading; the novel's instrument is a narrative convenience.
- *A 5% "commission" on $1 trillion ($50 billion).* Real bond-dealing spreads are measured in basis points; this is a story-sized fee.
- *Who decides intervention.* In Japan the Ministry of Finance decides and the Bank of Japan executes; the novel's "Bank of Japan's cannons" follows popular usage, and its Ch. 8 reference to the MOF international bureau is the accurate one.
- *"Rental fees" on the funds' shorts.* Correct in direction: in 2003 US rates (1%) exceeded Japanese (≈0%), so a long-yen, short-dollar position carried a negative carry; the cost was modest, not crushing.

**Who bears the risk.** Angela sells the dollar "options" on to **Japanese financial institutions, excluding Keika's**. Keika takes a fee; the currency risk lands on the domestic banks and insurers that Chapter 8 showed were already selling shares to cover losses — and on the Bank of Japan's balance sheet. As in the real intervention, Japan's savers end up holding America's war debt.

**And the seed of 2008.** "Angela also told me that she'd decided to suggest the vultures look into subprime loans while they were still trying to stop the bleeding. All the preparations for the next collapse were in order." Defeated funds needing to make back losses will **reach for yield**; subprime securitisation offered it. In Vol. 5 Ch. 4 Runa said, "I won't be the one who pulls that trigger." Here someone on her side points the funds at the trigger and she records it without objection. The volume ends with her praying about 2008.

## §6. The laundering machine — Anastasia, Karafuto Bank and Wall Street

The standard model of money laundering has three stages: **placement** (dirty cash enters the system), **layering** (transactions obscure its origin), **integration** (it re-emerges as legitimate wealth). Chapters 6 and 7 give each stage a body.

| Stage | In the novel | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Placement | Narita warehouse trade (weapons for drugs with the Chinese mafia); Karafuto as the gateway for Russian, Chinese and Golden Triangle flows | Physical crime at the border |
| Layering | **Anastasia** — a Swiss private-bank account opened by Karafuto Bank on Northern Japan's orders, labelled "the Romanov family treasure"; Wall Street securitisation (shell companies → securities → mixed into pools) | The account's story is the cover; securitisation makes the money untraceable |
| Integration | Karafuto Bank's loans to oligarch businesses "as collateral for that piggy bank's funds"; Russian sovereign bonds whose coupons were partly paid with laundered money; Karafuto reconstruction spending | Classic **back-to-back loans**: a deposit secures a loan that comes out clean |

Four further points.
- **A state bank as the washing machine.** Karafuto Bank was nationalised at reunification and could "use tax money to cover deficits whenever they wanted and say they were disposing of bad debts." Public bad-debt disposal — the very policy the series has followed since Vol. 1 — becomes a way to launder losses through the budget.
- **Runa's Wall Street operation broke it.** About $15 billion of the losses she inflicted in Vol. 4 were Karafuto Bank's. The coup is, in part, a creditor's revenge.
- **The US seeded it.** "One source of Karafuto Bank's funds was money the United States had budgeted for undercover operations meant to sabotage the Eastern Bloc" (Ch. 7). The Cold War's covert finance outlived its purpose and became criminal finance — a pattern with real precedents (BCCI, the Afghan arms pipeline).
- **The name is the plot — and it was seeded in Volume 1.** After the 1997 kidnapping (whose ransom, Ch. 2 reveals, came from Karafuto Bank), Yuujirou reassured her that the kidnappers would not bother the boys "if they were after Romanov treasure" (Vol. 1 Ch. 4). A fund that may be released only to "a legitimate heir to the Romanov family" needs an heir. Runa, three-quarters Russian and a Romanov descendant (Vol. 1), is the one person who could turn a laundering fiction into legal title. She is told none of this.

## §7. Oil — the national-champion offer and why it was a trap

- **The real background.** Japan's state oil developer, the **Japan National Oil Corporation** (JNOC, est. 1967), accumulated enormous bad assets through equity stakes and loan guarantees to exploration firms (about ¥1.4 trillion of non-performing assets by 1999, *Oil & Gas Journal*). Koizumi's November 2001 reform plan ordered JNOC **abolished**, its risk money capped and its development assets evaluated and sold; its functions passed to **JOGMEC** in 2004, and the "national flagship" idea led to the INPEX–Teikoku Oil merger of 2006. Japan's crude-import bill was about **¥4.6 trillion in 2002 and ¥5.3 trillion in 2003** (Japan Customs); Toudou's "roughly six trillion" is a round, high figure.
- **The offer.** METI would spin off Keika's resource division as the core of a national oil company — making her "the oil queen of a major Japanese oil company" — and then fill it with its own people, paying her dividends. In agency-theory terms it is the classic separation of ownership and control, designed so that the managers (the ministry's people) capture the firm and the owner receives "an allowance."
- **The hidden liability.** Nichika Oil Development, under the state corporation and banked by Karafuto Bank, hides about ¥2 trillion of losses. Accepting the offer would have made Keika the dumping ground — a reverse takeover of losses. "Nichika" reads 日樺 (Japan–Karafuto) — Volume 7 spells it "Nichikaba" — and is *plausibly* modelled on JNOC's Sakhalin vehicle, SODECO (*my identification*).
- **Toudou's arithmetic.** "It's only two trillion yen": controlling a third of imports means ¥2 trillion a year of flow; at rising prices, margins on that flow could repay ¥2 trillion within ten years. It is a real point about why oil producers tolerate exploration losses — and why METI thinks a girl with Russian and Gulf profits is the natural owner.
- **The outcome.** Nichika files; its losses go to Karafuto Bank's books and through the RCC; the resource division becomes **Gulf Oil Development** under Okazaki and then Keika Resource Development; METI's flagship is built, but on her terms, after the losses are public.

## §8. Politics as theatre — Koizumi, Izumikawa and the July election

- **"Politics was nothing more than a terrible play"** (Ch. 7). Three staged scenes structure the volume: the family farce at the main residence (Ch. 7), Koizumi's bow and double handshake in the party cafeteria (Ch. 8), and the press conference she calls on the Narita platform (Ch. 6). In each the performers know they are performing; the audience (relatives, press, voters, markets) is the target.
- **Koizumi wins every scene he enters.** He apologises to her before the cameras, takes Izumikawa's hand while holding hers, and so turns the opposition's best card — a Keika-financed defection — into a picture of party unity. Runa: "he'd still jumped in to steal the leading role… that was exactly why I feared getting into a battle with him." Her line "blessed by demons" echoes Vol. 4's "Prime Minister Koizumi has good luck."
- **The election is altered.** The real 2003 election was held on **9 November 2003**: the LDP fell to 237 seats and the DPJ rose to 177, its best result, on the first "manifesto" campaign. The novel moves it to late July and gives the coalition a landslide, crediting the opposition's collapse to the Karafuto money scandal. The opposition's manifesto still lifts its proportional vote — the real result's trend, kept.
- **Izumikawa's choice** — "I'm someone who's already reached the place he wants to be" — is the decisive political act of the volume. Staying as deputy prime minister, then foreign and crisis-management minister, he gives up the premiership the opposition offered.
- **Katou and Hosokawa.** Two real politicians' falls are folded into the world's history: Hosokawa's "national welfare tax" (proposed and withdrawn within a day in February 1994) becomes an enacted "Karafuto restoration tax" that toppled a noble prime minister (Ch. 2); Katō Kōichi's resignation over his secretary's tax evasion (April 2002) and return as an independent (2003) become Katou's apology tour in Sakata (Ch. 9). Through Nakamaro (Ch. 3), Katou is also the man her father died to protect.
- **The warning.** Tachibana's "It resembles the time period when the government was overthrown" and Runa's "A restoration. That word was like a curse" point the series at its next political danger: not elections but a movement of the 24 million discontented, of the kind that made the Second February 26 Incident.

## §10. What is Runa, in this volume?

Volume 6 multiplies the answers and makes them about **blood and money** rather than talent.

1. **A leader without title** — VP of a subsidiary, owner of a fund that does not legally exist (Ch. 3).
2. **A target of war** — "the noblewoman who incited the war" to Islamic extremists (Ch. 6); "a victim and a heroine of the war on terror" to Washington and Tokyo (Ch. 7).
3. **Collateral** — the Romanov heir whose existence would make the Anastasia "treasure" real (Ch. 6).
4. **A dynastic asset** — Europe's candidate for "empress consort" of Russia, "the secret weapon that'll bring Russia in" (Ch. 10).
5. **A laundering mechanism in waiting** — "Money dirtied by blood and greed is made clean through the blue bloods. Her talent, and her own blue blood, may mean she'll have to choose herself as that cleaning mechanism someday" (Ch. 10) — exactly the game's ending (Vol. 5 Ch. 9: the Karafuto tax haven sheltered by her immunity).
6. **A signal** — the market reads her moves as political (Ch. 9); her reputation is the trade.
7. **A girl** — perfume that smells of grape juice, a bike ride to the sea, a parfait with Eiichi, a heart that goes *ba-dump* (Chs. 3, 5, 6).

The last is the volume's answer to the others. Kanbe's advice — choose the people at your side — is set against Frankfurt's "History wants her to go back to Russia."

## §11. The era — nostalgia and trauma in 2003

The afterword places the volume at the bottom of the "lost decade": the Nikkei near its post-bubble low (7,607.88 on 28 April 2003), banks on public money, firms dying of market-value accounting. The novel loves and loathes it at once.

- **Trauma, accounted for.** Kanebo (Kanegana) carrying losses; Huis Ten Bosch (Holland Town) and Sogo as Nakayama Sohei's wounds; golf memberships as bubble-era financial products; Osaka's land debts and Kansai Airport; UFJ and Mizuho; JNOC's buried losses; Daiwa's hidden trading losses. Each real failure is named in a glossary and re-staged with Runa as the one who does not have to fudge.
- **Nostalgia, catalogued.** The 253 series and the 485 series; the Yamagata Shinkansen stalling at Fukushima during Obon with the lunch shops sold out; 2channel threads with their ASCII "(σ゜∀゜)σ Excellent!!"; late-night B movies and *Tetsuko no Heya*; Square and Enix merging; Sega dreaming of hardware; IBM's ThinkPad era ending; the LDP canteen's katsu curry before an election; *The Big O* quoted in the rain. The railway material in Ch. 9 is the author's own enthusiasm, and the novel lets Runa spend ¥1.2 trillion on it.
- **Respite as a value.** The volume's comedies (Chs. 5, 9) are not breaks from the theme. They are what the theme is protecting: "I couldn't help wanting to become an adult with Eiichi-kun and my friends" (Ch. 3).
- **"Buy stocks right now, and hold them forever."** The afterword's joke is the series' premise in one line: someone from the future who knows how 2003 ends. The author would have been rich, he says, "although that would also mean I might not be an author now" — the same trade-off Runa makes between using her knowledge and keeping her life.

## §12. The missing demonstration, in Volume 6

The series' world has never seen a city destroyed by a nuclear weapon (Vol. 5 Part II §3a): what it lacks is not nuclear use but a demonstration. This volume keeps the theme alive in three places.

- **A shark film nearly hands the button to a hard-liner.** The pretzel incident is real (January 2002); its in-world version transfers nuclear authority to "that hard-liner vice president for a few minutes," who "had even seriously considered pressing the button." Aides stop him with a joke about the Golden Raspberry Awards. Comedy is all that stands between the world and its first demonstration.
- **The narration states the doctrine plainly.** "As the situation in Iraq deteriorated, debate about the use of nuclear weapons was heating up rapidly in the United States. The world was learning from Afghanistan that they needed to inflict more fear than was inflicted, or else their people and land would be the next ones turned to scorched earth" (Ch. 5). Afghanistan's "genocide" (Ch. 6) remains this world's substitute demonstration.
- **Terror shifts from cities to people and money.** Narita's attackers target a girl and a CIA officer; the Europeans fight with currencies; Ch. 9 ends: "A new war fought with money, not with blood." The bomb is still undemonstrated, and the volume suggests why that matters: a world with no shared image of catastrophe keeps reaching for one.

## §14. Where the volume strains

- **Financial mechanics.** The "bond options" that bypass Congress and a 5% fee on $1 trillion are not real-market instruments (see §5). The narrative needs a private actor to stand in for the Ministry of Finance; the economics underneath (signalling, short squeezes, intervention) is sound.
- **Timeline compression.** The real 2003–05 sequence (Mizuho's raise, UFJ's scandal and merger, JNOC's abolition, IBM's PC sale, Galvin's exit, the November election) is fitted into one summer. Consistent with the series' method, but the reader should not use the dates.
- **Ambiguous sentences.** "It still controlled 49.9 percent of their shares" (Ch. 9 — Vol. 7 Ch. 10 confirms Keika Corp holds 49.9% and her Swiss account 50.1%) and "Can you believe it lost two whole yen…?" (Ch. 8) are unclear in English; the map gives the likelier readings.
- **Production slips.** Thread posts dated 27 and 29 July in the same thread; the glossary's "2006" for JNOC's successor (JOGMEC was formed in 2004); "my grandson" for a great-grandson; "diplomatic immunity" for noble immunity.
- **Tone.** The narration's sexualisation of its thirteen-year-old protagonist in Ch. 5 (the swimsuit shoot, the review's predatory point of view, the "image video") sits awkwardly with the rest of the volume's seriousness about children and adults.
- **Voices missing.** As in Volume 5, the Iraqi war is discussed only by people far from it; the Karafuto poor appear as houseboats, bicycles and a girl with a grenade.

# Part III — Synthesis

## Alternate-history ledger

**Preserved:** Japan Post's launch as a public corporation (April 2003); the Takenaka-era drive to end bad debts and the injection of public funds into weak banks; market-value and consolidated accounting forcing share sales; Mizuho's scramble for about ¥1 trillion of capital; UFJ's conflict with the regulator over hidden bad loans (moved earlier); Kanebo's cosmetics carrying its losses; Huis Ten Bosch and Sogo as the Industrial Bank of Japan's wounds; Square and Enix merging in April 2003 and DigiCube's collapse; Sega out of hardware; IBM preparing to leave PCs; Motorola's turmoil after Iridium; the Iraqi Governing Council and the CPA's de-Ba'athification; the Schwarzenegger recall; Koizumi's reshuffle and dissolution; the first "manifesto" campaign; Katō Kōichi's fall and return; record Japanese currency intervention with proceeds in US Treasuries; Moody's raising Russia toward investment grade; the post-9/11 aircraft glut; the Tohoku Shinkansen's Hachinohe extension and its "parallel line" problem; the Fukushima Station bottleneck.

**Altered:**
- **The 2003 general election** is held in **late July** (really 9 November) and gives the ruling coalition a landslide (really the LDP lost ground and the DPJ gained heavily) (Ch. 8).
- **UFJ (Gowa Osan) goes to Bank of Tokyo-Mitsubishi (Imperial Iwazaki)** in 2003 after the FSA catches it embellishing bad loans — the real scandal and merger came in 2004–05 (Ch. 8).
- **Mizuho (Honami) joins Toyota (Teia)** through a ¥1 trillion share allotment (Ch. 8).
- **JNOC's successor plan becomes a two-trillion-yen bankruptcy** (Nichika) and a Keika subsidiary (Chs. 8–9).
- **The yen surges to ¥108 in July 2003 and then collapses to ¥120** under a squeeze (Chs. 8–9); really the yen strengthened from September 2003 into early 2004 despite intervention.
- **Huis Ten Bosch** is bought by Keika "for the sake of patronizing culture" (Ch. 3); really it went into court reorganisation in February 2003 and was taken over by Nomura Principal Finance.
- **IBM's PC division, Lucent and Motorola's best parts** are bought by a Japanese group (Ch. 9); really IBM sold to Lenovo (December 2004).
- **The pretzel incident** happens during the Iraq war and briefly hands nuclear authority to the vice president (Ch. 5).
- **Turkish and Iranian armies occupy parts of Iraq** (Chs. 2, 7, 10).
- **Hosokawa's national welfare tax** was enacted as a Karafuto restoration tax and brought him down (Ch. 2).
- **The Fukushima rebuild and the Itaya tunnel** are funded in 2003 (really planned for 2027 and the 2030s).
- **A JSDF public-security operation** is ordered at Narita (never ordered in real postwar history) (Ch. 6).

**Wholly fictional:** Karafuto Bank, the Karafuto Reconstruction Agency and the Anastasia account; Gekkou Investment Firm; the Keika Rules; the Narita attack; the ark cities; the Choufuu Council foundation; Keika Resource Development; the European plan for a Romanov consort; *Samurai Ghost Shark vs. Swimsuit Noblewoman*; the four school factions; the game.

## Interpretive possibilities

**Strongly supported**
- The coup is the logical result of informal power meeting formal accounting; the volume's resolution is to formalise her power and share it (Part II §§2–3).
- Runa's strategy has changed from winning to "harassment" — arranging for others to carry the outcomes — and this is the volume's picture of her growing up.
- Her Romanov blood has become the central currency of the plots against her: the Anastasia account needs an heir; Europe needs a consort.
- The yen trade is a privatised version of Japan's real 2003–04 intervention, and like it, puts Japan's savings behind America's war.
- The volume carefully plants her complicity in 2008 (Angela's subprime suggestion) while she prays to survive it.
- Her friends, not her money, are the volume's answer to Kanbe — "you can choose the people at your side."

**Plausible**
- The European plan is the bridge between the present plot and the game's ending (a laundering haven fronted by a noble heiress).
- Tachibana took part in the fall of Northern Japan's government, which would make him both the man who served the North's enemy and the man who drove her father to his death.
- Mark Barnes is playing his own game, using Runa to bury Karafuto Bank's losses.
- The Choufuu Council foundation, chaired by Kiyomaro, is designed to be her shield and the main family's dignity at once — a structure that may later be turned against her.
- Eiichi's trillion yen makes the Teia zaibatsu a power and him a rival as well as a suitor.

**Speculative**
- Gekkou's money is the same Northern Japanese fortune that financed the game's Runa.
- The girl terrorist at Narita was one of the Toyohara-type orphans the North trained — Runa's associates' mirror image.
- The "Russian satellite company" will matter (a navigation or surveillance asset, after Vol. 5's GLONASS subsidy).
- The "restoration" movement will merge with the nobles who want to keep their privileges.

## On the "What is Runa?" question

Volume 6 answers less with names than with **uses** others plan for her.

- **Names she is given:** "the young lady"; "little missy"; "Your Little Majesty… where do you intend to become queen?" (Fuchigami, Ch. 2); "a superstar of history" (Ch. 2); "a puppet for the United States" is Angela's, not hers (Ch. 1); "the noblewoman who incited the war" (Ch. 6); "a victim and a heroine of the war on terror" (Ch. 7); "the oil queen" (Ch. 8); "the Marilyn Monroe of the Heisei era" (Ch. 5); "the secret weapon that'll bring Russia in" (Ch. 10); "empress consort" (Ch. 10); "the ornament" (Asuka, Ch. 7).
- **Names she gives herself:** "a junior high schooler now"; "a humble maiden" buying dollars; "a young maiden of marriageable age"; "an elegant young woman"; "a charismatic businesswoman" she does not want to become (Ch. 3); "a citizen of this country" who respects "the will of the people" (Ch. 8); Scipio at Carthage (Ch. 9).
- **What the plots say:** to Karafuto Bank she is the legal heir who would turn laundering into inheritance; to Europe she is a dynastic bridge to Russia; to METI she is the owner of a national oil company; to Washington she is a symbol whose retreat would encourage terrorists. Each wants her blood, money or image — not her judgement.
- **What the household says:** "the Happy Prince," who will give herself away (Ch. 4); a girl with "no time to rest."
- **What she says to herself:** "I won. I'm still winning… I'm going to lose someday… God, if you exist, I only ask that you help me through the battle of 2008."

The volume's quietest answer comes from a choir girl with no power: "Do we have a duty to be happy?" — "We don't want people who know us to see us unhappy." Runa: "It's so obvious, but I didn't even see it." Against the continent that says "History wants her to go back to Russia," Kanbe's line stands as the series' counter-thesis: "You're a child, so you can choose the people at your side—it's not for this world, or for history, to decide for you."
