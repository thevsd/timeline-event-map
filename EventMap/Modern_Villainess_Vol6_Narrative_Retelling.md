## Before we begin: A river that's hard to turn

The author's afterword to this volume contains the best description of the whole series that I've read anywhere, so let me start with it. "I've been writing my story with the mindset that history is like a large river and always heads in the same direction. However, dramatic historic events can still change its flow." For Keikain Runa, he says, those events are three: "the 9/11 terrorist attacks, the Iraq War, and the 2008 financial crisis." And the whole series "depicts Keikain Runa spending years laying the groundwork to prepare for the global financial crisis in order to change history. It's meant to show just what a difficult process changing the flow of history can be."

Hold on to that image of the river, because Volume 6 is about how hard it is to push.

It covers "spring and summer 2003," a few months, and in them almost everything that could go wrong for Runa nearly does. Her own executives plot to take her empire from her. A state bank in Karafuto, the poor northern territory that has haunted the last two volumes, turns out to be the money behind the plot, and behind the scheme that destroyed the girl in the game. Terrorists come for her at Narita Airport. Her blood, which until now has been backstory, becomes a prize: a laundering account wants a Romanov heir, and by the last page a cabal in Europe wants to marry her off to bring Russia into its camp.

And Runa, for the first time, doesn't try to win by charging straight at her enemies. Her own word for her strategy is "harassment." She arranges for other people to trip over what they've hidden, and lets them carry the consequences. It's a very different heroine from the one who tried to take on a war in Volume 4, and I think it's the clearest picture so far of what growing up might look like for her.

The volume moves in a regular rhythm, crisis and then respite: business, then philosophy, then friends, then comedy, then terror, then finance, then spending. The soft chapters aren't filler. They're where the book puts its answers to the question a professor asks her early on: whether she's thinking about her own happiness at all. And, as in Volume 5, a lot of it is told without her. The first and last chapters don't include her at all, and the frame keeps widening, from a room of exhausted accountants in Tokyo to a window in Frankfurt looking out at the European Central Bank.

The afterword was written in spring 2024, the author says, just as Japan's stock market finally passed its bubble-era peak. In 2003, when this book is set, it was near its lowest, around 8,000 yen, and people had just started talking about "the ten lost years." He was a junior employee then. If he had a time machine, he knows what he'd tell himself: "Buy stocks right now, and hold them forever."

That joke is the series' premise in a single line: someone from the future who knows how 2003 ends, wondering whether knowing would have been enough.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Karafuto**: Sakhalin. In this world a Soviet-backed communist state, Northern Japan, ruled the Japanese half until 1994; it is now a poor, reunited territory of Japan.
- **Romanovs**: Russia's imperial family until 1917. Runa is descended from them through her mother.
- **Laundering (money laundering)**: disguising illegally earned money so it looks legitimate.
- **Nikkei**: Japan's main stock-market index.
- **Bubble**: Japan's late-1980s boom in land and share prices, which burst around 1990–91.
- **The lost decade / ten lost years**: the long stagnation that followed the bubble.
- **European Central Bank**: the central bank of the euro area, in Frankfurt.

## Chapter 1: The room where the lights never go off

Volume 6 opens, as the last two volumes did, by showing us what Runa's decisions cost the people who carry them out. This time it's a room in her bank that literally never turns its lights off.

It's the financial analytics room, which has to add up the accounts of Keika Holdings and the whole group, and it can't, because the group keeps changing shape. That's "mostly thanks to the young lady's shopping sprees." The staff here are, frankly, the group's under-performers, moved in "as a restructuring strategy." It's "better than being forced to resign," the narration admits, but it's "the shadowy side of the rapidly expanding Keika Group." They stare at their monitors "like ghosts." Akamatsu, Teisen Ishii, Teimen, Kanegana Textiles: Runa keeps buying, and they keep failing to close the books.

The reason it's so hard has a name: consolidation accounting. Japan is switching to accounts that add up a parent company and everything it controls into one picture, and value its holdings at today's market prices. Imagine a family that has always kept separate piggy banks for each child, suddenly told to count every coin in the house as one total. You can't hide a deficit in one jar with a surplus in another any more. The glossary puts it plainly: it "made it harder to separate accounts to hide losses—or hide profits."

And what the accountants are finding is uncomfortable. One of Runa's purchases, Kanegana Textiles, has kept itself afloat by letting its profitable cosmetics business quietly prop up everything else, until the cosmetics side "ended up having to fudge their numbers." One tired worker sighs: "And the Keika Group is doing exactly what Kanegana does…" The difference is only that Keika doesn't need to fudge, because its money comes from one place. "They're the pillar of the Moonlight Fund, and the Moonlight Fund is what supports the rest of the group." The Moonlight Fund is Runa's private investment fund, the real engine of her wealth, and it's run out of a department of her trading company.

The office gossip in this room is a map of every problem in the book. The former Finance Ministry men on the bank's board will soon finish their terms, and "Replacing them will be a game of musical chairs." The gossip has it that the next chief executive won't come from Far Eastern, the old bank Runa started with. There are rumours of a merger with Iwazaki's bank, and puzzlement that her brother Nakamaro has been removed from the board. Her secretary Angela has been promoted to run the securities arm's North American division, and "people say she's a puppet for the United States." Nationalists, who once helped elect Governor Iwasawa, want to stop her: "Are you sure you want European and American hedge funds to play with the Moonlight Fund, Japan's treasure, as they please?" And once Runa's bank lists its shares on the stock market, the fund itself will have to come out into the open. "It was built without structure for a reason, so it'll definitely come under attack."

Then the door flies open and someone with bloodshot eyes throws down a pile of documents. "She did it again! The young lady just bought Zugagaga Entertainment!" Heads hit desks, papers fly, screams echo. The young lady, the narration adds, will never know.

Down the corridor, her banker Ichijou walks with his young deputy Katsura, trailed by a retinue the staff mock as a "daimyo's procession," and asks, almost to himself, "Isn't it sad?… That being at the height of your glory doesn't matter when the next problem arises." In his office he gives Katsura what looks like a demotion: a move to the group's trust bank, "in charge of the young lady's assets." Katsura takes it calmly. Ichijou, half joking, tells him he's the second person in the world he'd most like to punch. The first? "Isn't it obvious? That would be Okazaki Yuuichi." Then the real reason: "this company's game of musical chairs is about to get a lot more intense." Katsura guesses who'll be the next chief executive. Angela. Ichijou doesn't deny it.

In New York, Angela has dinner with her daughter Jennifer, whom she has raised alone, says goodnight, and changes faces. "This face belonged to a Central Intelligence Agency agent." On a secure line to the CIA, she reports that "the coup within the Keika Group won't ignite," thanks to Tachibana, though outside forces are interfering. Langley's priority is stability in Asia now that Iraq is "wrapping up." Koizumi went "all in with us on Iraq," and the president doesn't want "our mutual friend the prime minister" fighting with the young lady. Angela promises Runa won't oppose him: "It seems she truly won't oppose him this time." The silence on the line tells you how much Washington believes that.

The CIA has news too. The police now think Middle Eastern extremists were behind the failed Shinjuku Geofront attack, and that they gave Japanese cells a job: "to eliminate the young lady." If anything happens next, it will be based in Karafuto. And Angela gets two assignments. One is to take in a protected witness, a man who laundered money for the Russian mafia on Wall Street until "a certain young lady went a bit over-the-top and broke the washing machine very publicly." The other is to watch a man inside Runa's own group, Tenmabashi Mitsuru of her trading company, "one of the Japanese businessmen who made quite a fuss in places like London and Jerusalem," with "lots of friends in Washington." Even Okazaki is careful of him. Angela looks out at the skyline. "The Twin Towers… They used to be right there…"

In a Manhattan hotel, Karin Viola, now head of Keika Electronics Union, recruits her old friend Chloe Meyers, who left their former company with her and has been working on congressional staff ever since. What did Washington teach her? "That our proxy war was nothing more than child's play." Karin needs someone "who will never betray me," because the Japanese board members could unseat her at any moment. Chloe's price, a full team and offices on both coasts and in Tokyo, is granted instantly, which tells Chloe exactly how exposed Karin feels. Then Chloe asks who really matters in the group. Karin lists Tachibana, Ichijou and Toudou, and then one more name, the man who recruited her and Angela's "mortal enemy": "Okazaki Yuuichi, the manager of the Moonlight Fund. I believe he's a key player in the Keika Group."

And in Hong Kong, that key player is visiting a triad office that has grown rich betting alongside him, where a gangster who rose on those bets calls him "Brother," and where some of the ¥100 billion reward Runa gave him is hidden. He wants information on "Gekkou Investment Firm," a copycat ("gekkou" means "moonlight") protected by senior Chinese Communist Party officials and backed by a Shanghai organisation. Then the door opens and in walks Ryuu Suzune, Runa's schoolmate and "associate," in a qipao. She's the triad boss's granddaughter, and she's been sent to marry him. "It's not right for two men to be whispering in front of a girl of marriageable age," she says, in perfect Japanese, which would be clever if she weren't a junior high schooler.

The chapter ends in the street, under a giant poster of Runa on a department store. Okazaki thinks: *My lady, you're really going to shake this world up, aren't you?… Tell me, my lady, what will you show me next? What will you show the whole world next?* Then he shapes his fingers into a gun, points them at the poster, and says, "Bang!"

I love that ending, and it chills me. It's a gambler's salute to the person who gives him the biggest table in the world. It's also a man surrounded by spies, smugglers and copycats, pointing a gun at the girl he serves. The book doesn't tell us which it is. And every scene in this chapter is the same question asked a different way: who holds Runa's empire after the founders? Ichijou is preparing his exit, Karin is buying loyalty, Angela is the heir and also the CIA's officer, the nationalists want to stop an American running "Japan's treasure," and Okazaki is being married into a triad. The group has become so large that only consolidated accounts can show what it is, and what they show is a holding company carried by one profitable division, with a schoolgirl at the top who has no title at all.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Consolidation accounting**: combining a parent company and everything it controls into one set of accounts.
- **Current value (mark-to-market) accounting**: valuing assets at today's market price.
- **Restructuring**: in Japan, usually a polite word for cutting staff.
- **Moonlight Fund**: Runa's private investment fund, the real source of her wealth.
- **Musical chairs**: here, the scramble for executive posts when senior people retire.
- **Listing / going public**: selling a company's shares on the stock market.
- **Hedge fund**: a lightly regulated investment fund that makes aggressive bets.
- **Daimyo's procession**: the retinue that followed a feudal lord; here, a mocking name for executives' entourages.
- **Trust bank**: a bank that manages assets on others' behalf.
- **CIA**: the Central Intelligence Agency, America's foreign intelligence service.
- **Witness protection**: the American programme that hides and protects key witnesses.
- **Proxy war**: here, a battle for control of a company fought by collecting shareholders' votes.
- **Triad**: a Chinese organised-crime society.
- **Qipao**: a close-fitting Chinese dress.
- **Associates**: the girls placed around Runa at school as companions and guards.

## Chapter 2, part one: The last days of the bad-debt era

"ON APRIL 1, 2003, the semigovernmental Imperial Japan Post was born." Koizumi's long campaign to privatise the post office has taken its first step, and the markets read it as a signal: the government is about to finish Japan's bad-debt crisis by force, and weak banks will be merged or nationalised.

For a decade, the series has been about that crisis. A bad debt, remember, is what's left when someone borrowed to buy something at bubble prices and can't pay it back. In Volume 1, Runa's banker Ichijou explained it to her with a pudding that cost ten thousand yen and wouldn't resell for a hundred. Her bank, Keika Holdings, was built by swallowing banks stuffed with that kind of pudding. And now, sitting in her office after a Golden Week dinner, eating an actual pudding, she explains to Ichijou why the era is ending without her. Her bank, "which has basically functioned as a garbage can," is going to be listed on the stock market as the government's showpiece of a successful clean-up. "Who's supposed to save troubled banks after that? No one is around to fill that role."

Nobody has even asked Keika for help this time. Ichijou knows why. Minister Takenaga, the minister in charge of the economy and the banks, has said on television again and again that Keika will be taken public, and absorbing another bank would delay that "and allow you to hold on to your power, my lady." The prime minister doesn't want that. Runa's own narration draws the lesson: from now on, bad debts are to be handled "not through tactics that crossed into a gray area but through fundamental principles in accordance with the law and established processes." Her private, improvised rescue system, the Keika Rules, is being retired by the state.

"Well, I'm a junior high schooler now too," she says, "so I can't get away with being reckless any longer." Ichijou, who has been her gambling partner since she was five, gives her a gambler's advice. "The hardest part of gambling is quitting while you're ahead. But now you've got the dealer going out of their way to signal you. It's best to obey and head home with your winnings." She knows exactly what the signal means: "And leave a casino that'll ban me from entering again, right?"

Then they go through the patients. Gowa Osan, one of the big banks, won't survive. It's behind on its bad debts and has angered the Financial Services Agency (the FSA, the government office that inspects banks) by resisting its audit, so it will probably be broken up "as an example." Honami, another, is in trouble too, and desperately trying to raise a trillion yen. Iwazaki's bank and Futaki-Yodoyabashi are competing to absorb them. Angela had suggested that Keika swallow both and become a world-class megabank. Runa has refused. "I'd remain an outside observer this time." (There's a comic footnote about Honami: it had relied on a computer system her group helped build, and when she withdrew, the bank's people coined the slogan "We can't go to Keika for help!")

Then Ichijou mentions something new, something that never existed in her first life. Karafuto Bank, once the central bank of the communist Northern Japan, was nationalised at reunification and loaded with all the bad debts of every bank in Karafuto. It might now be merged with Honami or Gowa Osan. And Runa goes very still, because she has worked out where she's heard the name. In the game, the girl she replaced vanished after getting involved in money laundering, and a laundering scheme needs a bank. "Karafuto Bank had been the name behind that money laundering, and the institution that controlled me in the game." She asks Ichijou to look into it: she doesn't want to buy it, but "I think they'll end up the eye of the storm."

"Sadly, that prediction would prove correct."

The next scene is one of my favourite bits of comedy in the volume. Runa has long wanted to eat katsudon, the fried-cutlet rice bowl that detectives in Japanese TV dramas give suspects to make them confess, in a police interrogation room. So she visits the police box near her tower with enough for everyone: "You don't want any katsudon? But I had some made for everyone…" Inspector Ono sighs: "You watch too much TV, little lady." Director Maefuji, the Public Safety officer still banned from visiting her family's house, simply starts eating. It turns out to be beef cutlet with Hokkaido rice and matsutake mushrooms. And then one of them informs her that katsudon is actually banned from interrogation rooms, because it looks like bribing suspects. *Clack!* She drops her chopsticks, and both policemen laugh.

But she's come to trade information, and what she gives them is startling. When she was kidnapped as a small child in Volume 1, the ransom money came from Karafuto Bank. Maefuji isn't surprised. The police already suspect Karafuto is "the new place chosen to launder underground money" that fled Hong Kong when it was handed to China in 1997, and that the vast sums spent rebuilding Karafuto after reunification partly "slip[ped] out of sight." The bank, run by the prefecture and the government's Karafuto Reconstruction Agency, "was the entrance and exit for all that money. Also, this katsudon is delicious."

Then he tells her a piece of this world's history she didn't know. When the North collapsed, Japan was governed by the opposition, and its prime minister, a nobleman, won huge popularity by steering the country to reunification. Then the bill came due. He brought in a "National Welfare Tax," which was "essentially a Karafuto restoration tax," and it brought him down. The governments that followed didn't want to pay for Karafuto either, so they left its rebuilding to the great conglomerates, and dirty money followed. Runa finishes the thought: "Zaibatsu contain nobles, and nobles have diplomatic immunity." Crimes were waved through as being for the sake of unification. "The prime minister took the ultimate responsibility, while the government was reduced to a nest of demons. That's the kind of place Karafuto is, my lady."

Inspector Ono adds something sadder, from his years as a street detective. "I probably shouldn't say this, but there's no 'good' and 'evil' when it comes to money, little lady. The yakuza may use homeless people to commit their crimes, but those homeless people still need yakuza money to live. The longer I do this job, the more I think that's the one problem that can't be solved."

Her maid Eva, a CIA officer, asks the question that hangs over the rest of the book. "But are you really going to open that Pandora's box? You might damage the current administration severely." "Yes, I am," Runa says. "I know the prime minister would open it if it was him."

I like how this chapter quietly closes the series' first great story. The bad-debt era that made Runa rich is ending by law, without her, and the state is taking her toys away, gently and for good reasons. But in the same breath the book opens its next story, and it's the one she has been dreading since Volume 1: the money that ruined the girl in the game has a name, an address and a history, and it has been close to her all along. It paid her kidnappers when she was small.

- **Bad debt** (non-performing loan): a loan the borrower probably can't repay in full.

- **Listing / going public**: selling a company's shares on the stock market.
- **Keika Rules**: Runa's clean-up procedure from Volume 1: fire the managers, cancel the old shares, bring in new money, and send the bad loans to the RCC (the Resolution and Collection Corporation, a state company that buys bad loans).
- **Megabank**: one of Japan's giant banks formed by mergers.
- **FSA**: the Financial Services Agency, Japan's bank inspector and regulator.
- **Golden Week**: a cluster of public holidays in late April and early May.
- **Money laundering**: disguising illegally earned money so it looks legitimate.
- **Katsudon**: a bowl of rice topped with a fried cutlet and egg.
- **Public Safety**: the Japanese police branch that handles espionage, terrorism and foreign agents.
- **Zaibatsu**: a family-controlled business conglomerate.
- **Diplomatic immunity**: here, the English translation's usual term for Japanese nobles' legal immunity in this world.
- **Pandora's box**: in Greek myth, a box that released every evil into the world when opened; here, the hidden political loans and losses that could hurt many people if exposed.

## Chapter 2, part two: A coup because she keeps winning

The weekly executive lunch has become a banquet for five hundred people, and Runa, who still can't drink, gives the shortest speech in corporate history: "I don't have anything to address." She believes eating together builds solidarity. What it mostly does, she notices, is make the factions visible. In her railway, people from the two big Imperial Railway companies, East and West, are fighting for Tachibana's seat. In her bank, the old city bankers glare at anyone connected to Angela, who has been named heir. Karin works the room in English. In her trading company, mid-merger, everyone is sounding everyone else out. Only Okazaki sits beside her and simply eats his lunch.

She's introduced to three newcomers: Mikihara, the railway's next president; Chloe Meyers, Karin's new deputy; and Mark M. Barnes, a former Wall Street fund manager Angela has hired for the securities arm. Barnes glances at the bank's top table and says, "you haven't fired anyone big yet, have you?" The table's mood plummets. "That's not my style," Runa says. *Ah. This is a man of Wall Street, not any company.*

Then, at the door of her private room, an old man from the Osaka office stops her. His name is Tenmabashi Mitsuru, he used to work at one of the trading companies she bought, and he gets straight to the point.

"My lady, a coup d'état is currently underway within the Keika Group to remove you from power."

She offers him *bubuzuke*, rice with tea poured over it. In Kyoto, offering it is a famously polite way of telling a guest it's time to leave. He accepts happily and asks for seconds.

Inside, with Tachibana, Ichijou, Toudou, Okazaki and Eva, he explains that the plot isn't in one company but all four: the bank, the trading company, the electronics group and the railway. Then he takes two dice from his pocket. "Now, tell me, do ya want odd or even? You've got lots of chips left to bet. Head home now, and you can still have yourself a triumphant sushi feast."

Okazaki understands first, and his explanation is the best single insight into Runa's position in the whole volume. Koizumi defeated her. That's not the problem. The problem is that before the defeat she kept winning, and she's still at the table. "They owe you for saving them, little missy," Tenmabashi says. "However, only a few of 'em—even inside the giant Keika Group—are capricious enough to bet the rest of their lives on your gambles." Picture yourself at a casino with a friend who has already won enough for everyone to go home rich, and who keeps pushing the whole pile back onto the table. "If you walk away now, we'll come out with our winnings… But if you stay behind and spend all your chips, none of us will get to go home either. Doesn't it just make sense that people would try to stop you?"

"This coup wasn't targeting me because I'd been defeated," Runa realises. "It was because I was winning and needed to be removed from the table." It's the most ordinary motive in the series, and the most convincing. These are people with millions, watching a girl bet hundreds of billions.

The rest of the diagnosis is uncomfortable. The Japanese executives are uniting against the foreigners she brought in; the railway's two camps are at odds; the trading company is playing musical chairs. "You're payin' the price for not purging those people with your iron fist when you had the chance." Ichijou is a minority on his own board, protected only as her spokesman and as one of the two people who can touch the Moonlight Fund. And Tachibana explains why now. Parliament is about to inject public money into all the big banks. That money becomes shares, a government stake that, if a hostile board refused to give it back, could outvote her. And the plotters mean to build up her adoptive father, the duke, as their figurehead. Tenmabashi puts it brutally: "That's how they're goin' to keep you locked away for good, little missy." The glossary has a word for it: *oshikome*, when a feudal lord's chief vassals simply locked him up and ran the domain without him.

Then the picture widens. Has she heard of Gekkou Investment Firm? Okazaki has: a copy of the Moonlight Fund from mainland China, so blatant that people ask whether it's theirs. "They're imitating the Moonlight Fund's actions to perform their money laundering. You've done too much, little missy." What they want, Tenmabashi says, isn't money. "It's fame. Just like 'the man who broke the Bank of England,' what they're really after is the Moonlight Fund's reputation as Her Majesty's Escort Fleet." The man who broke the Bank of England is George Soros, who bet against the British pound in 1992 and won. And Runa sees the real target. "It's the yen, isn't it?"

Here's the scheme, step by step. Iraq is going badly, so America will need to borrow and print money, and the yen is likely to rise against the dollar. Speculators who bet on that rise need what every big speculation needs: a banner and a story. Gekkou has been raising money by pretending to be her fund. Once she's locked away, it will use her name to attack the yen, with a story everyone would believe: that she's finally going after Koizumi. The opposition will claim her support, Izumikawa's faction will be lured across, and Koizumi will be forced out or into an election. Tachibana completes it. Since Runa refuses to oppose the prime minister, the plotters have switched targets to her father. If rumour says Koizumi was behind the coup, the duke "will have no choice but to fight against the prime minister to protect Lady Runa."

And the money behind Gekkou? Runa says it before anyone else can: "Karafuto Bank." The old man's eyes change from a silly tanuki's to a carnivore's. She's right.

"Pretty sad, ain't it? If you'd just been born twenty years earlier, you probably could've climbed to the very top of this country…" "Sad? I don't want to have to shoulder any more burdens than I already do. Toudou, make this man a vice president and put him in charge of stopping the coup." On his way out he leaves one more gift: Mark Barnes, the new man at the lunch, used to be a fund manager at Karafuto Bank's New York branch.

At the next week's lunch Tenmabashi, cradling his head as the newly announced vice-president, works out the timetable from the calendar. Parliament's public-funds vote will come to a head in July, which is also when any snap election would be. So: a coup at the bank in June; then the duke has Izumikawa bring a no-confidence vote; then an election in July. And the trigger? Not the electronics group, where everyone would expect it. "Traps are traps because they're laid where you least expect 'em." It's the railway. Her bank has lent everything for the Shinjuku bullet train and the Shin-Tokiwa line. Turn those loans into bad debts, and Ichijou can be thrown out.

Then he says something that stops her cold. He has a hunch where some of the money for the failed Shinjuku Geofront bombing came from. *The attacks were supposed to be in 2001… Ah, I see now.* "So the plan to remove me has been in the works for quite some time." Tachibana and Eva both look away, "which told me everything I needed to know." They knew. Karafuto Bank's losses have stayed hidden because they sit under the Reconstruction Agency, which answers to the Cabinet Office, out of the Finance Ministry's sight. And if the bombing was meant to turn her railway loans into bad debts? "What're a few terrorist attacks if they'll get spare change out of 'em?"

She goes for advice to the hospital her family built, where politicians in trouble hide from prosecutors behind noble privilege, to see former Prime Minister Fuchigami, the man she always got on with. He listens, looks out of the window, and asks her a question. "Your Little Majesty, where do you intend to become queen?" She privately thinks she could be queen of Karafuto if she wanted to. He raises his voice. "Your Little Majesty, you shouldn't be in control of a country… You shouldn't be queen of Karafuto, Russia, or any country. International society—history itself—treats actresses like you like you're disposable. Once they're done with you, you'll be lost to time." He even thinks it's good that Koizumi is leading Japan. And then, gently: "You're already a superstar of history who can't run away from anyone. Your stage was chosen the moment you were born."

She thanks him, and in her own head she quietly contradicts him: "Maybe I wasn't suited to a role in Russia, but I would be perfectly cast in Karafuto."

The last scene shows how far ahead the plotters already are. Two of her company's ferries are already in the Persian Gulf, and now the Americans want to buy all four, including two still being built. Runa storms into the American embassy and shouts at the ambassador, an act to give Eva cover while she gathers information. Tenmabashi phones and tells her she's walked into an "alarm trap," a trap whose only job is to make a noise when you step on it. The ships belong to her security company and are rented on paper to Japan's forces in Iraq; people on her own side wanted to hand them over to please the Americans. And by noticing (only because her private cabins were cut and she got the bill) and by going straight to the Americans instead of her own government, she has shown her enemies two things: how tightly she still controls her group, and that she hasn't stopped watching Iraq. "That's some good material you just gave the people tryin' to overthrow ya." The people who did it, she realises, meant well. They wanted to take away her "toys" before she could "play with any more fire." "That came from a place of good intentions; it was so incredibly wicked."

Then Eva, reading the embassy report, goes pale. Turkey is crossing into Iraq over the Kurds. Iran won't negotiate. And the occupation authority has banned the old ruling Ba'ath Party from public life, and "An estimated six hundred thousand armed Ba'ath Party members have now been set loose."

"There, in the deserts of the Middle East, Vietnam was trying to rear its ugly head."

That last line is Runa recognising the future she tried, monstrously, to prevent in Volume 5. Without the terror she wanted, the quagmire she remembered is forming anyway. And it's set beside a coup that, from top to bottom, is made of people who either fear her winning or want to protect her from herself. I think the volume's whole problem is in this chapter. The people around her can't tell any more whether she's a gambler to be stopped, a child to be protected, or a weapon to be captured.

## Chapter 3: It's your position that's wrong

The chapter's title borrows from Karl Marx, who wrote that great historical events happen twice, "the first time as tragedy, the second time as farce." The tragedy here turns out to be her father's death. The farce is the coup against her. But it opens with a baby.

"AH-BAH!" Runa is at the family house to meet her newborn nephew, and she touches his tiny hand as his mother shows her, and she calls him, in her narration, "the soul who was never supposed to have been born." In the game, his father Nakamaro died before he could ever be a father. This child exists only because Runa changed things. His name is Michimaro, and the ending "-maro" marks him as the heir of the house.

Then, alone with her brother, she asks the question she came to ask. "There's a plot in place within the Keika Group to remove me from power. Your son is one reason for that, isn't he?" "That's right," Nakamaro says. "I'm doing my best to put a stop to it too, but people get uglier when more money is involved."

He explains that there are two sources. The first is the branch families and old retainer houses, who have never given up the idea that the main family, her father or Nakamaro himself, should lead the group. Being a minor doesn't help: her legal guardians are her father and brother, so she has to "haul" them along behind her, and with Tachibana stepping down as her proxy guardian, that's harder than ever. The second source worries Nakamaro more: "the people who do us favors so they can cozy up to the Keikain family." People who think that if the family owes them, they can unseat her, and who might use the family's legal immunity to remove obstacles "legally or illegally," as has happened before. Worst of all, some of these plotters believe they're doing it for her. They've convinced themselves she'll be safer if the group is taken away from her.

And her father? He'll do nothing, Nakamaro says. Only if the government turns out to be behind the plot will he use the Privy Council, the nobles' council, to protect her. Runa thinks it's "just about the worst strategy I could imagine." Why not stop the coup first? Then Nakamaro tells her why, and it's "like a slap in the face."

"Father won't let there be a repeat of what happened to my uncle, your birth father… The Fellowship of Constitutional Government's administration in those days was what drove Uncle to commit suicide. He was backed into a corner to escape America's wrath and protect then-Chief Cabinet Secretary Katou. Father and I don't want you to end up like he did."

That's the tragedy in the title. Her father, Otsumaro, died because the ruling party needed someone to take the blame, and it was him. Runa's private reaction is very her: *Ah. When you put it like that, Father, it makes me want to go and support the opposition…*

Then she goes to see Professor Kanbe at his university, with her maid Erika pouring tea, and he gives her the most important lesson in the volume. He starts with history. Oda Nobunaga, who had soldiers enough to rule Japan, was betrayed by his general Akechi Mitsuhide. Caesar was stabbed by Brutus. "There's a reason behind everything." Her group, he says, has a fatal flaw. Does she know what it is?

"That I, the leader at the top of the organization, don't have the right to control it?" "Correct."

It's an astonishing thing to realise about a girl who controls trillions of yen. Her only official title anywhere is vice-president of TIG Backup Systems, the little company she and the three boys set up. Tachibana, Ichijou and Toudou act on her word, but she holds no post above them. Erika asks the obvious question, doesn't she own the Moonlight Fund? And Runa explains: "do you think they'd let a child withdraw hundreds of billions of yen? Tachibana and Ichijou made withdrawals as my guardians. At any rate, there's no official organization called the Moonlight Fund." Its staff in her tower are, on paper, employees of her bank and her trading company. Kanbe says the structure was meant to protect her from blame if anything went wrong, and it has worked, "Actually, I'd say too effectively."

There's an easy fix, of course: take a job that controls everything. "Power comes with responsibility," she says. "I wouldn't have time to talk to you like this anymore." "Right," says Kanbe, amused. "We call that 'growing up.'"

So she asks him the question at the centre of her whole second life, as a child's body with an adult's soul: "Just what is an 'adult,' anyway?"

He lights a cigarette, sets it on the ashtray, and never smokes it. However much she achieves, he says, most adults won't take her seriously, because she looks like a child, and he knows exactly what they'd say: "Stop babbling and go to school." She could, perhaps, tear down society and its laws if she wanted to. But then she'd be forced to decide everything and answer for everything. "History calls such people 'dictators.'"

"It's adults' duty to scold children when they go down the wrong path." "What about my path was wrong?" And his answer is the line I think the whole volume turns on.

"Don't misunderstand. You, personally, haven't made any wrong choices yet. But I don't know what the future will hold once the poison of power infects you. It's your position that's wrong."

Then he becomes very gentle. "You aren't considering your own happiness at all, are you? We speak quite frequently, but you've never once told me your dreams, your future aspirations, or what makes you happy." She could probably change the world by herself, he says, but she'd have to become its victim to do it. "Let me be clear, Keikain-kun. It's all right for you to seek your own happiness. You're a child, so you can choose the people at your side—it's not for this world, or for history, to decide for you."

And he offers her some "magic words" for the day she's an adult and a child's unreasonableness hits her: "Give up. We adults have already learned to accept that."

She wants to take his kindness. But she can't. To accept those words would be to resign herself to her own future ruin, the ending she remembers from the game. So she shakes her head. "It's a tough thing to be too much of a genius," Kanbe says. "I guess it's natural to reject those magic words, since you can see the future."

I find that exchange almost unbearably sad. Everyone tells her that a normal life is waiting if she'll only let go. The one thing she knows, and they don't, is that letting go is how the girl in the game was destroyed.

On her way out she runs into Eiichi at the coursework drop box. They take the same university class, two short kids in a sea of tall students. He asks her to come to the cafeteria with him; she agrees on condition that he buys her a parfait. "I couldn't help enjoying that exchange," she tells us, "and I couldn't help wanting to become an adult with Eiichi-kun and my friends. That was why I also couldn't help being aware that I was one step behind on the coup."

The second half of the chapter is two lessons in business. First, Holland Town, a Dutch-style theme park in Nagasaki, with canals and steeples that make her think of Venice. One of the struggling megabanks, Honami, has ¥200 billion of bad loans tied up in it and its executives trail behind her, sweating, hoping she'll buy. Her guide is Mark Barnes. "It's the real deal. I guarantee you that much." So why is it failing? "Isn't it obvious? Because it's the real thing."

He explains how theme parks really make money. The park itself doesn't pay. It gathers people, and the money is made elsewhere: railways carry the visitors, and housing built beside the park pays back the construction. But nobody needs a new housing district in a city of 250,000, and in the bubble the president refused to pre-sell the holiday homes, which would have been "like predicting rice profits while the paddies were still green." Why didn't the bank push him? Because the bank that sponsored the project was proud of it: its "charismatic former president proposed this theme park."

Runa sees herself in that man at once. "His charisma had made him untouchable, but that was his fatal flaw… Things could turn out the same way for me if I mess up." Mark: "I believe two hundred billion yen is a small price to pay for that lesson, my lady." Then he says something odder. If she held the place for fifty or a hundred years, it could become "an irreplaceable source of profits for you and the Keikain family." "Oh? Are you suggesting I turn this place into my own country?" He doesn't laugh, and she sees in his eyes "a sense of deep darkness." She hears Fuchigami's voice: *Your Little Majesty, you shouldn't be in control of a country.* "I don't feel like playing queen here," she says, and buys it anyway, "for the sake of patronizing culture." On the way home she asks Eva how far she can trust Mark. "It's best not to trust him, but as you can see, he's someone you can use."

Second, a film crew follows her into her own little company's office. Karin has hired a team to make training manuals for Keika Electronics Union and is having an American institute analyse the footage. When she first came to Japan, she says, nobody would teach her anything; she was told to "Learn to do the job by watching everyone else" and to "steal" techniques. That can't work for a company selling worldwide, with factories in East Asia and Karafuto. She gives two examples of what filming reveals: veterans quietly correcting a faulty machine that newcomers were never told about, and the women who served tea remembering every regular client's name and favourite drink, which turned out to explain a whole division's sales. "That's the strength of the United States, my lady. We know how to analyze things and put them into training guides."

Runa asks, a little mischievously, whether she's going to restructure anyway. "Of course." But she'll do it without wrecking the company. "Laying off workers after a merger is the worst thing anyone could do. The company would lose all the techniques and training guides it acquired." She wants to give every employee, even temporary staff, one share's worth of pay on top of their bonus, so that everyone feels the share price. And when Runa needles her that in America people raise share prices through layoffs, Karin answers with pride.

"…But how could I ever run the company so disgracefully when I know my successors will have to follow in my footsteps? Besides, my lady, you're someone who started out on the other side of that glass ceiling that I had to break through. People like Angela and me are providing one of the routes you can take in life."

"…Thank you, Karin."

The finished manual gains one extra line: *Lady Keikain Runa, major shareholder. Drinks grape juice exclusively.*

Three adults, three answers to her question. Her brother is the capable heir who knows his limits. Kanbe is the adult who scolds and consoles, and tells her she's allowed to be happy. Karin is the one Runa thanks: an adult is someone who leaves a path open for the people coming after. I notice that it's the only one of the three she answers with thanks.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Marx's "tragedy… farce"**: from *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* (1852), on history repeating itself.
- **Branch families / retainer houses**: lesser houses descended from or sworn to the Keikains.
- **Legal guardian**: the adult legally responsible for a minor.
- **Privy Council**: in this world, a council of nobles with real political power.
- **Noble immunity**: in this world, Japanese nobles' legal protection from arrest.
- **Fellowship of Constitutional Government**: the novel's ruling party.
- **Chief Cabinet Secretary**: the government's chief spokesman and coordinator, a very senior post.
- **Oda Nobunaga / Akechi Mitsuhide**: the warlord who nearly unified Japan, and the general who betrayed and killed him in 1582.
- **Moonlight Fund**: Runa's private investment fund, which has no legal existence of its own.
- **Dictator**: a ruler with total power and no one to answer to.
- **Megabank**: one of Japan's giant banks formed by mergers.
- **Bad debt**: a loan that probably won't be repaid in full.
- **Restructuring**: reorganising a company, often with layoffs.
- **Stock options**: the right for employees to buy company shares, linking pay to the share price.
- **Glass ceiling**: the invisible barrier that keeps women out of top jobs.

## Chapter 4: Her friends decide

Chapter 4 is one of the loveliest in the series, and Runa is barely in it. It's seven small scenes, each told from the side of someone who cares about her: her friends, their parents, the women of her household. In almost every one, an adult quietly hands a child a message about Runa, something like "Keep a little distance," or the news that her family "wants her to lie low," and the child has to decide what to do with it. Professor Kanbe has just told her she can choose the people at her side. This chapter shows us those people choosing her back.

It starts in a café, where Asuka has "just happened" to run into Yuujirou after his club ("a lot of work had been done behind the scenes"). She asks him, sweetly, "Would you like to become a teacher, Izumikawa-kun?" The word she uses is *sensei*, which in Japan is also what you call a member of parliament. He understands at once. "If I did, the Ehime electoral district would go from my surname to 'Kasugano,' wouldn't it?" She's proposing that one day he marry into her family and inherit her father's seat.

Her reasoning is coolly political. Her father will probably go no higher than cabinet minister. Cities are growing, the countryside is emptying, and the seat will come down to swing voters and younger opposition candidates. The two of them can stand for parliament at twenty-five, five elections from now. "Only ten years away." She doesn't want to run herself: a woman running in a rural seat is "a real demerit," and after watching her father, she doesn't want it. Her life has two choices, the narration says, to be a politician or a politician's wife, and she's fighting to choose the better one. Yuujirou's own family is in an ugly fight over his father's seat, and his father, the deputy prime minister, refuses to intervene: "If it falls apart here, then that's as far as it goes."

"I'm your escape route, Izumikawa-kun," Asuka tells him, with a perfect politician's-daughter smile. "Please don't ever forget that." She has seen, as women in this book always do, that he loves Runa, and that his best friend Eiichi loves her too. Then, almost carelessly, she passes on a piece of gossip: one of his brothers-in-law is said to be in touch with "someone from the Keika Group." When Yuujirou tells Runa later, she is genuinely surprised. Whoever it is, it isn't her.

The second scene is the one that made me cry. Mitsuya's mother sends him out in the rain with an umbrella to meet his father at the station, which can only mean his father wants to talk. They go to a family restaurant for sirloin steak, and his father, a rising star at the Finance Ministry, tells him what has happened. Because his son is Runa's friend, the ministry sees him as part of Izumikawa's faction, and in a bureaucracy, careers are built "based on a lack of demerits, not an excess of merits." His superiors have told him to think about "the good of the ministry."

"Is this really what you want?" Mitsuya asks. "Of course not!" His father raises his voice more than he meant to, then speaks more quietly. He had dreamed of becoming the ministry's top official, as his own father had. "I made it through the ministry's scandal and reorganization, and as I continue to rise, my outstanding son is becoming my weakness. I was angry at first, but then I was at a loss. I questioned what I'd spent my whole life doing." And then he chooses his son. He'll settle for a lesser post; he doesn't want to keep climbing "if it means ruining your future." He even knows, with a Finance Ministry man's pride, that "you've already made a lot more money than I have." "Ah. I feel a lot better now that I've finally said that. Do your best in life, Mitsuya."

Outside it's starting to drizzle, and Mitsuya quotes a line from an anime Runa likes: "'…It doesn't matter if some people dance in the rain without umbrellas. That's what it means to be free.'" His father grins. "Should we walk home without umbrellas too?" "I like the sound of that, Dad. Let's run home like we're dancing, okay?" The drizzle becomes a downpour. They arrive soaked, his mother is speechless, and Mitsuya catches a cold and misses school. When he comes back, he tells Runa he doesn't want her to give his father a job at one of her companies. "Hang on! That's the first I'm hearing of this!" Someone has been making offers in her name.

The third is Eiichi and his father, who speaks to him "as boss and subordinate," because the boy already runs a company. There's trouble in the Keikain family, the father says, "adults being busybodies, giving advice like 'Stop babbling and go to school.'" And he tells Eiichi why: "Iraq. It sounds like the young lady drafted plans for all the logistics involved in the war." Eiichi is lost for words. "I don't care whether she plays computer games or financial games, but war games would make her a death merchant, and that's not a title any child should hold." He laughs at himself as he says it, because in a real civil war abroad, soldiers bolted heavy guns onto his company's pickup trucks.

So what would the father have done, Eiichi asks, if someone told him to "go to school"? "Isn't it obvious? I'd have ignored them." He grew up as the young master too, he says, and worked for everything, and is now "nothing more than the Teia Group's leader." "Eiichi, you should treasure the position you have right now… If it's what you want, I wouldn't mind seeing you go as far as to throw away the Teia name." Then he adds that his son should learn to drink, carefully, because "Teaching children bad habits, then scolding them, is an adult's job." As Eiichi leaves, his father says, just loudly enough to be overheard, "Ah, I need to make that call to MITI…" That's the old name of the trade ministry. The meddling came from there, and the ministry has an interest in Runa's resource business. Eiichi mutters, "This is about oil…?"

The fourth scene is told in the first person by someone we've barely met: Machiyoi Sanae, a girl from the choir, who fell in love with Runa's voice the first time she heard her sing. "Ah. I was born to hear this." Runa is helping the choir prepare for an autumn competition, and the gossip is that the Berlin, Moscow and Vienna Philharmonics all toured Japan this summer hoping to recruit her. Runa, picking up the music for a choral piece called "Daichi Sanshou," a hymn of praise to the earth, says she probably should study music abroad if she can. "She sang it beautifully, but her face always looked sad."

Then Sanae lets slip something she shouldn't: Runa's family has been sending "tokens of appreciation" to the noble families whose daughters belong to the Courtiers, the nobles' clique, as "payment for joining." The adults have been buying Runa a social circle. "We're children, so we can't comment on the jobs adults do," Runa says, and Sanae hears a hidden anger in it. Then Runa asks her a strange question.

"Tell me, Sanae-san, do we have a duty to be happy?"

Sanae answers honestly, the way you would. "I think so. I mean, we don't want people who know us to see us unhappy, do we?"

Runa is lost for words. Then she laughs. "Ah ha ha! You're right. It's so obvious, but I didn't even see it."

I think that's the key line of the whole volume, and look who says it: a girl with no power at all, who will never do anything great, as she tells us herself, but who in that one moment "truly felt happy." Kanbe told Runa she was allowed to seek her own happiness. Sanae tells her she owes it to the people who love her.

The fifth scene is at Takahashi Akiko's house, where her father, a prefectural police chief, has a dojo and a party of officers drinking. They let her stay, which in Japan is a kind of initiation into adulthood, and then, "pretending to be drunk," they talk about Runa within her hearing. The Geofront plot proved Japan is a target. Runa is friends with the American president. "I heard the Keikain family wants her to lie low too." Even the duke doesn't have a grip on things. "…The problem is that the young lady isn't the type to just give in and run away when she needs to." Akiko understands she's been given a message to carry. "On that day, Akiko thus became an adult": she drinks a cup of sake and collapses, and her mother makes all three men kneel and apologise.

The sixth is Katsuki Shiori, stopped in a corridor by Lydia, the senior from Karafuto. "Tell her, 'Keep a little distance.'" Shiori's own parents have said the same. If Runa falls, Lydia warns, their positions will suffer. "So?" says Shiori. "In that case, we'd just live perfectly normal student lives." And Lydia's voice drops low enough to make her shiver. "Normal is when you can expect tomorrow to be the same as today," she says. "I believed that too. At least, until the Berlin Wall came down." Then she hands Shiori a sealed envelope, supposedly a list of Keikain and Iwazaki relatives behaving badly, stamped with the crest of the vanished Northern Japanese state. Shiori passes it to Runa's people. The paper inside, she's told later, is blank. Perhaps it was a test of her loyalty. She isn't interested.

And the last scene belongs to the women of the household. Tachibana gathers Runa's oldest staff, her secretary Aki, the head maid Keiko and Katsura Naomi, "the very first whom the young lady employed," together with Ichijou's daughter Erika, "one of the few people she's ever scouted," and tells them they will be "a breakwater" if the main family tries to push Runa around. Erika lists Runa's day: school, opera lessons, company approvals until dinner, emergency reports during her games at night. "She really has no time to rest." Naomi says the old motto was "study hard and play hard," but Runa's is "work hard, study hard, play hard," and "What she needs more than any of those things is time to rest." Tachibana, so rarely unsure of anything, admits that the people trying to rein her in "also wish her to be happy." "Lady Runa is trying to save people as she saved us. As her debtors, we're merely worried she'll exhaust herself."

Then Keiko, who has seen everything in her years in Ginza's nightclubs, says quietly: "Like in 'The Happy Prince.'"

In Oscar Wilde's story, a golden statue of a prince gives away his gold leaf and jewelled eyes to the poor, one piece at a time, carried by a swallow who stays too long into the winter, until the statue is grey lead and the swallow dies at his feet. That's what they're afraid of. Not that Runa is wrong, but that she'll give herself away. "We don't get in her way, but we just want her to be happy, so we won't let her be unhappy." Erika and Aki, the two youngest, promise each other to work hard for her, and don't notice the older ones watching them with smiles.

What I love about this chapter is that nobody in it is a villain, and almost nobody is powerful. Three fathers give something up for their children: Mitsuya's father his career, Eiichi's father the family name, even Yuujirou's father his control over his own succession. Set that beside Runa's own father, who, Nakamaro told her in Chapter 3, would do nothing. And each of her friends, handed an adult's message, chooses: Asuka offers an escape route, Mitsuya refuses a favour, Eiichi gets permission to do as he pleases, Akiko carries the warning, Shiori chooses "normal," and the household chooses to stand between her and the family. The answer to Kanbe's question isn't any one of them. It's all of them together.

## Chapter 5: A day off, with six cars behind it

After a coup, a dead father and a lecture on dictatorship, the book gives Runa a holiday. Chapter 5 is pure comedy, a run of small scenes from her "daily life," and I want to say one thing before I retell it. The chapter opens with a swimsuit campaign she poses for, for her own department store, and later includes a film shoot and a home video, and in those parts the narration dwells on the body of a girl in her first year of junior high in ways I'm not going to reproduce. I'll mention them only where the plot needs them. The rest of the chapter is sweet and very funny, and I'll give you that.

The first joke is about perfume. Her department store has made her a signature scent for its fragrance fair, and her head maid Keiko insists a lady must pay attention to more than her looks. So she wears it to school. Eiichi sniffs and announces he's figured it out: "It smells like the grape juice you're always drinking." "You're so stupid, Eiichi-kun!" When Yuujirou comes to make peace, she complains to him about Eiichi, and he gently points out that she drank half a litre of grape juice at lunch, and then adds that he's been noticing "some kind of herbal scent." Furious, she goes to the library to complain to Mitsuya, who doesn't even look up from his maths. She demands that he guess. "It smells like soap."

That night she stress-eats garlic steak and spaghetti. Then Keiko puts the perfume bottle on the table in front of her, unopened, the seal still intact. Runa overslept that morning after gaming late and never put it on at all. Keiko's lecture lasts two hours. Next day she wears a mask to hide the garlic, the boys think she has a cold and are sweetly kind to her, and she has to sit there suffering in guilty silence while her associates stare at her "with very cold eyes."

The second scene is my favourite. Runa, Asuka, Hotaru and little Mio, who calls the other three her "elder sisters," design perfumes together in Runa's tower. Runa's is clary sage, rose attar and musk. Asuka's has mandarin in it, because of course it does; Hotaru, asked what she'd wear, says simply "…The aroma of incense," and chooses sandalwood. Mio wishes aloud that "all my big sisters and I could be connected by a scent…" and takes one note from each of theirs. It's a lovely idea. When the four of them finally put their perfumes on together and get into one car, the mixture is so dreadful that they have to go home and wash. The next day Mio says that if she ever gets a boyfriend she'll wear her perfume for him, and the three elder sisters reply, in voices that drop several degrees, that they'd love to meet him. Even Mio can tell that Hotaru's smile "contained no such warmth."

The third scene is a bicycle ride. Runa won a bike on a television show and has never used it, so one evening she decides to ride to the sea. Two of her associates, Yuna and Glasya, come with her on road bikes, and insist she wear a helmet and pads, which accidentally launches a new fashion: young ladies in racing gear on shopping bikes. The seven kilometres from her tower to Takeshiba Pier take two hours, because of traffic lights, parked cars and pavements where bikes aren't allowed. People recognise her ("She's the young lady who won an Academy Award"), and she tells them she doesn't sign autographs on her day off.

Then they reach the water, and the book does something it does well. The Rainbow Bridge is glowing red in the sunset, the skyscraper lights are coming on, and out on the bay Runa sees houseboats. They came back in the bubble, when land was unaffordable, and now they house people from the old Northern Japan, mostly manual labourers working along the coast. Tokyo wants them gone, calling them "hotbeds for criminal activity." The government is converting old tankers into floating "ark cities," partly to screen "high-risk individuals"; one is going up off Kisarazu, and the underground city being built at Shinjuku will hold up to a hundred thousand people. Glasya, eating one of Keiko's sandwiches, pats her bicycle. "People from Karafuto, like me, see a bicycle as their first asset in life."

Twenty-four million people came south when the North collapsed. Public safety, Runa thinks, is improving, "but it wasn't necessarily good," so they don't ride home. They go by car, and it turns out that six cars have been following them all evening, including a van for the bikes. "Do we really need so many guards?" Eva: "Even this is on the conservative side. I think you'll understand that the next time you visit the United States."

She does, because the last part of the chapter jumps forward several months, to the Academy Awards. In Volume 5 the narration promised that the award for Best Supporting Actress would one day have her name on it, and here it does. The morning after she wins, a Cadillac full of Secret Service agents pulls up at a shabby studio on the edge of Hollywood that makes late-night zombie and shark films, and the newly crowned actress walks into an audition for the part of "shark bait." The studio boss had only offered her the role so he could use a look-alike, never dreaming she'd say yes. Every excuse he comes up with she knocks down. No sponsors? Her company will find some. No director? She phones hers, who "won the Academy Award for Best Director this year." Her secretary pays all the other actresses' travel costs from a case of used ten-dollar bills, because hundreds "would suspect they were counterfeits."

The result, *Samurai Ghost Shark vs. Swimsuit Noblewoman*, takes half a day to shoot and a week to edit, has an official budget of $243, and becomes a sensation on American television. The in-book review praises it for turning the prey into the hunter, "In the end, it's us fleeing from her," and adds a line that could describe Runa herself: "Monsters are monsters because they don't speak. In that sense, the girl herself is another monster."

And then comes the joke that isn't a joke. The president, watching the film, chokes on a pretzel and passes out, and for a few minutes the nuclear button passes to "that hard-liner vice president," who "had even seriously considered pressing the button." His aides stop him with one sentence: "If you press it because of this movie, we'll get a Golden Raspberry Award," the prize for the year's worst film. The president wakes up and laughs. Then the narration, without any change of tone, tells us something chilling: "As the situation in Iraq deteriorated, debate about the use of nuclear weapons was heating up rapidly in the United States. The world was learning from Afghanistan that they needed to inflict more fear than was inflicted, or else their people and land would be the next ones turned to scorched earth."

I laughed at the pretzel, and then I stopped laughing. Remember that this is a world in which no city has ever been destroyed by a nuclear bomb, so there's no shared picture of what one does. In this chapter, the only thing standing between the world and its first demonstration is a joke about a bad-movie award.

The chapter ends in a television greenroom, with Runa and her director turning down a stack of big-studio offers: a spy series with a love scene, a senator's part in a space opera, an elf in a fantasy prequel. The director tells her, seriously for once, to always keep her best work in the future. "Humans can grow so long as they always believe that, but the second they realize their magnum opus is in the past, they start going downhill."

I think this chapter is exactly what Kanbe told her she was entitled to: perfume, a bike ride, friends, a silly film. And every bit of it is fenced in, by guards, cameras, money and a nuclear joke. Even her trip to the sea ends with six cars. That's what a day off looks like for her now.

## Chapter 6, part one: The platform at Narita

It begins with a small, kind errand. Angela is flying home from New York, and Runa wants to meet her at Narita Airport. The car would get stuck in traffic and it's too windy for the helicopter, so Eva suggests the one option Runa had forgotten she owned: her private train, a three-carriage express waiting on a siding under her tower. Angela has asked for Runa to bring plenty of staff, Eva admits, so that she can "interview" Eva on the way about whether Runa can safely be left in her care.

So the trip turns into the household's "very first evacuation drill," and the platform fills up like a film set. The orphan associates carry non-lethal sonic weapons or riot shields, all of their maid uniforms are bulletproof, her maid Ryouko has a real gun, the tower's special forces come along, and so does a police inspector, Natsume. Runa mutters that it seems like too many people. Ryuu Suzune pats her shoulder: "My lady, it might help your subordinates if you acted selfishly from time to time, like a normal young noblewoman would." "Okay, okay. I'm being selfish! Yes! Tell them I'm coming through!" Runa can't help laughing. "This was like going on a dangerous picnic."

The train takes about an hour, and pulls in at a special VIP platform at Narita. Then the doors don't open. Police have heard gunfire at the edge of the airport.

Here's what has happened, which we learn from three different rooms at once. American intelligence warned Japan of a plot by Islamic extremists, men with Russian passports who came in through Karafuto, to storm her tower and kill "the young lady who campaigned as if she was an idol for the American president," hoping to frighten Japan out of the Iraq War. The police caught five of them in a houseboat hideout, and Runa's people quietly arranged to get her out of Tokyo: the trip to Narita was meant to be the first leg of a flight to Hokkaido. But the extremists had bought their weapons from the Russian mafia, and the mafia, at the last minute, sold them out to the police in a plea bargain. In revenge, the extremists leaked the mafia's own plot. The Russian mafia had been hired to assassinate Angela when she landed at Narita, because she is "a North American surveillance worker with access to the Moonlight Fund" and has been looking into money laundering in Karafuto. Angela's jet diverted to Haneda, and she's safe in the American embassy. Runa, sent to the airport to be safe, has arrived in the middle of the other plot.

The gunfight is at a mafia warehouse near the airport, which turns out to be both an armoury and a drugs store, where the Russians trade weapons for narcotics with the Chinese mafia. When Runa asks Inspector Natsume whether human trafficking and drugs are mixed up in it too, he just smiles grimly: "There's no reason the mafia wouldn't get involved with those things." The guns are old Northern Japanese army stock, leaked out through Karafuto. The drugs come from Southeast Asia's Golden Triangle, now in short supply because Afghanistan's poppy trade was destroyed "in the genocide," and they travel out through Hokkaido and Karafuto and across Siberia by rail. And the money is laundered, of course, through Karafuto.

In the government's crisis centre, Runa's maid Anisha, a former KGB officer with "unofficial connections to the Russian government," briefs the deputy prime minister in a suit. The whole thing, she says, was "nothing more than a commonplace internal miscommunication." The bosses made the deal, so the gunmen at the bottom were never told. And then she adds a detail that makes the room go cold. Six extremists came in by the Siberian railway. Five were caught. "So who planted the bomb in the airport?"

On the platform, every television channel is covering the siege. Commentators argue about whether the police have enough firepower, and someone mentions the "old imperial police" and the Second February 26 Incident, the coup that haunts this world. Then a newsflash: an Islamic group has claimed the attack, and "Their target is Lady Keikain Runa, the noblewoman who incited the war." She's trapped at Narita. "Who the hell leaked this?!" Natsume shouts. Her associate Misaki, the clever one in glasses, traces it in minutes. European paparazzi who follow royalty tracked her schedule, and a post in a train-fans' thread online spotted her private train leaving Kudanshita. (I can't help smiling at that, since Runa posts in those threads herself.) The European media bought the story, and the Japanese stations used it as an excuse to break the agreed blackout. "That star's safety wasn't too important."

Reporters rush the platform doors. So do frightened travellers, who've heard it's the safest place in the airport. Then a coin locker explodes inside the security perimeter. Nobody is hurt, but the perimeter collapses. Her phone rings: it's Eiichi, who happens to be at Narita to meet some staff from his company's American office. He's booked her a hotel, and he has a bulletproof car.

And Runa makes the decision that I think shows how much she has grown since Volume 4. Her body is trembling, but she keeps her voice steady behind "the mask of a noblewoman." "I don't mind. Please allow any evacuees to enter. The rest of us will escape to a hotel in Narita." Her staff object. "Tell the reporters we'll have a press conference in half an hour." She knows exactly what's at stake. This will be treated as terrorism, the opposition will use it to attack Koizumi's Iraq policy, and if her guards are filmed shoving frightened civilians away from her, she will have handed the coup against her "the ultimate weapon." So she turns to the inspector and to Eva. "Inspector Natsume. Eva. I believe in all of you. So please allow me to do my job." Natsume agrees. The travellers are let onto the platform, searched, but kept off the train. And Runa thinks, a little detached, that she once filmed a scene exactly like this.

In the crisis centre, meanwhile, Koizumi walks in, waves everyone back into their seats, and heads for a corner to listen. The police don't have enough people to search every locker and bin while also besieging the warehouse. "Sounds like you need more people, eh? I'll give the orders, then." And with that, almost casually, he orders the Self-Defense Forces out on a public-security operation, a domestic deployment that parliament has to approve. "However things started, multiple countries see this as terrorism. Ours can't fall behind in the war on terror. I'll take responsibility for any fallout." It's pure Koizumi: fast, personal, and he owns it.

On the platform Eva explains what the soldiers will do. They'll take over the siege so the police can secure the terminals, and they're bringing paratroopers and even tanks, "as a show of power." The mafia, unlike the extremists, won't die for their warehouse. Then Eiichi's car is let up to the platform entrance, and Runa walks out into a wall of camera flashes and resentment. "Damn, why does some kid get all those guards to herself?!" Reporters shout questions about her American friends.

She runs up the stairs and sees the car, and Eiichi, and he's wrestling a girl who looks about their age to the ground. The girl has a grenade in her hand.

"Grenade!"

Runa doesn't think. She slips past the associate meant to shield her, leaps off the backs of the reporters, flies through the air, and pins the girl's wrist to the ground. The pin stays in. Police and maids swarm over them.

Then she's pulled away, and she and Eiichi find each other in the crowd. "Why do you have to do things right out of movies, Runa?" "Why did you imitate me when you haven't even starred in a movie, Eiichi-kun?" He grins, radiant. "I just wanted to try it. I looked cool, didn't I?"

*Ba-dump.* "My heart jumped in a different way this time." She doesn't want to admit it, so she says what she always says: "Dummy!" They're driven to a hotel and, the narration notes with satisfaction, scolded thoroughly by everyone.

I want to stay a moment with that girl on the stairs, because the book doesn't. She's the sixth terrorist, about Runa's own age, and that's all we learn about her. I can't help setting her beside Runa's associates, the orphan girls her people bought and trained as guards. Two girls from the same broken places, one sent to die for a cause and the other to die for a duchess. And I think the platform scene is the clearest picture yet of the adult Kanbe described: someone who takes responsibility for everything in front of her. She opens the doors to the crowd, and she calls it politics. It's also kindness.

## Chapter 6, part two: An account called Anastasia

An hour after the grenade, in the American embassy, Angela is swearing more than she ever has in her life. The footage of Runa flying through the air has been played in every department. And the man she's swearing at is Mark Barnes, the former Wall Street fund manager she scouted for Keika Securities. He turns out to be the protected witness the CIA asked her company to take in back in Chapter 1, the man who "came to us with a few souvenirs." "You've got some nerve endangering my lady like that." He's unbothered. "I see you've become nothing more than one of her servants." "Don't forget, you got saved by a plea deal. You don't want to go to prison, do you?"

Mark explains himself with a shrug. He was Karafuto Bank's trader on Wall Street. When Runa wiped out "tens of billions of dollars" there in Volume 4, he was on the losing side, and he had to change his name, his identity and even his face, with plastic surgery, and go to the CIA for protection. He survived, the narration says, on courage and the trump cards he held. Now Angela tells him she has checked his story, and it's true.

"The Romanov family treasure really does exist."

Here is the most important revelation in the book, so let me take it slowly.

Mark gives it a name. "They call the secret bank account 'Anastasia.'" It was opened at a private Swiss bank by Karafuto Bank, on the orders of the old Northern Japanese government, supposedly to hold the lost treasure of the Romanovs, Russia's last imperial family. Mark can't help laughing at the name, and you can see why: Anastasia was the tsar's youngest daughter, the one legend said had escaped the massacre. The money, the story went, would be released "when a legitimate heir to the Romanov family emerged."

That was only the label. In reality it was a laundering machine. Money laundering means taking dirty money, from sex, drugs or violence, and making it look clean by passing it through legitimate business. Think of a washing machine. Dirty money goes in as a "deposit" to the Romanov treasure. Then Karafuto Bank lends money out to perfectly respectable-looking Russian companies, using that deposit as security. The companies belonged to oligarchs, Russia's new super-rich, with mafia ties. The money that comes out the other side is a bank loan, and nobody asks where a bank loan came from.

It became, Mark says, "a piggy bank for the Russian mafia," and it suited everyone. The oligarchs, afraid of being purged by the Russian president, took up the cause of reviving the Romanovs rather than move against him. Survivors of the old Northern government, afraid of Karafuto breaking away, joined in. Officials of the Karafuto Reconstruction Agency and the prefecture saw it and looked away. "It was a system that left everyone happy, at least in the beginning."

Then the system broke, in two blows. The first was Russia's financial crisis in 1998, which ruined the oligarchs and left Karafuto Bank holding their loans. Because it was a nationalised bank, it could quietly cover its holes with tax money and call it bad-debt disposal. The second was Runa. "Of the tens of billions of dollars the young lady erased, about fifteen billion were debts related to Karafuto Bank." And once the oligarchs fell, people started to notice "the true treasure—the Moonlight Fund."

Angela finishes the thought, and it's the part that chilled me. Koizumi's government is now determined to clear every bad debt in Japan, so Karafuto Bank can't hide its losses much longer. "But what if the Romanov family treasure actually existed? It'd give you everything you needed to clean up your mess. In fact, that treasure's owner would be the young lady with the Romanov family's blood in her veins."

Do you see what that means? A laundering account that can only be released to a Romanov heir needs an heir. Runa, descended from the Romanovs through her mother, is the one person alive who could turn a criminal fiction into a legal inheritance. Her blood, which has been backstory since Volume 1, has become a financial instrument. Put bluntly, she is collateral. And the series planted it six volumes ago. After Runa was kidnapped as a small child, Yuujirou reassured her that the kidnappers wouldn't bother the boys "if they were after Romanov treasure." And in Chapter 2 of this volume, Runa mentioned in passing that the ransom money had come from Karafuto Bank.

But who wrote this drama? Only a few groups, the narration notes, are rich and determined enough to send an assassin after someone in the CIA, and the Russian president, who is on bad terms with his country's mafia, isn't one of them. Mark, enjoying himself, gives Angela a hint. "You know the young lady makes her moves with dollars and yen. So why don't you consider that other currency?" Angela goes pale. Oil is traded in dollars, and that's part of what keeps the dollar strong. And one country had been trying to change that: Iraq, which wanted its oil paid for in euros.

"Exactly. These are European investment funds. In other words, the young lady's games and the war in Iraq are entirely to decide what the hard currency of the twenty-first century will be. The witches in Europe have been writing this entire script."

I'd take that with a pinch of salt, at least for now. It's a man with a new face, talking to the CIA, pointing away from Wall Street and Moscow. Eva's advice from Chapter 3 applies: don't trust him, but you can use him. The last chapter of the volume will show us that he's at least partly right.

The chapter ends that night with a television special. Armoured cars marked *Kitakaba Security*, Runa's own security company, which employs former Northern Japanese soldiers, have joined the siege line, along with thousands of guards and riot police. The commentators replay "the moment Lady Keikain Runa personally stopped a terrorist attack," and one of them explains that nobles have trained in self-defence ever since the Second February 26 Incident. The boy who tackled the terrorist is named: Teia Eiichi. Then come the politics. The Self-Defense Forces haven't been deployed like this since that same old coup, and the opposition promises an investigation. Washington says it's "prepared to support our ally through their predicament by any means necessary." A Foreign Ministry source warns that losing Japan's many troops in Iraq "would change the United States' fundamental strategy in the conflict." And the snap election Koizumi was said to be planning may have been derailed. Then, live, the paratroopers arrive at Narita, a Type 90 tank is unloaded from its trailer, and a white flag appears from the terrorists' building.

So the chapter ends with a surrender and a question. Runa's blood, the Romanov treasure, the yen, the euro, Karafuto Bank, a coup inside her group: the book is gathering them into one knot. And at the centre of the knot is a girl who, as far as the book shows us, knows none of this yet, sitting in a hotel suite in Narita, being scolded for jumping on a terrorist.

## Chapter 7: The adults put on a play

The day after Narita, Runa is driven under heavy guard to the main Keikain residence instead of her own tower, so that "the 'adults' of the Keikain Dukedom would handle the inevitable matters of responsibility." And that night the adults hold a meeting about her, without her.

It opens with a bow. Angela, in front of the duke, his relatives and his aides, bows "so perfect that she almost looked like a Japanese person." Everyone in the room understands what it means for an unofficial agent of the United States to bow to them, and she brings a message: the American president is, "unofficially," worried about having drawn a child into such danger, which is "his form of an apology." Then Nakamaro, Runa's brother, who has already "unleashed the full force of his anger" on the police, says what the relatives are all thinking. "Do I need to be clearer? I don't want Runa starring as the heroine of this war on terror!"

You can feel the room lean forward. This is the chance they've been waiting for. If Runa can be made to step back, as a child should, then the rights to her group can pass to the dukedom, which means to her father Kiyomaro and his heir Nakamaro. Angela pleads, not as Runa's aide but frankly as someone looking after American interests: "If my lady steps back now, that will merely show the terrorists that she's yielded to them." Washington has looked into everything, even Kiyomaro's donations to the opposition party, because if Koizumi falls, Japan's troops will probably leave Iraq. Then the duke speaks. "The Keikain Dukedom isn't struggling financially, nor do we have political ambitions. All we want is for our family to be safe. I'd like to ask how the United States plans to guarantee that safety."

Before Angela can answer, Tachibana's phone rings. "It's Deputy Prime Minister Izumikawa." He puts it on speaker. Izumikawa says he'll hand over the phone, and everyone in the room knows there's only one person a deputy prime minister hands the phone to.

"This is Koizumi. Yesterday's incident was caused by a failure of the Japanese government. I, Koizumi Souichirou, would like to promise Lord Keikain that such a thing will never happen again."

Kiyomaro takes the phone, and his answer is beautiful. "I'll place my trust in you, Prime Minister Koizumi. I can do that because of the way you once scolded her as an adult. I have faith that you'll take responsibility for her like an adult as well." Koizumi promises to say so in the Diet, Japan's parliament. The call ends. Nobody in the room is foolish enough to cross both the prime minister and the United States, and the relatives' chance is gone.

Then the narration lets us in on the secret. The whole scene was staged. "Politics was nothing more than a terrible play, and a politician could prove their caliber based on how seriously they performed their role in such a play." It was "an abrupt farce between Tachibana, Angela, Kiyomaro, Nakamaro, and Izumikawa," cleared beforehand with the president and the prime minister, and the Americans were listening the whole time, through "the listening device Angela wore and the bug on Tachibana's phone." The purpose was to shut down the relatives quickly, and to keep Runa where Washington needs her, as "both a victim and a heroine of the war on terror." Even Nakamaro's anger was a role. And to give the audience their climax, Kiyomaro ends the meeting with a decree: "Still, there's no mistaking that we're in an unhealthy situation, so we'll have to strengthen the Keika Group's corporate governance. I'll also increase the power of the Choufuu Council, the meeting of presidents." "Not a single soul spoke up to protest Kiyomaro's declaration."

I love this scene, because it's Kanbe's lesson from Chapter 3 played back to us as theatre. Kanbe told her that an adult scolds children and takes responsibility. Here her father turns those very words into a bargain with the prime minister. And the adults protect her with the same weapon she uses on them: a performance.

But not everything was acting. In a limousine afterwards, Tachibana and Angela listen to Nakamaro on the phone. "It was such an obvious act," he says lightly. Then the narration tells us that he and his father had seriously meant to take Runa home and make her live as a normal student, and that it was Tachibana and Angela who talked them down. Nakamaro still has to scold her tomorrow, and his voice goes cold. "There won't be a next time. If there is, I'll ignore whatever the world tries to say to me and drag her back to the main family myself." "Even Angela had broken out into a cold sweat as she listened." It's the voice of a brother who loves her, and it's also a door quietly locked behind her.

The book then wanders through the rest of Tokyo, and each stop is a small satire. In a TV station, producers moan that they can't get her on air: she turns down nearly every offer, and her own company's adverts with her in them play just once per late-night slot to make them feel exclusive. "A duke's daughter would never understand the hearts of us lowly TV men." The people, the narration says, "wanted to see more of her—not to try to understand what might be going on in her own heart at present." The news people give the attack their verdict: "As far as entertainment goes, it was about a seven out of ten." And the narration answers them drily: "The grade came from people who saw reality as something that only happened on TV screens." They also pick up a rumour that the Keika Group has sent campaign managers to California, where the governor has just been recalled, and they guess she must be helping the liberals. They have no idea, the narration says, that "a movie actor was on the verge of becoming the conservative governor."

At the little police box near her tower, Inspector Natsume comes back from writing his second letter of apology about the same girl. His colleague Ono greets him with "Hey there, two-timer. You're a real playboy, aren't you?" The Keikain family has let it be known that it wasn't his fault, which in bureaucrat-speak means: this will hurt your career, but if you ever retire, there's a job with us.

And here the chapter slips in its economics, so let me slow down. The public story of Narita is Islamic terrorism. The truth, which has reached "the man in the Oval Office," is that the target was Angela, and that the Russian mafia set the whole thing up to protect its laundering machine. That machine ran through Wall Street, and it used the most modern tools there were. Dirty money went to shell companies, which are companies that exist only on paper. It was turned into securities, which are tradable financial products, and those were mixed in with ordinary securitised products and sold on. Think of pouring a jug of dirty water into a vast pot of soup and selling the soup by the bowl. Once it's mixed in, nobody can point to which spoonful was dirty. Wall Street, Natsume says, went along with it "while they feigned ignorance," because the dangerous money paid better interest.

Mixed into that soup were Russian government bonds, IOUs from Russia's government, which still had a junk rating, meaning the rating agencies judged them too risky for serious investors. And who had bought them in bulk? Keika Holdings, among other Japanese institutions, insuring the bet with options on oil and other resources, since Russia's fortunes rise and fall with the price of oil. So Runa's own bank is now one of the buyers Russia's debt depends on. And the last twist is the darkest: one source of Karafuto Bank's money turns out to be "money the United States had budgeted for undercover operations meant to sabotage the Eastern Bloc years earlier." The neocons, the hawks in Washington who are struggling over Iraq, have turned on the CIA. The two policemen, the narration notes, know nothing about any of this.

Then we come to the part of the chapter that made me happiest. At a table at the back of the school cafeteria, Runa's associates hold a crisis meeting while one of them keeps Runa busy elsewhere. Their leader, Nanami, looks exhausted; it's an open secret that after Narita she went to her room and hugged her bisque doll. Misaki, the adviser, says the unsayable: "Wouldn't it be more constructive to conclude that we're just not needed?" Yuna snaps back, "We have nothing without Lady Runa!" Riko, the girl Runa slipped past, asks how anyone could have predicted it. Miu says, "I knew the young lady pulled off amazing feats in her TV shows and movies, but I never expected her to try it in real life too." The footage has been analysed, and the grenade "probably would've gone off if Lady Runa hadn't done anything." Yulia and Irina, the two girls with ties to the CIA and to Moscow, report that their handlers asked them what on earth they'd been doing, and that they answered, "Could you have protected her in those circumstances?"

What hurts them, I think, is that they can read her. A normal client would have run or hidden behind her guards. They understand the wish behind what she did, "I don't want to endanger any of my associates," and that makes it harder, because it leaves them wondering what they're for. Suzune jokes, "Should we put a leash on her?"

And then Asuka and Hotaru arrive, Hotaru having eavesdropped on the whole meeting with her uncanny knack of going unnoticed, dragging Yuka, the adult who supervises the associates, by the hand. They put one of Runa's own comics down on the table. "What you're missing is an adult!"

After school, in a meeting room in her tower, the associates and the whole security staff gather. In the middle of the floor, kneeling formally until her legs go numb, is Runa, wearing a sign: "I ESCAPED MY GUARDS AND OVERPOWERED A TERRORIST." "This is really embarrassing…" "You reap what you sow," says Asuka, and Hotaru just nods. Their smiles don't reach their eyes, and the associates realise, watching them, that her friends were frightened for her too. Her secretary Aki, when Runa protests that she's an elegant young woman, asks, "How could an elegant young woman overpower a terrorist?" Runa decides that silence is her best option.

The adults admit their own mistakes. Captain Nakajima says that their worst error was charging straight into the airport instead of turning back at Narita Station, and the conclusion they reach is simple: "Fleeing wasn't a mistake. The problem was that there was only one path of escape." Runa, still kneeling and trembling, raises her hand like the boss she is and announces she'll buy hotels next to both airports and a safe house near Nishi-Funabashi Station. Asuka, who is somehow running the room, gives the instruction that matters: "So long as the adults do a good job guarding you, Runa-chan, you have to be sure to define their roles."

Misaki asks, half teasing, for all of them: "Do you not need us after all?" And Runa, who can't kneel any longer and has collapsed onto her hands and knees, answers with her whole heart while Hotaru pokes her calves.

"Of course I do! I'm so short on personnel! It's really bad! I'm training you guys for ten, maybe twenty years into the future!"

That line moved me more than anything else in the chapter. "Ten, maybe twenty years." The narration adds, gently, that "The game's story dictated that the young lady would meet her downfall before the girls were even adults, but that was an issue for another time." She knows that. She remembers the game. And still she's planning for a future in which she is alive and these girls are grown up and working beside her. "That's why I'll study together with you, play together with you, and when I'm in danger, you'll… Asuka-chan, don't poke my calves either!" So the roles are defined at last: at school, the associates are her aides; outside school, the adults take charge.

Her public penance is a training video in which Kitakaba Security puts her in an angel-wing harness and Aki drags her to safety. The harness sells out at her group's department stores. In the 198th online forum thread about her, the posters spell out her name one syllable per post, until someone writes "*yank*" and then: "C'mon, let's run away, my lady."

The chapter closes on a pier on the Nihonbashi River, where Tachibana shows her a boat with bulletproof glass. "A boat… I didn't think of that." "But you were the first one to use it, my lady." And she remembers: as a small child, fleeing her pursuers, she escaped by boat down this very river. Now the route is being rebuilt as part of a whole web of escapes: by river to Haneda or the heliport, by a train that splits in two halfway so that half of it becomes a decoy, by safe houses. "It's most important to have this option available, even if you don't use it. That alone is a way of preparing for the enemy." She also learns that the soldiers came to Narita partly "as a political distraction," while quietly scouting secret routes for special forces into central Tokyo, and that Tachibana has involved the Keika Group in that planning. A route west to the American air base at Yokota is judged too hard because of the railway timetables, and her railway-fan heart understands at once: "In other words, the line operators would die—or, maybe more accurately, they'd kill me."

Then she asks whether all this isn't a bit much. "Later, I'd learn that he was smiling about things I didn't know." "This country may seem superficially peaceful right now, but I believe things are very dangerous when you dig a little deeper. It resembles the time period when the government was overthrown." People who want to disrupt things, he explains, start with legal methods, and when those fail they turn to crime, and Narita has shown them that disruption is possible.

She understands. "A restoration. That word was like a curse." It's the word Japan's rebels have reached for again and again, ever since the Meiji Restoration, when the shogun was overthrown in the emperor's name, worked so well. "The powers with whom you've aligned yourself are still claiming victories and working on backing their opponents into a checkmate. At times like these, certain people who can't be controlled resort to terrorism." She thinks of the twenty-four million people in the lower classes, and how little she knows about what they feel. And then Tachibana says one more thing, with that smile: "This series of events also reminds me of the successful purge of the Northern Japanese government."

I can't read that line without a chill. Tachibana is the man who, as Volume 2 told us, pushed her father to his death. And now he's talking, with a smile, about the fall of a government as something he remembers. The book doesn't explain. It just promises, through Runa, that one day she'll learn what he was smiling about.

So the adults put on two plays in this chapter. One, in her father's drawing room, keeps her in power. The other, at the edge of a river, is a rehearsal for running away. And in between, on her knees in a room full of people who love her, a girl who knows how her story is supposed to end makes plans for twenty years from now.

## Chapter 8, part one: The bank that couldn't borrow overnight

The chapter opens in the trading room of Keika Holdings, Runa's bank, where a few hundred monitors glow and "Their faces weren't a healthy color." Someone mutters, "The call money market isn't looking too good. It's hard to lend and borrow when everyone's so nervous. This is just like what happened in '98." Then the bad news starts arriving, one company at a time.

A textile firm in Aichi files for bankruptcy protection with ¥240 billion of debt. A housebuilder in Osaka follows with ¥310 billion. A golf company is expected to go next. Ichijou, the banker who runs Keika Holdings for her, has called Runa in during a break from school, and he greets her with a piece of folk wisdom: "In this industry, they always say that companies fail on Mondays." She knows why. Banks shut at the weekend, and those two days decide whether a struggling company can find the money to open on Monday. And all of these companies have one lender in common: Gowa Osan Bank.

Runa admits she "practically wanted to cry." "I'd worked so hard to get rid of bad debts, and now new ones were popping up." When she points out that a textile company shouldn't be in property at all, Ichijou explains: "They couldn't survive in the textile business alone, so they lent out their former factory sites and got into the real estate industry…" "Yep. I get it now." She lowers her head to the table a little too fast and bangs her forehead.

To see why this matters, it helps to remember the pudding. Back in Volume 1, Ichijou taught a very young Runa what bad debt is by buying her pudding for ten thousand yen and then failing to sell it to anyone for more than a hundred. The pudding stood in for land and shares bought at bubble prices. A bad debt is what's left when someone borrowed to buy the ten-thousand-yen pudding and now owns something worth a hundred: the loan can't be repaid in full, and the bank is stuck with the difference. These Osaka and Aichi companies all bought very expensive land in the bubble, and that land is falling in value again.

What's new is the rulebook. This spring, Japan introduced what the book calls current value accounting, which means a company must record what its assets would fetch today, not what it paid for them. Under the old rules, you could keep writing "¥10,000" on the pudding's label for years. Now you have to write "¥100," and the loss appears overnight. "I'll repeat that these companies are largely victims of the current value accounting introduced this spring," Ichijou says, and he puts it more grimly still: "They really opened Pandora's box."

Gowa Osan's real danger isn't simply that it's losing money. Banks live on lending each other cash overnight, through what's called the call market. Picture neighbours who borrow a cup of sugar every evening and return it the next morning without a second thought. That works only while everyone trusts everyone. The novel has its own image for it, and it's a good one: "Money could be described as blood, and it flowed through companies and people as if they were organs, with banks serving as the hearts that kept it pumping." "Interbank markets like the call money market were the aorta," the great artery. Once the other banks start wondering whether Gowa Osan will still be standing in the morning, nobody lends it the sugar. The narration calls it "a hardening of the arteries," and a bank in that state can collapse however much it owns. Honami, another of the megabanks, may be next.

So Runa acts on instinct. "Continue making deals with Gowa Osan Bank. If you have to, you can give them a committed credit line of up to one trillion yen." A committed credit line is a promise to lend up to a fixed amount whenever the borrower asks, within agreed conditions. By offering one this large, Keika would be telling the whole market that it stands behind Gowa Osan "as a lender of last resort," the one who lends when nobody else will. It's what central banks do in a panic. It's also exactly the kind of grand rescue that got her into trouble in the first place.

While Ichijou takes a phone call, Runa glances at Mark Barnes, and the book cuts back to a conversation she had earlier that day, in the underground safe at Keika Holdings, the one room in the building that nobody can listen to.

"Don't look into his identity?! What do you mean?!"

Angela has told her not to investigate Mark. And then, with a sad face, she tells her as much of the truth about Narita as she can. The girl with the grenade was an underling who had come to collect weapons. The terrorists' real plan was to attack Runa's tower. And the gunmen at the airport warehouse? "They were there to assassinate me."

Runa freezes. Someone was willing to try to kill a woman still unofficially tied to the CIA, which means someone who doesn't mind a fight with the CIA. A chill runs down her spine, and "Tachibana placed his suit jacket over my shoulders, thinking I was cold." I love that small gesture, the old butler noticing her shiver and getting the reason wrong. "An abyss was rapidly expanding before my eyes." "It was a truly bottomless pit of darkness." And what pulls her back from its edge is the faces of the three boys, Eiichi, Yuujirou and Mitsuya, and the fun of her school days.

So she asks, very quietly, the question of a girl who wants to live. "Can I ask you something? Will I be safe if I don't know the details?"

"Yes," Angela says. The whole world now knows about the failed assassination, so anyone who attacks her will be crushed. "The problem is that if you know these details, I'm sure you'll try to go on the counterattack. That will devolve into a merciless all-out war, and the United States doesn't currently have the mental capacity to allow such a conflict."

Runa works it out. If she ever fights these people, America will back them, not her. But as long as she stays at the centre of the stage and doesn't look behind the curtain, America guarantees her life. Someone, somewhere, has made that deal for her since Narita. "They were doing this so that I could be happy. I hadn't even known it was going on."

"…I'll pretend you didn't tell me anything. Whatever happens, I won't get involved."

"Then I speak for the United States, though unofficially, when I thank you for your thoughtfulness."

I find this one of the saddest and most grown-up moments in the volume. In Chapter 3, Professor Kanbe offered her his "magic words," "Give up," and she refused them, because giving up is how the girl in the game was ruined. Here she gives up something after all: the right to know who wants her dead. "The happiness I'd had so far—and their emotions and desires—were holding me back. That was why I couldn't ignore everything and charge forward." She lets the adults protect her on this one matter so that she can keep her friends and her school days. And we, the readers, know something she has just agreed not to learn. In Chapter 6, Angela worked out that the people behind Anastasia need a Romanov heir, and the only one alive is Runa.

Back in the trading room, the phone call brings more bad news. Honami Bank has announced a ¥1.3 trillion deficit and is asking for a ¥1 trillion loan and ¥2 trillion of public money. Runa remembers with a groan how Japanese banks always take government money together, in step, so that none of them is singled out, and she suggests Keika do the same: ask for ¥500 billion of capital and ¥500 billion of public funds. "We can just return it the day we get it." Then a life insurer, Tsukihi Life, asks to join her group. The Narita attack shook the markets: the yen jumped three yen and fell back, and the Nikkei, Tokyo's main share index, which is quoted in yen, fell by about a thousand yen, a record. Under the new accounting rules, falling share prices create paper losses, so financial companies sell shares to cover them, which pushes prices down further, a spiral. And Koizumi's government did nothing at all, which the markets read as a sign that it would clear the bad debts whatever happened to share prices. His approval ratings rose. Runa can only laugh.

Should they take the insurer in? "Under the circumstances, they must be really determined if they're coming to us! What choice could we make but to help them?" Then Mark, ever the cool professional, reminds her of Holland Town, the Dutch theme park from Chapter 3, which Honami still holds ¥200 billion of debt on. Buy it, and she'd have a bargaining chip with Honami. Runa waves it off: "The price is already down to a mere two hundred billion. That's nothing to me." Mark: "Perhaps it won't affect you at all, but two hundred billion is too much for office management to take on." Everyone turns to look at Ichijou.

And here Kanbe's "fatal flaw" comes home. Ichijou, quite calmly: "You're going to offer Gowa Osan one trillion in a committed credit line, bail out Tsukihi Life Insurance, and save Honami Bank's Holland Town? These would usually be matters for the board of directors." A company isn't a dictatorship. Runa has always been able to do as she liked because the Moonlight Fund is Keika Holdings' sole shareholder, but with a coup being plotted against her, a board meeting is the last thing she wants. Would they vote to remove Ichijou? Mark: "Probably. If I were the enemy, I wouldn't let this opportunity slide." So why did he raise it? "My job is to propose things that shareholders want." Runa, privately: *Damn it. This guy's so competent.*

Angela sorts it out like the future head of the bank that she is. Announce the insurer's rescue now and do the work after Keika Holdings is listed on the stock market. Buy Holland Town through the Moonlight Fund, not through the bank. And the trillion-yen credit line? "Your committed credit line to Gowa Osan… It's not going to fly. There's no other way to say it." It would wreck the bank's listing, embarrass Takenaga, the minister in charge of the banks, and enrage Koizumi. Worse, it would look like Keika was snatching Gowa Osan away from the two megabanks circling it, Imperial Iwazaki and Futaki-Yodoyabashi, "a very vulturelike move." And Ichijou would have to take the fall for her.

Runa hesitates. Then Ichijou shows her what he has become. He calls for an emergency shareholders' meeting and a meeting with Honami's chairman, and then he gives the trading room one quiet instruction: "Instruct the trading room that, starting today, we'll temporarily take any call Gowa Osan sells us with the premium of a 1.5 percent interest rate added." In plain words, Keika will lend Gowa Osan overnight money whenever it asks, but at a price set high on purpose. It's the old Japanese polite refusal turned inside out: the price says "Unofficially, we refuse," while the door stays open. Think of a toll gate that never closes but charges a steep fee. "Ah. Ichijou has become such a wonderful banker," Runa thinks, and grumbles aloud, "Honestly, 1.5 percent sounds like a rip-off to me…" "Well, I don't want to get fired either," he jokes. It's only a stopgap. "This next week will probably determine our victory or defeat."

Then Tachibana's phone rings. The two men who run her trading company, Toudou and his deputy Tenmabashi, are on their way, with news too dangerous for a phone. While she waits, Runa says the thing I think this whole chapter is about: "I'd been able to bail out Hokkaido Kaitaku Bank so recklessly because I knew the future and had managed to hold on to leadership this entire time. I had no leadership rights this time, though, so I kept falling one step behind as I tried to resolve things."

Their news is a trap dressed as a gift. A senior official at METI (the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry) has quietly approached Toudou. The ministry plans to turn the state oil corporation into an independent agency and gather Japan's scattered oil-exploration companies around a single core, and it wants that core to be Keika's own resource division, spun off as a company. Runa sees at once what they're doing. Oil is the heart of her fortune. "How could they attack my Achilles' heel so perfectly?" And then, to herself: "Stand up, Keikain Runa. You still have your life. You haven't been beaten yet."

Tenmabashi tells her to refuse. One of the companies under the oil corporation, Nichika Oil Development, is hiding paper losses. "They're hidin' a whole lot of paper losses over there. I'd bet it's as much as two trillion yen." The room goes silent, and Runa notices Mark silently mouthing to himself, "Hunh. So that's where they put those losses…" (Remember that. The book does.)

Okazaki, her gambler, is missing from the meeting because he's in Hong Kong, investigating Gekkou Investment Firm, the shadowy fund from Chapter 1, which had a hand in this. Behind Gekkou, Toudou says: "That would be the hedge funds." Hedge funds are investment funds that bet on markets, often borrowing to bet bigger, and these ones have placed bets on both sides, so they can switch at the last moment. "They probably plan to betray them at the last minute like Kobayakawa Hideaki at Sekigahara," the general whose switch of sides decided Japan's most famous battle in 1600. "Their main bet is on oil."

What makes the trap so clever is that the bait is real. Toudou explains, with a veteran's bluntness, how the industry sees her. "Indeed. Let me put it in a more accurate way: It's only two trillion yen." Japan imports a vast amount of oil. "Japan's crude-oil imports last year totaled roughly six trillion yen. Controlling a third of that would amount to two trillion yen. Furthermore, crude-oil prices are trending upward. Two trillion yen should be recoverable within ten years." The ministry is offering to make her "the oil queen of a major Japanese oil company." And what does it get? "Isn't it obvious? Once the organization grows to that extent, they'll need people on the inside. This is a takeover of the organization." The officials would run the giant company from within, like parasites, and pay her dividends to keep the host alive. "In other words, I'd become like the Keika Holdings of long ago?" That's the bank before Runa, a company the Ministry of Finance treated as its own territory. Toudou's image is perfect: "since the adults would basically be taking your toy, they'd be paying you back an allowance as compensation."

There's another pressure behind it. Group accounts that consolidate every company together are becoming the rule, and a giant fund with no legal home, like the Moonlight Fund, won't survive that for long. That's why Tenmabashi wanted the fund brought under head office in the first place. So she does need to reorganise. The offer just reorganises her out of power.

Then she finds the flaw, and slams the table. "That's it!" "I'm the face of the Narita Airport incident, so it would look like I'd struck some deal behind the scenes. Why is this plan still in motion despite that?" The answer is that it can't be stopped. "Once the world's second-biggest economic superpower got into a large-scale speculative battle, it was impossible to put a stop to it." Too much money is already committed, so the plan rolls on even though the people behind it can no longer get rid of her the way they meant to.

And then the phone rings once more. It's Izumikawa, the deputy prime minister: "He says that Prime Minister Koizumi has decided to reshuffle the cabinet, and Nagata-cho is buzzing like he poked a hornet's nest." A reshuffle in the middle of a parliamentary session, while the opposition is attacking him over Narita, can mean only one thing. "It sounds like the prime minister is eager to put up a fight." He will tighten his inner circle and call an election.

"How stupid…"

Runa smiles, sits down, and sips her grape juice as if everything were solved. Ichijou and Tachibana stare at her. She's sure now. "They'd messed up big time. They made an enemy out of Prime Minister Koizumi, a force to be reckoned with in the political world. He was the prime minister who'd been blessed by demons." She spent all of Volume 4 learning, painfully, what it costs to fight that man. Her enemies have just picked a fight with him, thinking they can win. She'll be "watching eagerly from the sidelines," she sings, and then she lays a curse on them, one they'll never hear.

"Go on. Antagonize that prime minister. The world and fate itself will attack you as the enemy."

That's the turn of the whole volume. A girl who can no longer win by force, because she has no legal right to command her own empire, discovers that she doesn't have to win at all. She just has to step aside and let the most dangerous man in Japan do it for her.

**Terms in this piece**
- **Keika Holdings**: Runa's bank and financial group.
- **Bankruptcy protection** (Corporate Reorganization Act / Civil Rehabilitation Law): Japan's two main court procedures for restructuring a failing company.
- **Bad debt**: a loan the borrower probably can't repay in full.
- **Current value accounting**: recording assets at today's market value rather than their purchase price (mark-to-market).
- **Pandora's box**: from Greek myth, a box that releases every evil once it's opened.
- **Call market**: where banks lend each other spare cash for very short periods, usually overnight.
- **Megabank**: one of Japan's giant banks formed by mergers.
- **Committed credit line**: a promise to lend up to a fixed amount on demand, within agreed conditions.
- **Lender of last resort**: the lender that steps in when no one else will, usually a central bank.
- **CIA**: the Central Intelligence Agency, America's foreign intelligence service.
- **Public funds injection**: the government putting money into a bank in return for shares.
- **Nikkei**: the Nikkei 225, Tokyo's main share index.
- **Paper losses**: losses on assets that have fallen in value but haven't been sold.
- **Life insurer / mutual company**: many Japanese life insurers are owned by their policyholders, so they must convert into share companies to take outside capital.
- **Listing**: selling a company's shares on the stock market for the first time.
- **Board of directors / sole shareholder**: the board makes a company's major decisions; a sole shareholder owns every share.
- **Moonlight Fund**: Runa's private investment fund, which has no legal existence of its own.
- **Penalty rate**: an interest rate set high on purpose, so it's used only in emergencies.
- **Hokkaido Kaitaku Bank**: the failing bank Runa rescued in Volume 1.
- **METI**: the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry.
- **Independent administrative corporation**: a semi-independent Japanese government agency.
- **Hedge fund**: an investment fund that makes large, often borrowed bets on markets.
- **Sekigahara / Kobayakawa Hideaki**: the battle of 1600 that decided who would rule Japan; Kobayakawa's defection won it.
- **Dividends**: profits a company pays out to its shareholders.
- **Consolidated accounts**: financial statements that combine a parent company and all its subsidiaries.
- **Cabinet reshuffle**: a prime minister replacing ministers.
- **Nagata-cho**: Tokyo's government district, used to mean Japanese politics.

## Chapter 8, part two: Harassment

The second half of the chapter begins with her own house being put in order, because "You couldn't accomplish anything unless your organization was in order internally." Her trading company is about to become Keika Corp Holdings, a merger of four old firms, and its vice-president, Tenmabashi, the sly old trader from the Osaka office, has drawn up the list of executives. Runa reads it with a slightly frozen smile. He's pleased with himself: "Since Keika's grown so quickly, we're short on hands. It was easy to keep up with the game of musical chairs."

His plan is a long one. Toudou, the president, will one day become chairman and keep the real power. Tenmabashi will serve as president under him. And in about ten years, Okazaki, Runa's young gambler and oil man, will take over. Her resource division will be spun off and merged with Gulf Oil Development, the oil company Okazaki already runs. Read that again and you'll see the point. The METI official in part one wanted to take her oil business and build a national champion around it. Instead, Tenmabashi has given her oil business, the heart of the Moonlight Fund, a home inside her own group, which "would solve the problem of the Moonlight Fund not being connected to an organization." Keika also lets it be known that it will consider rescuing Nichika, as a favour to the ministry, but only after Nichika admits its hidden losses in public.

Then comes a party, to celebrate the Choufuu Council, the meeting of her group's company presidents, becoming a proper foundation, an independent legal body that can hold money and shares. Her father, the duke, becomes its board chairman, which silences the family. It was Tachibana's idea after Narita, and Runa admits she can't control companies this size alone, so "his suggestion came as a lifeboat that I decided to use." The foundation is given 10 percent of Keika Holdings and 20 percent of her pharmaceutical company. In plain words, she has begun to share control of her empire and to tie it to her family: the first real answer to the "fatal flaw" Kanbe pointed out in Chapter 3.

Just before they walk in, Tenmabashi asks her what she's really after. Why make everything so stable? "Good question. If I had to put it in one word, I suppose I'd call my goal 'harassment.'"

That word is the key to the rest of the chapter, so let me explain it the way she does later: "The best part of harassment was that you didn't have to obsess over winning; harassment was all about tripping someone up." She has stopped trying to beat her enemies head on. She's going to put things in their way and let them fall.

The first trip-wire is laid in a family restaurant. Runa and Yuka, who supervises her associates, sit down with Mitsuya and his father, Gotou Mitsutoshi, a senior official in the budget bureau of the Ministry of Finance (the MOF). Mitsuya is alarmed when she starts talking about the coup out loud, so she lets him in on a secret: "the guests seated at all four tables around us are my bodyguards. Even the waitress is a maid of mine who's been outsourced." Father and son have ordered the same steak; she orders it too, and then, when Yuka, who has been dieting, protests and her stomach growls, a second one. And then Runa tells them everything. Mitsuya looks astonished. His father eats his steak without changing his expression at all.

He knows something already. "I know the international bureau is worried about money exchanges. They say the price fluctuations are obviously unusual." The international bureau is the part of the ministry that steps into the currency markets to defend the yen. Here's the danger. The yen has been rising against the dollar, and speculators expect it to reach ¥100. A rising yen sounds like good news, but for a country that lives by exporting cars and electronics, it's poison: every dollar of sales abroad brings home fewer yen. If a coup in the Keika Group threw the government into turmoil, it couldn't defend the currency, the yen might reach ¥80 or ¥90, and Japanese industry would suffer badly.

"So, now that you've told me this, what is it you want from me?"

"Nothing."

I love that answer. She knows that an official's first duty is to defend his own ministry, and that if he ignores this, the MOF will lose face. She doesn't need to ask for anything; he will carry the story to the international bureau himself, at the best price he can get for it. She treats it as a lesson for Mitsuya in how adults do business. Then she lightens the mood with a joke about marriage meetings, and his father laughs: "Ha ha ha! I bet having a woman like you marry my Mitsuya would be really stressful." "Dad." Runa stores Mitsuya's embarrassment away "in the photo album of my heart."

Gotou warns her that only the Minister of Finance can order an intervention, and that Nagata-cho, the government district, is in chaos over the reshuffle. "That's all right. I'll have my own fun until then." A gamble, she reasons, needs money on both sides. If the foreign hedge funds have bet on the yen, she will bet on the dollar. Mitsuya is alarmed: "Hang on, Keikain. Even you could get burned if you do that." "What do you mean? When Furukawa was in trouble, I moved four hundred billion dollars. This is nothing compared to that." Three people go completely silent.

In the car, Okazaki is waiting, delighted. "If you're having fun, that's all that matters, my lady. How much do you need to gather?" "I suppose I'll aim for a trillion." Yuka asks, "A trillion yen?" And Runa, smiling more widely than she knew she could, corrects her.

"No. Dollars. I'm going to gather a trillion dollars."

Here's how it works, simply put. She won't have a trillion dollars of her own. She'll use leverage, borrowing so that a small amount of her own money controls a much bigger bet. Think of putting down a deposit on a house: with a deposit of one, you control a house worth twenty. She settles on putting in fifty billion dollars of the Moonlight Fund's own money, which earns mostly in dollars from American tech and Russian oil, borrowed up twenty times over. And then, because she remembers her past life, she can't help adding the uncomfortable thought: "suffering massive losses on leveraged money and fatally wounding all the parties involved was basically what had caused the 2008 financial crisis…" She clears her throat and moves on. I didn't.

The other half of the plan was laid days earlier, when the American president phoned to check on her after Narita. "I may be a child, but I hope to fight alongside you," she told him, and then, sweetly: "The United States plans to fund the war efforts by issuing bonds, right? Please allow me to take them all on." Bonds are IOUs, written promises to repay ("I owe you"), and America pays for its war by selling them. Buying them means lending dollars to the American government, and holding dollars. Angela, who had feared exactly this, put her hand to her forehead and stared at the ceiling. "I told her not to do this."

Next comes a performance in the cafeteria at the ruling party's headquarters. "I'm here to hang out!" The press is everywhere. The cafeteria is famous for its curry, and at election time orders of katsu curry shoot up, because *katsu*, the breaded cutlet, sounds like the Japanese word for "win." Izumikawa, the deputy prime minister, sits down beside her, and when he says "I'll have the katsu curry," a ripple goes through the reporters. As they eat, she says, "I hope you'll see this as no more than a child's joke, but I plan to support you as much as I can." In front of the cameras, she has just promised his faction money for the election, even if the party cuts it loose. She has no intention of splitting the party. But she needs everyone to see that she could.

And then Koizumi walks in. He has cancelled most of his day to be there. In a room full of people who know about the tension between them, and in front of the TV cameras that have slipped in, he bows to her. "Sorry for putting you in danger, Keikain-kun." She holds out her hand: "Please lift your head, Prime Minister. I'm a citizen of this country, and I respect the will of the people." The flashes go off. And then, still holding her hand, he turns to Izumikawa and asks him, on camera, to stay on as his deputy "as the fate of the nation is determined this fall."

Runa shudders. If Izumikawa says no, it will look as if she's plotting to bring down the prime minister. If he says yes, he's tied to Koizumi's cabinet. And Koizumi has done it without even speaking to her, because this time he's dealing with the adult. Izumikawa answers like the old pro he is: "I'm someone who's already reached the place he wants to be. I prefer enjoying the katsu curry here over getting into boring topics like political realignments. If I left the party, I wouldn't even get to eat it anymore." The two men shake hands with her hand still in Koizumi's, and the party's greatest fear, a split before the election, vanishes in a burst of camera flashes. Her verdict on Koizumi is admiration laced with fear: "This man really was so impressive. We were staging a crummy play in his own backyard, and he'd still jumped in to steal the leading role. Of course, that was exactly why I feared getting into a battle with him."

Now the real trap. At the main Keikain house she meets Iwazaki Yashirou, the president of Imperial Iwazaki Bank, whom she calls "Uncle," and who has come, he says, "to see my grandson," by which he means her baby nephew, his granddaughter's son. "Would you care to buy Gowa Osan Bank?"

Remember the overnight lending at a penalty rate from part one? Gowa Osan can't afford the extra 1.5 percent, but it has no choice but to keep borrowing from Keika anyway. And now the Financial Services Agency (the FSA, the government office that inspects banks) has caught it making its bad loans look smaller than they are. The hole is about ¥200 billion, and the bank is frantically hunting for a partner. Runa offers to sell Iwazaki the ¥200 billion of unsecured loans Keika has made to Gowa Osan. It's a wonderful old proverb: "Throw in a shrimp, and fish out a sea bream." Gowa Osan is the shrimp. Imperial Iwazaki is the bream.

"Well, I'm not actually going to do that," she sings. A good negotiator names an unreasonable price first. Then she shows him what she really came to show him: the documents and secret account records of Nichika Oil Development. Nichika's main bank is Karafuto Bank, but Imperial Iwazaki has been quietly guaranteeing Nichika's loans for years, and Nichika has been hiding its losses from Iwazaki too. If this explodes, Iwazaki goes down with it.

Unless he takes Gowa Osan into the family. If Imperial Iwazaki absorbs the struggling bank in a rescue merger, with the stronger bank swallowing the weaker, it can use the Keika Rules. That's the clean-up procedure Runa invented back in Volume 1: fire the old managers, wipe out the old shareholders, and pass the worst loans to the Resolution and Collection Corporation (the RCC, a government company that buys up bad loans and collects what it can on them). Think of it as a disposal service for spoiled pudding, and since Japan runs on precedent, any bank may now use it. Imperial Iwazaki can rescue Gowa Osan and, in the same move, send its own Karafuto-linked bad debts to the RCC. Runa will fund any shortfall. Iwazaki starts wiping his forehead. "I see. There won't be a problem if we use the Keika Rules." "All right. I'll take the deal."

Then she tells us what she's really done. Once Nichika's bad debts go to the RCC, its executives will have to resign, and any crimes will be prosecuted. "I was having a third party—one that couldn't escape that mess—open Pandora's box. This was the ultimate harassment in my power." Iwazaki asks about Honami, and she answers, "This is my second time in this situation, so I've learned some lessons," with what she means to be a sweet smile. In the mirror she sees Yuka flinch. *Huh? That wasn't the smile I was going for at all…*

The headlines come one after another. Nichika files for bankruptcy protection with ¥2 trillion of debt. Karafuto Bank books a ¥2 trillion loss, and its debt reaches ¥3 trillion. Gowa Osan announces a rescue merger with Imperial Iwazaki under the Keika Rules, and the FSA doesn't object. Police and prosecutors raid Karafuto Bank, the Karafuto Reconstruction Agency and the prefectural government. "The Russian mafia's main money-laundering system on Wall Street has been exposed!", with payments traced to Karafuto officials and to opposition members of parliament. The box is open, and she never laid a finger on it.

That leaves Honami, and for that she goes to Eiichi. In the school library, with her associates guarding the doors, the two of them sit like ordinary students doing homework. "Can you help me? I don't get this part," she says aloud, while her notebook says: *Would you guys give Honami Bank a trillion yen?* They talk about homework, and write about banks, because the room might be bugged. He writes back asking why her group doesn't do it itself, and she explains that her group has its hands full with Karafuto, and then: *I don't care if we have to lend you our name. And we'll pay the entire trillion if you want.* All Teia has to lend is its name. And it would gain a great deal: whoever rescues Honami will be owed a favour by the bank and its many business connections. The Teia group has just broken away from the bigger Futaki group and has no main bank of its own, and a bank where industry holds the most power, with the chance of an insurance arm besides, is exactly what a car company needs. Then they look up from the paper, and she says it out loud. "But I want to solve this problem. Please help me." "…I'm not gonna say no, obviously." He looks away first. The next morning: Teia Motor takes ¥1 trillion of new shares in Honami Bank, which joins the Teia Group.

Later, she asks him when to send the money, and he says not to bother. "Don't bother. I got in a big fight with my grandpa and dad and decided to take care of the money myself. If I can't handle something like that, how am I ever supposed to surpass you?" She thinks he's never sounded cooler, until he adds, "Now I can say 'I'm the man who shouldered a trillion yen' when I propose to you someday."

"You say that, but I've shouldered many trillions of yen before…"

She means it as a joke. But his face twitches, and you can see him understand, for the first time, what that much money weighs: the lives and hopes that hang from every yen. His smile goes crooked, and it stings her a little. Then he says, softly:

"Ah… Have you been carrying this burden around all this time, even while you've had a smile on your face?"

"…Dummy."

She pulls down her eyelid, sticks her tongue out at him, and leaves, not in the mood, she says, to eat lunch with him. I think that's one of the loveliest beats in the series. Almost everybody in her life admires her money or fears it. He may be the first person her own age to notice that it's heavy, and that she has been smiling under it all this time.

Meanwhile, the world catches fire. Karafuto Bank's president and the vice-governor are arrested, and the governor resigns. The scandal lands squarely on the opposition, and the fuss over the troops at Narita fades away. On Wall Street, the speculators' bases are in chaos. They can't admit they're in a currency war, the humans who should be cutting their losses are too frazzled to do it, and the computers just keep buying yen, until the yen reaches ¥108 to the dollar and an analyst wails, "This chart looks like an EKG during a heart attack." Then Koizumi dissolves parliament and calls a general election, and the rate moves to ¥110. In his new cabinet, Izumikawa stays, as deputy prime minister, foreign minister and minister for crisis management, and the opposition's plan to lure him away and make him prime minister collapses.

The chapter ends, as Runa's chapters love to, on an internet forum on election night, where one poster called lunakeikain opens the thread: "Except they failed." with a pointing emoticon and "Excellent!!" The ruling coalition wins a landslide; the opposition's strongholds of Karafuto, Hokkaido, Aichi and the cities collapse over the Nichika affair; and lunakeikain happily explains the history of the Japanese left to anyone who'll listen. To a poster from the share-trading boards who bet against Japan and fears he's ruined, she offers some advice: "It's best not to go near the baldies. Unless you want to end up skewered." ("Baldies" is slang for vultures.) And the next morning: "The ruling coalition party's landslide victory has sent the dollar flying as of this morning!!" "Can you believe it lost two whole yen…?"

So this is what harassment looks like. She takes no bank, makes no grand rescue, and never steps into Koizumi's line of fire. She hands one rival bank a deal and asks one boy a favour, eats a plate of curry for the cameras, and quietly makes a trillion-dollar bet. Then other people carry everything: Iwazaki, Teia, Koizumi, the markets. It's the Karin lesson from Chapter 3, leave paths open for others, and the Kanbe lesson, don't take responsibility for everything, turned into a strategy. It's also, I think, the first time we've seen her win by doing as little as possible herself.

## Chapter 9: Wastefulness

"WHEN PEOPLE MADE EASY MONEY, they liked to spend it quickly. I decided to do exactly that with mine."

That's how the chapter opens, and for a while it's the funniest shopping trip in the series. Runa walks into Karin's office at Keika Electronics Union and announces, "I'm going to buy the PC company IBeM." Karin glares. "Can you at least tell me you're considering these things before you make up your mind…?" Angela has already priced it quietly at $1.3 billion, and Karin admits it isn't a bad buy. But she has a complaint first. "My lady, haven't you purchased companies and then completely ignored them? Like that game company." Runa had completely forgotten. The game company, a former console maker she bought into in Volume 5, has been crowing "Hyah ha! Now we get to make hardware again!" and Karin wants it handed over to her before it does something foolish, because it's the one company in the group that's actually doing well. Then she brings up the Russian satellite company Runa bought with Okazaki for ¥200 billion. Runa can't bring herself to admit that she bought it because "it reminded me of a video game villain." Karin: "My lady, money wastage is the enemy of any manager!"

The phone keeps ringing. Angela: America's tech industry, which has lived on Wall Street money since the dot-com bubble burst, has been "skewered" by the currency swings, and since "a sucker had come along ready to be parted from her money," everyone wants to sell to her. Would she like the company Karin used to work for? Karin: "Their patents are very appealing." And the best parts of M-Rose, a famous American company whose founding family stepped back "after the failure of iridium cell phones"? Yes to both. It will win goodwill in Washington and light a fire under the slow, old-fashioned Japanese side of her electronics business. Then Okazaki calls about the merger of two role-playing-game developers she'd promised to fund, between them weighed down by a troubled publishing department, a debt-ridden convenience-store retail arm and a failed film adaptation, on the sole condition that "they kept publishing their games and comics." Karin takes the phone away from her, smiling and furious. The bill for Keika Electronics Union comes to twenty billion dollars, and Karin lectures her late into the night.

Next, her railway company. "I'm going to build a second Seikan Tunnel!" she tells its new president, Mikihara, hoping to rile him. "Is that so?" He's unflappable. Under the joke there's a real plan. When a new bullet-train line, a Shinkansen, opens, the old local line running beside it is usually handed to a cash-strapped local company, and rural towns lose their through trains. She wants Keika Railway to run trains over those lines all over the country, and she says the thing that I think explains this whole chapter: "Rural areas can't support railroads on their own, after all. You have to take the money you earn in cities and send it back to rural areas, or else they'll get desolate quick." She'll revive an abandoned mountain line for sightseeing and ski trains, and pay to prepare stations at Aomori and Hakodate that the new Hokkaido Shinkansen will pass by. And the tunnel? "It's only four hundred billion yen, right?"

Then comes Obon, the summer festival of the dead, and she goes north to visit her parents' graves in Sakata. The weather grounds her plane, so she takes the Yamagata Shinkansen, packed with holiday travellers. Tachibana chats about railways and mentions, carefully, that "There's a spot up ahead that you'll want to waste your money on, my lady." On the way home, at Fukushima Station, the train stops. Fukushima is where the Yamagata trains join and leave the main line, and when the timetable slips, everything jams.

*We're not moving.*
*We're not moving.*
*We're. Not. Moving.*

She keeps her public smile on while shredding napkins under the table. Then one of her guards whispers to her, crestfallen: "My lady! The vending machines and lunch box shops are all completely sold out!"

*Snap.*

She signs the paperwork for a rebuilt Fukushima Station the next day, and throws in a new mountain tunnel for good measure. Keika Railway's wastefulness: ¥1.2 trillion.

And then Sakata, where the chapter softens. People from Sakata rarely take the Yamagata line; they go by Niigata, where the connections are poor. So she buys a retiring train, an old 485-series express, to keep at Niigata for her trips home, and rents it out when she isn't using it. Railway fans turn up in crowds to photograph "the young lady's train." There's also a petition: the empty department-store site in front of Sakata Station, on land that once belonged to Far Eastern Bank, the little regional bank where her empire began, should become a twelve-storey Keika building. Ichijou, who started his career at that bank, sighs over it. "In a way, Sakata was the current Keika empire's birthplace. You must be irritated by how people call Hokkaido Keika's fortress." Tachibana says gently, "I don't think it'd be a bad thing to have a rural area you can return to every now and then." Ichijou adds that in the old days, they'd say this let you "return home in triumph." She approves it, with conditions: a covered footbridge to the station, a supermarket instead of a department store, since big new stores by stations have been stealing customers from the rest of these towns, and free underground parking. "People from rural areas couldn't go anywhere that didn't have parking, after all."

I love how practical her tenderness is. Under the joke about wasting money there's a girl who has learned from two lives how small towns die, and who knows that what saves them is a car park and a train that runs on time.

For the ground-breaking ceremony, the four people who built the Keika Group, Runa, Tachibana, Ichijou and Toudou, travel north together, against Eva's protests. On the train the men discuss a gas pipeline the trade ministry is quietly planning from Karafuto down through Hokkaido and Tohoku, "shutting out the Ministry of Foreign Affairs." And at the ceremony, while the seating plan collapses into a polite squabble over rank, Runa finds herself sitting next to Representative Katou.

Katou is the politician from Sakata whose ambitions have run through the whole series, the reformer whose revolt failed, who resigned over his secretary's tax scandal and came back as an independent. He's back in the ruling party now, on what the narration calls "a sort of apology tour." His eyes have lost some of their life. He tells her that the Foreign Ministry is still tearing itself apart over a secret-funds scandal, and that the prime minister's office handles most diplomacy now. Then he asks her, suddenly:

"Keikain-kun, do you know what I did wrong?"

She smiles, a little sadly. "You weren't wrong. You were just ahead of your time."

"Thank you, Keikain-kun." And he bows to her, and the bow, she notices, is "much weaker and lonelier" than the one he gave her when they first met.

I had to stop reading for a moment there. Remember what her brother told her in Chapter 3: her father died to protect Katou, who was then the government's chief spokesman. And here, in the town where her parents are buried, his daughter gives the man he died for the kindest epitaph a politician could ask for. The book doesn't underline it. It just lets the old man bow, and the guests file out of the tent. Outside, passers-by are already calling the new building "Keika Palace."

Back in Tokyo, the spending goes on. At Narita, Okazaki shows her a private Boeing 747, a "bonus" that came with the cargo planes she bought for her delivery company, sold off cheap because "the previous owner went bankrupt during the currency speculation." "It's huge…!" "Of course it's huge. It's a 747, after all." She has the upper deck to herself; the rest will carry cargo. And she thinks, half joking, that with this plane, "I wouldn't have to flee to Kansai International Airport when my downfall came." That's a private wound. In the game, there was a single image from after the villainess's downfall: a girl standing alone in the departure lounge at Kansai airport, with one bag. And when she asks how bad an escape they're planning for, Okazaki answers with a teasing smile: "We're basing our efforts off the worst case of all time, obviously: the Second February 26 Incident."

Then she watches the Moonlight Fund being dismantled. Its office in her tower is being cleared out. Now that the new consolidated accounts demand it, the fund is being given a legal shape at last: it will become Keika Resource Development, merged with the wreck of Nichika Oil, as part of her trading company. "We didn't even get to keep half of them…" she murmurs, looking at the empty desks. Okazaki: "You should be happy that we held on to as much as a third." The fund became so famous during the currency battle that rival recruiters went after its staff, offering even junior traders a hundred million yen and targeting their families, "the outer moats." A third left; a third went back to their old jobs elsewhere in the group. Okazaki himself was offered "a measly hundred million dollars per year." "Since they failed to recruit you—the one they really wanted—this was nothing more than harassment, my lady." Her own word, handed back to her.

Now, finally, the book tells us how the currency battle was won, so let me take it step by step.

Step one was a lure. With the yen at about 120 to the dollar, Keika quietly bought yen, about twenty billion dollars' worth, pushing it up toward 110, and made sure the market knew it was Keika doing the buying. Everyone drew the same conclusion: "Keika is pissed with the government over its response to the terrorism, and now they're coming for revenge." The foreign hedge funds, who wanted exactly that fight, piled in and bought yen too. Then Koizumi bowed to her on television, the ruling party didn't split, and the Japanese investors quietly got out. The foreign funds were left holding a huge bet on a rising yen, with nobody on the other side.

Step two was the trap. To bet on a rising yen, the funds had borrowed dollars and sold them. Think of borrowing a friend's concert ticket and selling it for ¥10,000, expecting the price to drop, so you can buy one back cheaply, return it and keep the difference. That's short selling. But if the price goes up instead, you have to buy the ticket back for more than you sold it for, and you're paying your friend a fee every day you keep him waiting. The morning after the election, the yen fell two yen. The next day it fell three more, and the day after that, five. Runa had bought dollars on a colossal scale, in the name of funding America's war, and Okazaki had spread the rumour that Japan was buying a trillion dollars of American bonds. Then the Bank of Japan joined in with its own buying, "a trillion yen per day," and though the funds desperately bought yen, the bank kept buying dollars faster than they could keep up. "They're not like the Bank of England after all," Okazaki says, thinking of the bank that speculators broke in 1992. Wall Street, tangled up in the Karafuto Bank laundering scandal, couldn't help them. The president said nothing.

Her share, she tells us airily, was only a 5 percent fee on the deal, which comes to fifty billion dollars. "Or, in yen…I think it's up to 120 now. That makes six trillion yen. That sum more than covered any of my wasteful ventures." She and Okazaki marvel that this time, with Angela running Wall Street and Washington for her, nobody had to apologise and nobody had to take a hit.

And then comes the sentence I've thought about more than any other in this volume. "Angela also told me that she'd decided to suggest the vultures look into subprime loans while they were still trying to stop the bleeding. All the preparations for the next collapse were in order." Subprime loans are American home loans made to borrowers with poor credit, and they will be the fuse of the crash of 2008. In Volume 5, Runa refused to profit from that crash: "I won't be the one who pulls that trigger." Here she simply notes, without comment, that the vultures have been pointed at it.

She asks about Gekkou, the fund that started all this. It turns out to be backed by a sovereign wealth fund (an SWF, an investment fund owned by a government) in communist China. Okazaki's guess is that it was built on the old Northern Japanese government's secret money, left behind in Hong Kong. She thinks of the Chinese mafia's drugs and the Russian mafia's guns at Narita, and "decided to forget Gekkou Investment Firm for the time being." The real winners, the ones who made a fortune and ran, were European and American state funds. "Or, rather, you're the one who copied them."

And when she thinks about what happens when state-backed funds like these chase extreme returns, she asks, almost to herself: "…What awaited them…was a big crash?" "I felt like Scipio watching Carthage burn." The reference is to the Roman general who destroyed Carthage in 146 BC and, watching it burn, wondered aloud whether Rome would be next. The ancient historians say he wept.

Okazaki tries to cheer her up. Crashes come and go, he says; the losers rise and the winners fall. "First there was the Great Depression, then Black Monday. You're the winner right now, my lady."

"That's right. I won. I'm still winning."

She smiles, and swallows the rest. And then, in her head, some of the most frightened words she has thought in six volumes:

"I'm going to lose someday… God, if you exist, I only ask that you help me through the battle of 2008…"

I find that unbearably moving after a chapter of jokes. She has just made six trillion yen. She owns a jumbo jet and a new building on Sakata's station square. And all of it is, to her, only a stockpile for the one battle she knows is coming, the one her past life lived through, the one the series opened with on its very first page, dated 15 September 2008. The chapter's last lines, told in the voice of history, say the war is only beginning: "A new war fought with money, not with blood, was on the verge of breaking out."

So "wastefulness" turns out to be the wrong word, I think. Most of her extravagances are something given back: a railway line kept alive, a station unjammed, a town with somewhere to park, an old man allowed to bow with dignity. The only thing she is truly hoarding is courage, for 2008.

## Chapter 10: Somewhere in Europe

The last chapter leaves Japan behind completely. There's no Runa in it, no Tachibana, no grape juice. There's only a telephone line between two windows. One looks out over the European Central Bank in Frankfurt, which runs the new euro. The other looks out at London's usual grey sky from Canary Wharf, the city's second financial district. The narration calls the person in Frankfurt the instigator of everything we've just read. The person in London is a broker, a dealmaker who takes a fee for bringing buyers and sellers together. The book never gives either of them a name.

They begin with the people they bought. "So? Can we use any of the people we took?" These are the Moonlight Fund's traders from Chapter 9, lured away with fortunes. "Of course not. They're from the Far East and don't know anything about the latest methods of financial engineering." They're worth something only for what they know about the inside, and "we can just keep them on the payroll until we abandon them." I felt a little sick at that. A third of the people Runa trained and trusted, and to these two they're the opening act, already disposable.

Then the instigator explains what the whole affair was for. "We knew how things in Iraq would turn out, to some extent, but it's just the first game that will determine hegemony in this century. We're not just going to let those in the New World act all cocky forever." Hegemony means dominance, the power to set the rules for everyone else, and the "New World" is America. The euro, they agree, will let Europe match America's economy. Military power is another matter. So Europe needs Russia.

"That's why we need to get Russia on our side. Once they're part of Europe, we'll be able to rival the United States on equal terms. Europe lost the most in both World Wars, so this time, we have to be the winners of the Great Game."

The Great Game was the nineteenth-century contest between Britain and Russia for Central Asia, and the phrase has come to mean any great-power struggle for the world. And the piece they need to win it is a girl in Tokyo. The broker, who believes in talent and money and not much in bloodlines, can't quite hide the sneer: "And this young lady, Keikain Runa, is the secret weapon that'll bring Russia in?"

The answer is the most chilling line in the volume.

"She probably won't ever rule Russia, but if she goes back there as empress consort, this dream of ours will come to fruition. Of course, her husband will be chosen from blue bloods like us."

An empress consort is an emperor's wife. So the plan is a marriage: Runa, the Romanov heir, returned to Russia beside a European aristocrat, pulling Russia into Europe's camp. And they aren't the only ones. Britain turned down their proposal to side with America, and the broker warns that "There's evidence that the United Kingdom wants to secure the girl for themselves through royal diplomacy too," presumably with the Americans, who have no royals of their own to offer. "Whichever country the girl marries into, it'll probably change this century's world map. Aristocrats from various nations are after her."

They admire her, in their way. If Koizumi hadn't taken over her Iraq plans in Volume 4, they say, "it wouldn't have been impossible for her to establish an independent country as a tax haven on the Gulf," a whole country where money could hide from taxes. They talk about Iraq breaking apart, with Turkey in the north, Iran's army in the south and Saddam's party gone underground, and how they mean to drag America down there to weaken its grip on the region. And then the instigator says the line I think the author wants us to remember.

"That way, the girl will turn her back on the United States. History wants her to go back to Russia."

"You mean we'll pave that path with gold bars and shower her with flower petals in the form of paper bills?"

"Of course. Money dirtied by blood and greed is made clean through the blue bloods. Her talent, and her own blue blood, may mean she'll have to choose herself as that cleaning mechanism someday."

Do you remember the washing machine? In Chapter 1, the CIA complained that a certain young lady "broke the washing machine very publicly" on Wall Street. In Chapter 6, we learned that Anastasia was a laundering account that needed a Romanov heir to make it legal. And now, at the very end, Europe's financiers say it outright: the ultimate washing machine is Runa herself. Her blood is what turns dirty money into an inheritance, a dowry, a dynasty. In the game, the villainess was destroyed by money laundering through Karafuto Bank. Here, the people who want to use her are planning the same thing on the scale of a continent, and calling it a wedding.

They talk about Hong Kong, and China's old saying that it doesn't matter whether a cat is black or white, so long as it catches mice. "Aren't we also trying to create a new Hong Kong when we obtain the girl?" A new Hong Kong, a rich, tax-free territory between empires, run for profit. "Money is still money, even if it's stained with blood." Neither trusts the other. The instigator has already used their own noble connections to lay the groundwork, and the broker, we're told, is quietly reporting everything to British intelligence.

The narration remembers a Japanese prime minister of 1939, Hiranuma, who resigned when Germany signed its pact with the Soviet Union, saying that "the state of Europe is complicated and mysterious." Borders change, the book says, but Europe never does. And the two of them end their call with a joke that isn't one.

"Lord, forgive us for our sins. I promise I'll buy as many indulgences as you ask."

"Are you sure you should say that? Indulgences started a war, so I suppose we're no different."

Indulgences were pardons for sins that the medieval Church sold for money, and the scandal over them set off the Protestant Reformation and a century of religious wars. It's the perfect last image: two people who know exactly what they're doing, and assume they can pay their way out of it.

What stays with me is how this chapter answers Kanbe. In Chapter 3, the professor told Runa that she was a child, so she could choose the people at her side, and that it was "not for this world, or for history, to decide for you." In Frankfurt, a voice says that "History wants her to go back to Russia." The volume ends with those two sentences facing each other across a continent. One is a promise made to a girl. The other is a plan for her. And the girl herself, as far as she knows, has just won.

## Afterwards: What Volume 6 says Runa is

Every volume of this series is quietly trying to answer one question: what is Keikain Runa? Volume 5 asked who she is once you take her role away. Volume 6 asks something colder. What is she *for*? Almost everyone in this book has a use in mind for her, and almost none of them is asking what she wants.

Start with the plans. To a laundering account in a Swiss bank, she is collateral: "the young lady with the Romanov family's blood in her veins," the one living heir whose existence would turn a criminal fiction called Anastasia into a lawful inheritance. To the trade ministry she is a prize to be bought off, "the oil queen of a major Japanese oil company," paid an allowance while officials run her empire from inside. To the extremists at Narita she is "the noblewoman who incited the war." To Washington and Tokyo she is "both a victim and a heroine of the war on terror," too useful to be allowed to step back. To the markets she is a signal, so that when Keika buys yen, the whole world assumes it's revenge. And to a voice in Frankfurt she is "the secret weapon that'll bring Russia in," a future empress consort with a husband chosen for her. Every one of them wants her blood, her money or her face. Not one of them wants her judgement.

Notice, too, what she lacks. Kanbe pointed it out in Chapter 3, and it frightened me a little: the girl who controls trillions of yen holds no real title anywhere. The Moonlight Fund doesn't legally exist. She can't stop a coup by decree, because she has no legal right to command her own companies. In this volume, for the first time, her power turns out to be mostly other people's trust in her. And when that trust is under attack, she can't fight the way she used to.

So she doesn't. And that, I think, is the volume's real answer. Look at what she gives away. The Choufuu Council goes to a foundation chaired by her father. Her oil business goes to Okazaki's company. Her associates get defined jobs at school, and the adults get the rest. She leaves the glory of the currency battle to the Bank of Japan, the rescue of Gowa Osan to Iwazaki, and Honami to Eiichi's family. Even her enemies she leaves to Koizumi. Each concession is small. Together they amount to the thing every adult in this book has been trying to tell her: that an adult isn't someone who decides everything, but someone who takes responsibility within a role and leaves room for others. Karin said it best: "People like Angela and me are providing one of the routes you can take in life."

There's one gift the adults offer her that she refuses, and I understand why. Kanbe's "magic words," "Give up," would mean accepting the ending she remembers. But there's one she accepts, and it's the one that moved me most. In the soundproof safe, with Tachibana's jacket around her shoulders, she lets the Americans keep from her the name of whoever wants her dead. "They were doing this so that I could be happy." She lets that one thing go, for the sake of a parfait with Eiichi, a perfume that smells of grape juice, a bike ride to the sea at sunset, and a future she wants to grow into "with Eiichi-kun and my friends."

And that's the other answer the book keeps giving, between the plots, in its soft chapters: she's a girl. She bangs her forehead on a trading-room desk. She kneels on the floor under a sign that says "I ESCAPED MY GUARDS AND OVERPOWERED A TERRORIST" while her best friends poke her calves. Her heart goes *ba-dump* when a boy tries to be as brave as her, and she calls him "Dummy!" because she doesn't know what else to say. She tells a room full of girls she's training them "ten, maybe twenty years into the future," as if she's sure she'll be there to see it, when the game she remembers says she won't.

The people who love her mostly understand this. Her household fears she is the Happy Prince, the statue who gives away his gold until there's nothing left of him. Nakamaro warns that there won't be a next time. And two of the quietest people in the book give her the two truest lessons. A choir girl with no power at all, asked whether we have a duty to be happy, answers simply, "we don't want people who know us to see us unhappy, do we?", and Runa laughs: "It's so obvious, but I didn't even see it." And Eiichi, who has just paid a trillion yen to impress her, suddenly sees what that money weighs: "Have you been carrying this burden around all this time, even while you've had a smile on your face?"

I think that's the heart of it. Six volumes in, Runa is still the cleverest person in every room. What's new in this one is that people have begun to see the weight she carries, and that she has begun, a little, to let them help with it.

But the book doesn't let us rest there. It ends with two sentences that face each other across the whole volume. The first is Kanbe's, spoken to a child in a university office with an unsmoked cigarette burning down in the ashtray: "You're a child, so you can choose the people at your side—it's not for this world, or for history, to decide for you." The second comes from a window in Frankfurt, from someone who has never met her: "History wants her to go back to Russia."

Remember the author's river from the very beginning: history runs in one direction, and only great events can turn it. Runa has spent six volumes trying to turn it before 2008. This volume shows her learning that she can't do it alone, and that perhaps she doesn't have to. Other hands can push too. And it ends with her, the winner, six trillion yen richer, smiling at Okazaki and saying, "I won. I'm still winning," while inside, where nobody can hear, she prays.

"I'm going to lose someday… God, if you exist, I only ask that you help me through the battle of 2008…"

That's what Volume 6 says Runa is. She is the most valuable piece on everyone else's board, and she knows it. And she is a girl who has decided, against everything she remembers, to plan for a long life, and who is frightened, and who goes on anyway.
