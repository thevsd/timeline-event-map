**Naming notes.** The cast list now calls **Toudou** "Keika Corp Holdings managing director and CEO" and still calls **Okazaki** a "young employee of Keika Corp Holdings," although Vol. 6 made him a managing director and president of Gulf Oil Development. **Tokitou Aki** is listed as "Secretary." **Tenmabashi Mitsuru** ("Keika Corp vice president") and the associate **Akibe Riko** now appear in the list. **Katsuki Shiori** is "from a Keikain branch family… daughter of Katsuki viscount"; Ch. 2 adds that her mother is the duke's legitimate daughter, so she and Runa share a grandfather. **Kurimori Shizuka**'s "main bank is Keika Bank"; the text has Ichijou rescue her family from Far Eastern Bank's Niigata branch (the bank's earlier name). **Kanna Mizuki** is "heir to Kanna family of fortune tellers/high-class sex workers." Several important figures are **not** in the list: Elder Kaihouin (never given a personal name), **Douanji Harumichi**, **Kanna Sera**, **Ono Kenichi**, **Natsume Kentarou**, **Nakajima Atsushi** and **Kasugano Yousuke**. One passage of Ch. 6 calls Akiko "**Tachibana** Akiko" (a slip). Vol. 6's "Nichika Oil Development" is spelled out here as "**Nichikaba**" (日樺, "Japan–Karafuto"). Ch. 10 gives Tomamu's liabilities as "twenty-two **million**" and then "twenty-two **billion**" (a slip). The prefix "**Imperial**" keeps standing in for Tokyo (帝都, *teito*) — the Imperial Game Show is the Tokyo Game Show — and the academy is still "Imperial Gakushuukan" (Gakushūin). Noble privilege is again "**diplomatic immunity**."

**Content notes.** Chapter 6's central conceit is that aborted fetuses and children who died young — many born to "those with no means of getting by except selling their bodies" — were used as fuel for the nation's luck. Chapter 2 reports a forced double suicide ("atone with their lives") and razor blades in letters; Chapter 6 describes an option, refused, in which a sniper would shoot a schoolgirl to drive out a spirit. Chapters 4 and 6 discuss the sexual activity of thirteen-year-olds — Kanna Mizuki's partners, contraceptive pills, her advice to Runa — and the boys' talk about Runa's body; a photographer asks her for nude photographs (refused). Chapter 1 gives a voice to ethnic contempt ("Anyone from Karafuto is completely disposable!"), and Chapters 4 and 6 show bullying of Karafuto-born students. The map reports this as the text gives it and quotes no more than the plot requires.

**Structural note.** The volume is built on a **ghost-story frame**. Three of the academy's "Seven Wonders" (the vanity mirror and the chain letters in Ch. 2; the dormitory zashiki warashi in Ch. 6), the fox god (Ch. 10) and the old man's fall (Ch. 13) form the supernatural arc. Between them sit politics and economics (Chs. 1, 3, 7, 9, 11–12) and respite (Chs. 4, 5, 8). Where Volume 6 widened its frame chapter by chapter, Volume 7 **narrows** it: from a national television debate to the school's toilets, dormitories and storerooms, with the wider world reduced to documents at the end (Chs. 11–12). Three third-person sections give voices to girls whom adults placed around Runa — **Kurimori Shizuka**, **Katsuki Shiori** and **Takahashi Akiko** — and each is freed by her own choice. Runa does not appear in Chs. 1, 9, 11, 12 or 13.

**The author's stated thesis (afterword).** "This was originally the story of Keikain Runa's downfall, but when the story was novelized, I decided to tear up that plotline and write the lead-up to a happy ending in which Keikain Runa's life is never ruined." In the original, "the dissolution of the postal service in 2005" was "the event that sealed her fate." A regular on 2channel's "Prime Minister Koizumi's Luck Is Too Amazing" threads, the author decided that "Keikain Runa needed to have amazing luck too, and that was how her childhood 'zashiki warashi' friend—Kaihouin Hotaru—came to be." The junior-high years will now be stories of the supernatural, "with the two girls connected to the supernatural fighting against this 'incredible luck,'" and "that's why I brought in the amendment to the Worker Dispatch Act as new material this time." Watching the 2024 elections, he found public anger at the dispatch amendment still alive but little memory of the 1997 failures of Sanyo Securities, Hokkaido Takushoku Bank and Yamaichi: "the line between 'our past' and 'history' must exist somewhere around that point in time." And: "A story where no one winds up happy in the end is far too depressing, even though it was reality."

Welcome to the twenty-first century, where money controls everything!" "**Let's use the amended Worker Dispatch Act to speed up layoffs.** We'll abandon unprofitable businesses and rake in more income, so that we can bring profits back to the shareholders!" A business-magazine scoop: "**Inner turmoil! Hidden debts discovered at Teikyou Airlines?!**" Then: "**Credit default swaps**—or CDSs, as they're called. Wall Street is fighting hard with City about the payment for those." "Money doesn't belong to any country, and it isn't inherently good or evil. It moves freely, washing away even fate as it does so." "Bang! There'll be plenty of chances to target them. # Part I — Chapter-by-Chapter Map
## Chapter 1 — The Overnight Broadcast and the Country's Post-Election Path

**Narrative position:** An all-night television debate the night after the July 2003 general election — a ruling-party representative, an opposition representative and Professor Kanbe as "special guest" — followed by a short third-person coda in Kyoto: an old man shouting at his television "as the summer departed." Runa does not appear. The chapter does for Volume 7 what Ch. 1 of Vol. 6 did for that volume: it sets the public agenda (Karafuto, labour, nobles' privileges, Iraq) and introduces, unnamed, the volume's last antagonist.

**What happens**

*The debate*
- The ruling party has won "a supermajority"; it reads this as "resounding approval of the Koizumi administration's reform policies." The opposition: "the manifesto we created to call for a change in the administration wasn't enough to sway the public."
- On Narita and Karafuto Bank: the ruling side refuses to let terrorism change the government and will not comment on Karafuto Bank. The opposition is "appalled" that the prime minister used the attack in "his characteristic theatrical politics" by bowing to Runa at party headquarters.
- On the war on terror, the opposition invokes anti-American feeling at Koizumi's election and history: "**Please think back to the Vietnam War. We followed in the footsteps of the United States, deploying Japanese troops, and what were we left with?**" and the Gulf War grudges behind 9/11. "As the United States' ally, isn't it our duty to advise them of the right path, now that their anger is blinding them?"
- **Kanbe's analysis:** the Karafuto Bank scandal hurt both sides, but "a group of liberals, and some members of the left-wing side of the opposition party, were connected to the former Northern Japanese government, and they maintained those relationships even after unification"; Narita "showed the public that Japan is America's right hand in this war on terror." Both causes of the landslide were external; watch the party's **internal conflict over postal privatisation** — no bill has yet been tabled.
- Viewer survey: the economy first, then unemployment, then diplomacy and security; postal privatisation fourth.
- The ruling side: bad debts are resolved by public money, but banks now hesitate to lend and demand repayment; re-employment after restructuring is the problem.
- **The opposition's platform:** improve living conditions in **Karafuto**; protect former Karafuto residents on the mainland, who "work in much worse environments"; stop companies replacing laid-off workers with cheaper Karafuto labour by amending the **Worker Dispatch Act** "so that all employees can be hired under identical circumstances"; reform the **Privy Council** and end nobles' "immunity from arrest."
- Kanbe: "many Karafuto nobles—as well as the former Northern Japanese government members who received peerages upon reunification—used their special privileges to commit crimes without fear of arrest… the main culprits behind the Karafuto Bank scandal are going to walk free"; American and Russian pressure will follow.
- The opposition compares Karafuto to **East Germany** after reunification (low wages, bankrupt state firms, rising unemployment) and cites "**twenty million residents of Karafuto descent in Karafuto, and a few million more**" on the mainland.
- Kanbe on both sides: who protects Karafuto workers exploited by zaibatsu, and who protects mainlanders who lose their jobs to them? The crux is "**globalism and neoliberalism… you'll offer opportunities but never lower the bar**." Karafuto workers are cheap partly because they do not need identity guarantees or social security; a reform requiring them would put everyone on an equal basis — and the root is Karafuto's corrupt administration.
- The ruling side: ID requirements for temporary workers through the Dispatch Act; Koizumi's motto "**Leave civil matters to civilians**." The opposition: unification's costs were dumped on zaibatsu and big firms, and Kanbe's maxim shows "the arrogance of the victors." Kanbe: should Karafuto people, now in an advanced country, get "an even lower bar at the expense of those from the mainland?" The opposition: "All human beings are of equal value… It's the job of politicians to correct that."
- The ruling side on Iraq: Japan is with the multinational forces and "still actively involved in **southern Iraq**."

*Kyoto*
- "Turn it off. I can't stand another second of that garbage." An old man in Kyoto rages: "Now's our chance to unshackle ourselves from the United States and gain true independence, but no one's willing to seize the opportunity!"
- The narration: dissidents in the ruling party, "decorated military heroes and conservative newspapers" tried to rouse Governor Iwasawa and Deputy PM Izumikawa to change the government and failed; the old man has lost his Nagata-cho connections without realising it.
- "Once that's finished, this country will start to walk its own path again… **We still lack the god that will protect this country!**"
- **The revelation:** "the girl who'd been sacrificed in order to turn her into a guardian god of Japan" — **Kaihouin Hotaru** — "was, in fact, trying to become human again through the power of Kobou Daishi's mandarins." The ritual, "performed with the grandfather of one of the girl's friends, Keikain Runa" — **Hikomaro** — "had been meant to bring prosperity to Japan, but it was now malfunctioning due to the meddling of the Allies and the modern era of land development." Hotaru's parents "had given up on the old man's delusions."
- "**Anyone from Karafuto is completely disposable!… They're the foundation we'll build our country's prosperity on top of!**"

**Revelations**
- The July election produced a ruling supermajority, credited by Kanbe to two external shocks: the Karafuto Bank scandal (which exposed the opposition left's ties to the old Northern regime) and Narita.
- This world's Japan **sent troops to Vietnam**.
- Former Northern officials were given peerages at reunification, and their noble immunity will let the Karafuto Bank culprits go free.
- Karafuto is Japan's internal low-wage labour reserve: twenty-odd million people.
- **Hotaru's sacrifice was a state ritual**, performed with Hikomaro to make a guardian god for Japan; an old man in Kyoto still believes in it, and her parents have broken with him.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the **Worker Dispatch Act** — "amended in 2003 and implemented on a very large scale… a safety net during the restructuring… but it also indirectly contributed to the lasting job drought afterward."
- **Historically real, used accurately:** Koizumi's "leave to the private sector what the private sector can do" (*minkan ni dekiru koto wa minkan ni*); the postal-privatisation split inside the LDP; the 2003 "manifesto" campaign; Japanese banks' credit crunch after the bad-debt clean-up; post-reunification East Germany (wage gaps, the Treuhand's closures, unemployment); the debate over globalisation and "neoliberalism" in Koizumi-era Japan; Japan's Self-Defense Forces in southern Iraq (Samawah, from 2004); Kōbō Daishi (Kūkai) as the founder of Shingon esoteric Buddhism.
- **Altered:** Japanese troops in the Vietnam War (Japan sent none); a supermajority in 2003; the SDF already deployed in summer 2003.
- **Fictional / in-world:** Karafuto, its 20 million people and its nobles; the Privy Council's role; the guardian-god ritual.
- **Context from earlier volumes:** "Kobou Daishi's mandarins" are **Asuka's Ehime mandarins** — Hotaru "was saved by Asuka's mandarins" (Vol. 5 Ch. 7; the friendship began with a mandarin in Vol. 1 Ch. 3, and Vol. 4 brought "Great Master Tanuki" mandarin jam). Ehime is in Shikoku, the land of Kūkai's pilgrimage, so the gift carries the Great Master's protection — *my reading of the phrase*.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The volume's political subject is Karafuto as an underclass.** The debate sets out every theme the later chapters dramatise — exploitation by zaibatsu, mainland resentment, crimes sheltered by noble privilege — and the Kyoto coda gives it its ugliest voice: "Anyone from Karafuto is completely disposable."
- *Strongly supported:* **Kanbe now speaks to the nation.** The economist who lectured Runa privately in Vol. 6 Ch. 3 is a television authority; his maxim ("offer opportunities but never lower the bar") is the neoliberal position stated honestly, and the opposition's answer ("All human beings are of equal value") is given its dignity.
- *Strongly supported:* **The supernatural plot is political.** Hotaru, who in earlier volumes was a gentle "zashiki warashi," was made one by a national ritual involving Runa's grandfather. The old man's "god that will protect this country" is a nationalist religion of sacrifice — the volume's last enemy, named in its final section ("The End of the Old Man's Delusions").
- *Plausible:* **"Independence from America" links the old man to the nationalists of Vol. 5** (the "ghost of antiquity," Iwasawa, Tsurui) — the same current that "indirectly caused the Second February 26 Incident."
- *Plausible:* **The Vietnam line changes the meaning of the Iraq dispatch.** This Japan has fought beside America before and remembers what it cost, which makes the opposition's anti-war case stronger than in our 2003.

**Connections:** Vol. 2 Ch. 8 and Vol. 3 (Hotaru as the dollhouse spirit; her sacrifice backstory, Vol. 2 Ch. 8 in the context file); Vol. 4 Ch. 7 (Hotaru invisible to cameras); Vol. 5 Ch. 1 (the American paper on the nationalist "ghost of antiquity"), Ch. 6 (Karafuto as low-wage zone), Ch. 7 (Hotaru chooses to become human; Kōbō Daishi); Vol. 6 Ch. 3 (Kanbe), Ch. 8 (the election; Karafuto Bank), Ch. 7 (Tachibana's "restoration" warning).

## Chapter 2 — Imperial Gakushuukan Academy's Seven Wonders: The Vanity Mirror in the Concert Hall and the Chain Love Letters

**Narrative position:** Runa's first person, with two third-person sections from the victims' side — **Kurimori Shizuka** and **Katsuki Shiori**. Autumn term 2003. Two linked ghost stories in which **Kanna Mizuki** works for Runa for the first time as a professional ("this was exactly the sort of thing I kept her around for"), with Hotaru as the unseen helper. The second story contains the most detailed glimpse yet of **the game's own timeline**, seen in Shiori's dreams.

**What happens**

*The vanity mirror*
- At a slumber party Mio reports a "Seven Wonder": a vanity in the concert hall whose reflection will take your fears away. The student council must deal with students making wishes at every mirror. Hotaru, half asleep: "A crossroads wish…"
- Kanna Mizuki explains: crossroads are boundaries between realms (Jizō statues and roadside shrines mark them); "Mirrors are symbols of the spirit realm"; "wishing for something from the spirit realm without taking any precautions sounds like suicide." Tachibana Yuka distrusts her. Mizuki draws **the Emperor, upright**: "The situation won't get any worse." Her fee: "Pay whatever you like… That's generally what we Kannas ask for."
- **Kurimori Shizuka's story (third person).** The Kurimori Group, a Niigata zaibatsu descended from Edo-period merchants that made its fortune in the United States (shipping, agriculture, fisheries), was ruined by bubble-era resort development. **Ichijou**, then assistant manager at Far Eastern Bank's Niigata branch, rescued it — and asked a favour: "**The young lady of the Keika Group desires friends in her life. Will you please provide the young lady with a friend?**" Shizuka was sent to Tokyo, "a hostage and a sacrifice all at once." On meeting Runa she fell "victim to the curse called 'admiration.'" In junior high the associates replaced the old followers and she was put in a different class: loneliness. Her father still struggles; her mother cries at every Tokyo-bound train.
- In an empty dressing room: "I wish I could be like Keikain-san." Her reflection, smiling like Runa, answers: "I can do it." She changes — studies hard, plays sport, makes friends — and starts spreading the mirror rumour.
- Mizuki: "That girl is possessed." But "Being possessed isn't always bad… she really did change for the better. Do you have a reason to change her back… Is it your responsibility?" Runa thinks: "I was essentially someone who'd 'possessed' Keikain Runa as well."
- Hotaru (unseen by the associates) writes on Runa's back: "The storeroom on the third floor." The supply ledger shows a vanity lent by **a bankrupt Karafuto noble family**, almost certainly caught up in the laundering scandal; such families lend valuables to the school to store them or raise their value — "This table would get a lot more valuable if the Keikain Runa used it." Mizuki traces it to "a royal European family in the Middle Ages who forfeited it to a pawnbroker."
- The trigger: Runa has been rehearsing **Faust** in the hall. Mizuki: supernatural events are "phenomena and analogies combined"; naming them exorcises them ("when a child becomes invisible, people write them off as… a 'zashiki warashi'"); she has categorised this one as ***The Phantom of the Opera***. Shizuka is Christine; Runa: "I guess this makes me Carlotta, doesn't it?"
- The plan: let the spirit try to harm Runa so that "Christine" can shine. The associates object; Runa overrides them: "**Yuka-san, do you want me to be the kind of person who abandons her friend to protect her own safety?**" (The narration: "moral obligations and *naniwabushi* still existed in our country. Of course, that was also why everyone here would experience the hell of deflation.") Mizuki: seventy percent — fifty from the Emperor card, twenty from Hotaru. "Be sure never to let go of that girl."
- Runa buys the vanity and ships it to the Kanna house. Her lesson passes without incident. Shizuka, at a café: "I recently experienced a waking dream… I wanted to be just like you." — "You can do it." — "You're my idol, Keikain-san. I knew you'd never do anything mean." Mizuki: because Runa is her idol, the spirit could not use Shizuka to hurt her, and the pact broke.
- Payment: Runa offers **a blank check good for up to a trillion yen**. Mizuki returns it and asks only for her tea. Runa's reason for the offer: it was worth more than a trillion "to keep someone with skills as rare as hers on my team—especially since **I knew that my downfall came after losing her**." Hotaru, asked what she did: "Not telling."

*The chain letters*
- After Narita, Runa effectively runs the academy's security; cameras make anonymous notes impossible. Paparazzi photographing her at the school pool from a distant tower block were spotted by "a former Northern Japanese military sniper" on her staff.
- Letters pour from Mizuki's desk — razor blades inside; twelve to her ("You're a thief. Give me back my boyfriend"), four to Runa, three of them chain letters ("Send this exact letter to three other people, or you'll never meet that special someone"). Mizuki: as a recent, protected favourite she draws everyone's envy. Runa: "Who exactly do you think I am?" — "You're Lady Runa, right?"
- Mizuki stops Runa opening the last letter: "killing someone with a curse isn't a crime in this country." **Her theory of curses:** they are "paradoxical" — misfortune makes a curse manifest, so knowing you are cursed is enough; knowing little, Runa can "settle the score" with any small mishap; but a curse for sleeping with someone's boyfriend weighs as much as the grudge. (She leaves for a date. Everyone thinks: *You deserve those curses!*)
- Hotaru stares at **Katsuki Shiori**, who blurts: "I-it wasn't me! I got one of those letters too!" She has four.
- **Shiori's story (third person).** Her mother is the duke's legitimate daughter: she and Runa **share a grandfather**. Her other grandfather, Viscount Katsuki, commanded the **Third Guards Division**: "It was Duke Keikain and me who saved this country" — when a threatened assassination of the prime minister during the Pacific War brought a change of government, the Guards protecting the capital "hadn't revolted like those involved in the February 26 incident." At the group's fiftieth-anniversary party she heard the adults' gossip about Runa (heir to the bankrupt Far Eastern Group, backed by Far Eastern; the main family and branches unhappy) and her father's instruction: "**Shiori, you'll be working for Runa-sama, not Nakamaro-sama.**"
- Her first love was her home tutor, a young medical graduate related to the Keikains. He worried where Runa would be placed in the family and whether she should have male attendants — "**We don't want any pesky flies buzzing around her.**" A week later he and his girlfriend died in a double suicide. Shiori never learned that it "was linked to the failed kidnapping attempt on Keikain Runa, and that the pair were forced to atone with their lives as a gesture of apology over their use of noble diplomatic immunity."
- She watched Runa with Asuka, Hotaru, Mio and the three boys and never felt seen; the associates became a wall; then Kanna Mizuki arrived and "pesky flies" surfaced "as a curse." A voice: "Shall I grant your wish for you?" A letter in her locker.
- **The dreams — the game's world.**
  1. Her tutor's wedding: "**Iwazaki has absorbed most of Keika now**, but thanks to my job, I'll still be able to get by."
  2. In junior high, at Runa's side (Mizuki on the other): "**As the heir to the Keikain Dukedom… I declare to the people of Karafuto, burdened with financial hardships… Karafuto will be remade into a special economic zone, becoming a tax haven and legal haven…**" Ryuu Suzune, smiling and appraising: "Myself and all the Chinese expats are hoping you can bring them to fruition." Runa whispers to her: "**Are you familiar with subprime loans? I hear that American and European investors are buying them up right now.**" Shiori "had no idea that her words would be a curse of destruction."
  3. In the Courtiers' hall, Yuujirou and Mitsuya ask Shiori to help: "**bad blood between the Keikain and Takanashi families**"; Takanashi Mizuho has been the scholarship students' champion against Runa, "the head of the noble faction"; Kanna Mizuki is "a troublemaker" who "roped Takanashi Mizuho, Keikain Runa, Teia Eiichi, and others into this big mess." The student council will recommend Mizuki for **the Teia Group's study-abroad programme**; Shiori must persuade Runa (the council's vice-president) to approve.
  4. Then Shiori at Runa's side with Mizuki gone: "**You received a call from Okazaki Yuuichi-sama of Gekkou Investment Firm.**" — "The one being organized by **Pacific Global Investment Fund**? **Angela Sullivan**-sama, their Far Eastern regional fund manager, has requested time to meet with you." Runa: "Well, she's just some spokeswoman for the company, but **I'll treat her nicely as my puppet. ## Chapter 3 — The Young Lady Versus the Scavengers

**Narrative position:** Runa's first person; early autumn 2003, after the summer's yen battle. Five scenes: Tachibana's briefing on a newspaper campaign, the main Keikain residence, Shisuka Lydia's confession, a film night with Okazaki, and a walk with Tachibana through her old Den-en-chofu garden. The volume's business-and-politics chapter: a new kind of Wall Street investor, a plan for a Northern Karafuto state with Runa at its head, and the autumn Diet's three Karafuto bills.

**What happens**

*The scavengers' advertisement*
- Newspapers carry ads, placed through several agencies by **Wall Street funds**: "Businesses belong to the shareholders. Managers must put in the work to raise their share prices." Tachibana: "most likely targeting the Keika Group." These are not the burnt vultures of Vol. 6 but "vultures who feast on the bodies of other vultures" (Mark Barnes's phrase) — Okazaki calls them "**scavengers**."
- Their complaint: "Any shareholder would be angered to see a company make off with tens of billions of dollars, then seemingly squander it on corporate acquisitions and public-works projects." Their offer: "If you're not going to spend your billions in profit effectively, let us do it for you instead."
- Their creed, from an American business-school essay: (1) top-down decision-making and standardisation; (2) global sourcing; (3) cost cuts through personnel reductions; (4) return the capital to shareholders.
- Markets call her spending spree "**dumpster diving**" — she bought struggling companies for more than they were worth. Runa realises she is a scavenger too.
- Tachibana: she has proved herself as a manager — "the sixty billion dollars you managed to raise" — which is exactly why the scavengers attack **the disadvantages of having you in charge**: (1) she is a minor, so the group's future depends on "the wisdom… of a child"; (2) there is only one of her, and holding the reins of four great companies leaves "no time left to live a life of your own." Her shopping spree "set the troubling precedent of your willingness to intervene in company management," undoing the point of reorganising and listing.

*The main residence*
- A line of cars outside: people caught up in the **Karafuto bribery scandal** hoping the dukedom will lend them noble immunity. The Keikains have used theirs only for whistleblowers in Keika Rules bailouts (Ichiyama Securities) and to save Tachibana.
- **Nakamaro:** the discovery of the laundering at Karafuto Bank's New York branch has produced over a hundred arrests and suicides, but no principal has been touched; the Karafuto officials who held proof used their privileges to flee — **to Northern Karafuto**.
- **Northern Karafuto's status:** Soviet territory managed by Northern Japan before the USSR fell, then seized; brought into Japan at reunification; claimed by both Japan and Russia and governed by neither — a crossing for the Chinese mafia and terrorists. Clean-up operations there uncovered the scandal. The Mamiya Strait would have been a better border; "This country has experienced a land war with the Soviet Union—or rather Russia." Japan took on the North's debts and Southern Karafuto's reconstruction and cannot afford to impose law in the north.
- Nakamaro, unusually bitter: "I think we reunified without considering the consequences. Even though we had Germany's example… Not only did we take on Karafuto's debts, but we're now making an enemy of Russia." Then the warning: "**Northern Karafuto is trying to establish a new nation and make you its ruler.** In other words, once this bribery investigation reaches the true masterminds… they'll try to install you as their new country's leader in order to escape punishment for their crimes. What makes me most furious is that another group gave them that very idea."

*Lydia*
- Shisuka Lydia: her father, **Marquess Shisuka**, has "retired" so her elder brother can take over. "**That's right—we've been involved in money laundering… Thank goodness we have diplomatic immunity as nobles.**" The marquess saw Koizumi's reform coming.
- On the new-nation plan: "A group of people has actually been putting money into that idea for a while now, and the bribery scandal suddenly made it seem more realistic." Eva and Anisha check with their old services; Kitagumo Ryouko goes home to Karafuto to ask her old comrades.
- Lydia's whisper: "**Nichikaba Oil Development**, who you reportedly bailed out, issued bonds. Now that they're worthless, I hear certain people are buying up as many as they can." The scavengers are behind this too.

*Wall Street, with popcorn*
- Runa watches a rented videotape of an old Wall Street film with **Okazaki** (Yuka brings grape juice and popcorn). "I wanted something more compelling than the real-life version… in the end, they always stick to the right course." She reflects that she is "basically engaging in an extreme form of insider trading by building my fortune with the help of my past-life memories."
- Okazaki: the scavengers really are buying Nichikaba's bonds — the bonds expose Nichikaba to lawsuits that could stop its merger with **Gulf Oil Development** into **Keika Resource Development**. From their side, "they stopped you from wasting two trillion yen, just so they could earn your favor." They want the cash — from the government, Iwazaki or her — and "their real goal is to become your allies. However, they're trying to save you with Wall Street logic." And the new country? "**That new country would be a tax haven and a legal haven. Is there any more to it than that?**" So the scavengers will be fighting Koizumi too.
- Okazaki, gently: "**Your goal is to change the world itself, my lady, and you don't care whether you lose your Keika Group in the process. Right?**" Tachibana and Ichijou sense it; Ichijou "made sure to give me a stern reminder." "I won't abandon you, even when you're ready to put everything on the line and make your true gamble… until now, your gambles have always had sixty-forty or seventy-thirty odds." "**You haven't yet put your affairs in order so that you can truly gamble everything, have you?**… I've already bet everything on you… you've already got hundreds of thousands of chips—over a million, if you include their families. You'll only get one chance to bet all those chips at once… So if the time comes, please don't forget that." (Yuka forbids him a cigarette; he takes a candy one.)
- The film ends with the wrongdoers punished. Runa: "It would never have turned out like that in real life." Okazaki: "Right—because there's such thing as diplomatic immunity."

*Den-en-chofu*
- Her old Den-en-chofu manor is now the associates' dormitory; her bedroom has been left untouched. Tachibana, now a president "without the right of representation," is back in his butler's uniform. "You've worked very hard… I'd like to tell you that it's time for a rest, but I don't think you'll listen."
- "The Keika Group has gotten so big. It's too big… Without people like you around, it would probably crumble underneath me." — "You understand this, yet you continue to expand?" — "People asked me to save them, so I did." Inwardly: "**It's all because no one ever reached out and helped me when I needed it all those years ago.**"
- **The autumn Diet:** the showpiece is a bill to strip nobles of immunity, but Koizumi's real aims are **a Karafuto special-zone bill** (abolish the Karafuto Reconstruction Agency, make the region a special economic zone and a proving ground for deregulation) and **an amendment of the Worker Dispatch Act** (more jobs, low wages). Committee time decides what passes; he needs at least one. Tachibana: after Narita "the persecution of second-class citizens is growing so severe that the prime minister can no longer ignore it," and Karafuto has discredited immunity.
- Runa quotes Caesar: "However poorly a precedent may turn out, when you go back to its origins, you'll find that precedent was set with good intentions." Koizumi is "fundamentally… my enemy… our real relationship was something closer to mutually assured destruction."
- Her decision: **back the Karafuto Special Zone Act**; resist the dispatch amendment this session — "It wasn't like I'd forgotten all the suffering it had caused me so long ago" — though it would suit Keika and the other zaibatsu, and shareholder capitalism will push it through eventually; on immunity, she believes it "shouldn't exist," but the issue "hadn't existed in my past life… I was terrified to make a decision about an event I knew nothing of."
- Tachibana: "**I, Tachibana, shall serve you for as long as I am alive.**" She looks back once at the manor: "Little by little, the future was turning into something I'd never seen."

**Revelations**
- A new class of investor ("scavengers") is courting and pressuring Keika with shareholder-value doctrine, and buying the defaulted bonds of **Nichikaba** — the full name of Vol. 6's "Nichika," confirming its Karafuto reference.
- Karafuto's guilty officials have fled to **Northern Karafuto**, a no-man's-land claimed by Japan and Russia; a long-funded plan to make it a new state with Runa as ruler — a tax and legal haven — has been revived.
- The Shisuka marquessate laundered money and is hiding behind noble immunity.
- Okazaki, Tachibana and Ichijou all know that Runa would sacrifice the Keika Group to change the world.
- Runa's first-life suffering involved the Worker Dispatch regime.
- Koizumi's autumn programme: Karafuto special zone, dispatch amendment, abolition of noble immunity.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the film = ***Wall Street*** (1987; the "greed" speech; insider trading); the four principles as the practice of "famous companies such as GE Capital, as well as Japanese McDonald's"; the Caesar quotation.
- **Historically real, used accurately:** the shareholder-value creed of the period (Jack Welch's GE; activist and "vulture" funds in Japan — Steel Partners, the Murakami Fund); distressed-debt investors who buy defaulted bonds to gain leverage in restructurings; Japan's "Big Bang" financial reforms; committee-centred legislating in the Diet; the 2003 Worker Dispatch amendment (manufacturing dispatch allowed from March 2004); German reunification's costs; the Tatar (Mamiya) Strait; Den-en-chōfu.
- **The Caesar line** is from Sallust's *Conspiracy of Catiline* (51.27), in Caesar's speech: "All bad precedents have originated from good measures" (*omnia mala exempla ex rebus bonis orta sunt*) — *my identification of the source*.
- **Altered / fictional:** Northern Karafuto as a Japanese-claimed no-man's-land; the scavengers' campaign; Nichikaba; the Shisuka marquessate.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **"Tax haven and legal haven" is the game's ending, offered again.** Vol. 5 Ch. 9 reconstructed the game's downfall as a Karafuto laundering haven sheltered by her immunity; Ch. 2 of this volume showed the game-Runa proclaiming it. Now real people — fugitives, scavengers and "another group" — are funding the same plan, with her as its face.
- *Strongly supported:* **Okazaki names the series' secret.** "Your goal is to change the world itself… you don't care whether you lose your Keika Group." For six volumes the group has looked like the goal; to the people closest to her it is a stake. His "put your affairs in order" is a gambler's advice — and an echo of the game's Shiori.
- *Strongly supported:* **The scavengers' critique is right, and Tachibana says so.** A minor who intervenes in management, with no successor and no life of her own, is a governance risk. The volume keeps the series' honesty: the antagonists' logic is the logic of the era, and partly correct.
- *Strongly supported:* **Her first life shapes her politics.** "No one ever reached out and helped me when I needed it" explains the rescues; "the suffering it had caused me" explains her resistance to the dispatch amendment. The employment-ice-age generation's grievance (Vol. 4 Ch. 11) is now a policy position.
- *Plausible:* **The future is leaving the script.** Noble immunity "hadn't existed in my past life"; she is "terrified" of decisions without foreknowledge. The more she changes, the less her advantage — the paradox Kanna Mizuki called "the curse of making the future definite" in reverse.
- *Plausible:* **"Mutually assured destruction" with Koizumi** applies the series' nuclear vocabulary to their politics: neither can destroy the other without being destroyed.
- *Speculative:* **"Another group gave them that very idea"** points at the Frankfurt plan of Vol. 6 Ch. 10.

**Connections:** Vol. 1 (Ichiyama Securities; the Den-en-chofu manor); Vol. 3 Ch. 4 ("The Last Days of the Den-en-chofu Manor"); Vol. 4 Ch. 9 (immunity used for Tachibana), Ch. 11 (her first life); Vol. 5 Ch. 9 (the game's Karafuto haven; "the guillotine"); Vol. 6 Ch. 3 (Kanbe's "fatal flaw"), Ch. 7 (Tachibana's "restoration"), Ch. 8 (Nichika, Gulf Oil Development), Ch. 9 (Keika Resource Development; "dumpster diving" purchases), Ch. 10 (the European plan); this volume Ch. 1 (the Karafuto debate), Ch. 2 (the game's proclamation).

## Chapter 4 — The Young Lady's Daily Life: Autumn 2003

**Narrative position:** Runa's first person in a string of comic episodes, with a closing third-person scene among the three boys; September–October 2003. Like Vol. 6 Ch. 5, a "daily life" chapter that is also about money, family and the image industries: a midnight snack run, her own fashion brand, a staged negotiation inside the group, the school's bathroom politics, a TV game show, a Formula One weekend at Suzuka, and two conversations about sex and marriage. A bonus story sits inside the chapter (the game show's later episodes).

**What happens**

*The midnight convenience store*
- After late-night gaming she goes down in pyjamas and a jacket to the 24-hour store in Kudanshita Tower. **Anisha** on duty: Runa has "grown" — she no longer sneaks out (they once tore up every cardboard box in the tower looking for her when she practised Hotaru's hiding trick; even infrared failed: "you'd practically become a one-woman army"). "Would you like me to pull up a video from the Narita terrorist incident right this minute?"
- Head of security **Nakajima Atsushi** comes down; the store is staffed by guards and maids (robbers are overpowered and walked next door to the police box). Consommé chips, mini cookies, pudding, an unfamiliar apple tea. Nakajima buys snacks but will return for his beer after the shift change: Anisha — people drop their guard near the end of a shift, "so it's important to remove any temptations." Upstairs, **Saitou Keiko** is waiting: "Shall we have a little chat?"

*Tír na nÓg*
- The photographer **Ishikawa Nobumitsu** proposes her own brand; a sweating **Teisei Department Stores** executive explains: fans want to copy her outfits, which come from many brands on many floors; a single shop, sourcing directly from manufacturers, would cut out the brands; items she has worn sell **50% faster**; department-store clothing has struggled since the bubble because teens and twenty-somethings stopped buying there.
- Ishikawa turns out to be an **independent director of Teisei** (now a Keika Railway subsidiary): he overspent on outfits for her and had to take the post. Runa had never read the subsidiaries' board lists — "I really felt the importance of separating ownership from management."
- The brand is named **Tír na nÓg**, the Celtic land of eternal youth, to be "scrap[ped]… once you reach the age where 'young' no longer applies." Ishikawa: "Every moment in a young girl's life is short-lived, so I want to make a record of them." (He asks for nude photos; she refuses.) After Narita it sells out; it even sells the **angel-wing harness**.
- **The staged negotiation.** Kanegana Cosmetics (a Keika Corp subsidiary that kept Kanegana Textiles afloat) wants her to use only its products; Ishikawa wants the world's best. The executive weeps on his knees: "I won't beg you to save us! I'm only asking for a chance!" The outcome was fixed in advance by **Mikihara** (Keika Railway), **Toudou** (Keika Corp) and **Tenmabashi**, who advised her to attend "study how companies worked"; the meeting is held so both sides record it. Ruling: "**Make something that Ishikawa-sensei will approve of… If it's up to global standards, I'll wear it.**" Kanegana's lipstick poster — "*I have yet to fall in love, but when it's time, I'll be prepared*" — multiplies sales tenfold.

*The bathroom wars*
- With six "queen bees" per grade, each with a retinue, a trip to the toilet becomes a **daimyo's procession**: scouts claim a stall; simultaneous arrivals become prestige fights that can draw in parents. Shiori: "we nobles are what remains of the kuge and daimyo." Runa: "But my family weren't among the new kuge nobles who showed up during the Showa era!" Nanami offers a pill "that helps you hold it in" — "It's Western medicine, after all" (Eastern-bloc dark humour). Runa sees why the game's Runa "had always had a flock of girls behind her."
- Her solution: she donates **a prefabricated building with bathrooms** (and an expanded office for Kitakaba Security). **Head Librarian Takamiya Haruka** gets it approved — on one condition: "**You'll be the one who cleans that bathroom, Keikain-san.**" Runa agrees gladly: nobles "couldn't let ourselves look content" with special treatment; Ichijou still cleans his executive bathroom.
- **Asuka** asks to "borrow your bathroom" — an alliance before the queen-bee politics boil over. The eye of the storm is **Kanna Mizuki**: "she's been with five different boys." If she became pregnant, the backlash would fall on her sponsor. Runa reflects that the game's Runa probably used Mizuki's body and connections "for her own gain," until Mizuki "was eventually ousted and sent to study overseas," protecting herself "by remaining the jester that she was." The prefab turns out to contain a rest room with a bed and shower, added at Mizuki's request. Runa: "Don't forget to use birth control, okay?" — "I could birth and raise a child from anyone's seed."
- **Shisuka Lydia** asks the same favour: her family's role in the **Karafuto bribery scandal**, mocked nightly on talk shows, has turned into bullying; "bullying targeted at anyone from Karafuto had started popping up." Runa and her associates are spared because television portrays her as Narita's victim. Her price: Lydia and Mizuki help her scrub the bathroom.

*The Young Lady's Challenge (and bonus story)*
- Having bought a game company (Zugagaga) "to waste money," she agrees to guest on a late-night game show when a segment collapses; the AD hands her **a 3D mecha action game** ("dancing"; you pay for your bullets; arenas). She is hooked: "Wouldn't it be easier for me to purchase this company and run it myself than for me to be its pilot?" The AD refuses to put her in a swimsuit: viewers "would rather see you play the game."
- Later episodes: three 1980s arcade games found in highway-side restaurants. How games entered her life: after visiting a grave in Sakata, waiting for a taxi in a roadside restaurant with arcade cabinets, feeding in hundred-yen coins "until my butler finally scolded me." What games mean to her: "**I think I'd call them 'just'**… a game exists in a system with a clear set of rules, and if you follow them, you're guaranteed to win."
- **Imperial Game Show 2003:** amateur photographers mob her; Aki: "In the old days, we could have cut those photographers down" (secretly clutching her own camera); Ishikawa appears and is dragged away. On stage she plays a 16-bit falling-block puzzle game against twenty raffle winners (950 entered) and shakes hands with those who beat her. A journalist asks about Zugagaga: PvP and online play "utilizing communication lines," and arcade experiences "that can't be replicated on home consoles." The AD: "You really are a young noblewoman, aren't you?" A thread (no. 261): lunakeikain protests "she's still just a junior high schooler!" and reports a creep.

*Suzuka*

Runa sings **"Kimigayo"** in a **Teia Motor Co.** race-queen outfit, rejecting the costume meant to flatter the producer; the Teia team reads the signal. "The red Prancing Horse rode its way to victory."

*Avanti: the boys*

Yuujirou: "the four of us are already adults in a way" — their tea is a TIG Backup Systems board meeting (TIG is building **a core banking system for a certain bank** and will build one for Keika Holdings). Mitsuya, flatly: "**We'd be lying if we said that your body didn't excite us…** You come here and give us orders, looking exactly like an adult; you squeal over video games on TV… What do you think we're supposed to do about that?" Each is worth hundreds of millions in TIG shares; Yuujirou receives marriage-meeting photographs.

He has refused a marriage meeting with a **Futaki** daughter: Futaki (merged with Yodoyabashi, both Edo-period houses) wants to rebuild its group and is eyeing **Honami Bank**. "Is there another girl in all of Japan who could bring about more returns than you?" *Ba-dump.* "You idiot." "Becoming an adult meant choosing another person. But I hoped to experience times like these just a little longer."

*The locker room*

*The boys alone*

Eiichi admits desire (the Game Show swimsuit, the race-queen outfit); Mitsuya has Ishikawa's photographs (via **Nozuki Misaki**, secretly a contract programmer for TIG who spends her pay on computers); Yuujirou bought the photobook, and his source is **Eva Charon**, "whom he'd met through his father." ## Chapter 5 — The Young Lady's Imoni Party

**Narrative position:** Runa's first person; mid-autumn 2003, a trip to **Sakata** with the Ichijou family and much of the household. A travelogue chapter — the imoni party on the Mogami River, a girls' tour of Sakata and Mount Chōkai, a hot spring — that carries three serious threads: Ichijou's "return in triumph" and future, the meaning of a hometown, and the narrator's verdict on the autumn Diet's laws.

**What happens**

*The trip and the hotel*
- **Ichijou Erika** asks to come along to Sakata, her and her father's birthplace, for an **imoni party** (taro stew, a Yamagata autumn custom; "We'll be barbecuing too"). The personal train fills with the Ichijous, Tachibana and Yuka, Keiko, Aki, Katsura Naomi, **Katsura Naoyuki and his family**, Keikain relatives, associates and guards.
- They stay at an old hotel, neither Keika nor Far Eastern, to be demolished when **Keika Central Sakata** ("Keika Palace") opens. Ichijou: Far Eastern Bank held it as collateral because it made a little money; during the bubble "They actually tried, but I put a stop to it" when Far Eastern Developments rode the boom (it later went into corporate reorganisation); "At first people hated me for leaving profits on the table, but now they're grateful." The Ichijous married in its hall.
- From the Uetsu Line: the Sea of Japan coast made "sacred ground" by a Hokkaido TV show's moped stunt; Atsumi Onsen; the Shonai Plain's ripe rice, autumn leaves on Mount Gassan, snow on **Mount Chōkai**.

*The imoni party*
- At Kasen Park on the **Mogami River**, in Sakata's notorious wind: one giant pot fed by a brand-new excavator (later lent to a construction firm for luck), fifteen smaller pots, three tents, six minivans, **more than two hundred guests** — "Everyone here used to work for **Far Eastern Bank**." Ichijou: "We'll all be eating from the same pot." "No one would try to poison you at an imoni party… It would violate the party's sanctity."
- Runa peels taro with the local girls for cable TV. "Hey, what's with the pork in the—" — "My lady, that's a forbidden topic to discuss in Shonai" (Shonai imoni: pork and miso). She leads the toast: "Cheers!" The mayor and the Keika Bank branch manager surround Ichijou: it is "a celebration of Ichijou-shi's triumphant return from Tokyo."
- Tachibana: "**Some people in Yamagata Prefecture want Ichijou-shi to be made the next prefectural governor.**" With Keika Holdings' CEO title (and later representative rights) passing to **Angela**, Ichijou, still young, needs "a second life"; and "**Katou-shi lost his position, and I hear they've had trouble finding his successor**" — Keika needs someone to hold Yamagata.
- Ichijou, a little drunk: "I'm not going to become prefectural governor… **If I were that competent, I would never have let Angela-san take over Keika Holdings.**" On the bubble: "I didn't take advantage because I couldn't, not because I didn't want to… I knew the bubble was going to burst. **I just sat at the base of a tree and waited for a rabbit to fall into my clutches.**" The second "rabbit" was Otsumaro's **COCOM violation**, which shook Far Eastern Bank and, with the bubble's end, made Ichijou head of the Tokyo branch for bad-debt disposal — where he met Runa. "I told you before that I'd be going home in glory, right?… **This is all I need. Could you ask for anything more?**"
- After the broadcast, a lobby from Yamagata City asks her to eat *their* imoni too.

*The girls' tour*
- With Erika, Yuka and Aki: the **Sankyo rice warehouses** — Sakata as "the City of Merchants," the Shonai rice route to Edo and Osaka; the **Honma** family ("I may not be able to become a Honma, but at least let me be a daimyo instead"), who restored Shonai Domain's finances; Far Eastern Bank as heir to Sakata's merchant tradition. A civic hall — once Far Eastern Bank's cultural annex — is being completed with Keika money; Erika and Aki want her to sing at its opening.
- Runa: "At first, my goal had just been to survive. But then, when I didn't want to leave Aki-san and the others, I'd gotten Tachibana and Ichijou roped into everything. Only now was I realizing that, somewhere along the way, **I'd formed a strong bond with this city.**"
- The **Ōjishi** (giant lion heads) built after the **1976 Sakata fire** as a symbol of recovery; Yamagata grape juice; **Sakata Lighthouse**, wind turbines going up, oil tanks and power stations, **tankers bearing the Keika moon-and-cherry-blossom funnel mark**, a refinery security firm with the same badge.
- Vertical traffic lights and snow poles; a bumpy national highway — "**the Koizumi administration's reforms slashed local budgets**, so they just can't keep up." The narration: Tanaka Kakuei's Plan for Remodeling the Japanese Archipelago gave the regions hope and debt; after the bubble "many local regions had been left with piles of bodies," Far Eastern Developments among them. Erika: thanks to Keika, Sakata is fairly well off, and the **Great Heisei municipal mergers** may bring more road money.
- **Maruike-sama**, a spring-fed pool at Chōkai's foot "where Ryūjin-sama dwells"; **Ōmonoimi Shrine** (first shrine of Dewa; the mountain itself is the god) and Fukurakuchi-no-miya.
- At sunset, **Jūroku Rakan Iwa** (the Sixteen Arhats carved into seaside rock from the Bakumatsu to the Meiji period by local monks, as a prayer for Buddhism, for drowned fishermen and safe seas). Erika: the monks "they're said to have thrown themselves from the cliffs so that they could watch over and protect the carvings forever." Today's monks say, "Anything with a form will someday lose that form." Erika: "what was the point in dying for them?" Aki: "I don't think there was any need for them to die." Yuka asks Runa. Runa, as a villainess who still feels "my duty to fulfill the villainous role I'd been granted," **empathises with the monks**.
- **The narrator on the Diet:** the **Karafuto Special Zone Act** and the **Worker Dispatch amendment** are under deliberation, both meant to help the unemployed of the bad-debt era and the displaced former Northerners. "**The results had already made their mark on history. The issue of immigrants and refugees would worsen tensions in Japan, and temporary relief for the unemployed would become permanent, causing a job drought that would bring misfortune to an entire generation.**" Runa repeats Caesar's "good intentions" line (Yuka, like her grandfather, asks who said it).
- Runa: "Juroku Rakan Iwa will also be worn away by the waves within another hundred years or so, but **it's wrong to go to extremes just to preserve it. That's why we should commit the scene before us to memory**, at the very least." A guard takes a photo of the four of them; it becomes "like a treasure to all four of us."

*Chōkai Onsen*
- The lodge's great bath is reserved for her; to apologise, the public baths are free for everyone "on the Keika dime." A salt spring — "basically just warm seawater."
- **Girl talk.** The imoni party was secretly **a marriage meeting for Erika**: suitors included an **Imperial Iwazaki Bank** executive, **the vice-president of Karafuto Heavy Industries** (an Iwazaki family member transferred from Iwazaki Heavy Industries, its real manager), a Hokkaido Diet member's son, a Yamagata Diet member's son and local heirs; her friends drove them off. Runa: "**The Iwazaki zaibatsu was trying to surround me**" — through Keika-Iwazaki Chemical, Sakurako, and now her staff.
- **Aki** is arranging a marriage with **an executive of Keika-Iwazaki Chemical**; the husband will be **adopted into the Tokitou family, founding a new Keikain branch family — "the highest-ranking clan dedicated to my protection."** Erika's family would rank next. (Since Vol. 1 Aki has been implied to be Hikomaro's daughter by Saitou Keiko; the new branch would be Keikain by blood.)
- Erika: "my hope is that Sakata will become your hometown… **Tokyo is a lovely place to live, but it's not a good place to 'come home to.'**" The top floor of Keika Central Sakata is to be Runa's apartment; with her parents' graves here, "Sakata actually did feel a little like my hometown."
- Leaving for ramen, they pass "some sort of nearby statue" of a historical figure; Runa's mind is on noodles. (The glossary names **Ishihara Kanji**, "a Japanese army member who wrote *The Theory of the Final World War*.")

**Revelations**
- Ichijou is being courted to become **governor of Yamagata** to fill the gap left by Katou's fall; he refuses and calls himself less competent than Angela.
- Ichijou's career was built on two "rabbits": the bubble's collapse, which he foresaw, and Otsumaro's COCOM scandal.
- **Aki will found a new Keikain branch family** through an Iwazaki-linked marriage, ranking first among Runa's protector houses; the Iwazaki are courting her inner circle (Erika too).
- Keika's Sakata presence: a refinery, tankers, wind power, a civic hall, Keika Central Sakata with an apartment for her.
- The narrator confirms that both autumn laws "made their mark on history": refugee tensions and a generation's job drought.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** ***Suiyō Dō Deshō*** (the Hokkaido TV variety show) and its "Darumaya Wheelie" incident; imoni's regional variations; **Ishiwara (Ishihara) Kanji**, the army officer behind the 1931 Manchurian Incident and author of *Sekai saishū sen ron* (*The Theory of the Final World War*), a native of Shonai buried at the foot of Mount Chōkai.

- **Uncertain:** the legend that the carving monks threw themselves from the cliffs is reported in the novel as a field-trip story; I have not found it in standard accounts.
- **Fictional / in-world:** Far Eastern Bank and its Sakata roots; Keika Central Sakata; Karafuto Heavy Industries.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **Sakata is offered to her as a home — the counterweight to every "country" she has been offered.** Fuchigami's "where do you intend to become queen?", the Courtiers' Karafuto state, Mark's "your own country," Northern Karafuto's new nation: all are thrones. Erika offers a place to say "I'm home." The chapter's whole design (rice, merchants, shrines, a fire and its recovery, a photograph) makes the case.
- *Strongly supported:* **Ichijou is the anti-Runa.** A man who saw the bubble coming and *waited*, who refuses power ("If I were that competent…"), who wants only a riverside party in his hometown — "Could you ask for anything more?" He is the adult who has learned Kanbe's "Give up" in its gentlest form, and the book honours him for it.
- *Strongly supported:* **The Rakan scene is the volume's meditation on her own fate.** Monks who die to guard what will erode anyway; a villainess who "empathized" with them because she still feels bound to her role; her conclusion — not to go to extremes to preserve a thing, but to remember it — is the opposite of self-sacrifice. It quietly answers Ch. 3's Okazaki ("your true gamble").
- *Strongly supported:* **The narrator passes judgment on the period.** "Temporary relief for the unemployed would become permanent, causing a job drought that would bring misfortune to an entire generation" is the series' clearest statement of its era-trauma — the Worker Dispatch regime that Runa (Ch. 3) remembers from her first life.
- *Plausible:* **The ignored statue is a joke with an edge.** Ishiwara Kanji planned the real Manchurian Incident and preached a "final war"; this world actually had a Manchurian War with the Soviets. Runa walks past him thinking of ramen — a respite chapter brushing past the man whose ideas made her world's history.
- *Plausible:* **The Iwazaki "encirclement"** — of Erika, of Aki's marriage, of her family by Sakurako — shows the zaibatsu managing her through the people around her rather than through her, as Ch. 4's boys described.

**Connections:** Vol. 1 Ch. 2 (Far Eastern Bank; Ichijou's recruitment; the COCOM scandal), Ch. 9 (Katou and Sakata); Vol. 2 (Katsura Naoyuki; Karuizawa); Vol. 4 Ch. 9 (Katou's fall through the Sakata land); Vol. 1 (Aki as Hikomaro's implied daughter by Saitou Keiko — which makes a Tokitou branch family a Keikain line in blood as well as name); Vol. 6 Ch. 3 (Kanbe; Mark's "your own country"), Ch. 9 (Keika Central Sakata; "return home in triumph"; Katou); this volume Ch. 3 (the Diet's Karafuto bills; Caesar).

## Chapter 6 — Imperial Gakushuukan Academy's Seven Wonders: The Zashiki Warashi of the Dormitories

**Narrative position:** Runa's first person, with long third-person sections from **Takahashi Akiko** and a closing scene seen by **Kanna Mizuki**; autumn 2003. The third "Seven Wonder," and the volume's supernatural climax: the academy is revealed as a national machine for making *zashiki warashi* out of dead children; Hotaru was born to repair it; Runa pays to destroy it. The chapter ends with Hotaru's grandfather — the "old man" of Ch. 1 — and the librarian who stands up to him.

**What happens**

*The dormitories*
- Runa learns the academy has dormitories, one per faction and sex (Kurimori Shizuka lives in the Lords' girls' dorm, Akiko in the Stewards'). The academy has over ten thousand students from elementary school to graduate school; the area around it is a student town.
- The associates rotate between Kudanshita and the Den-en-chofu manor; Keika has **bought an apartment building near the school** for guards and future high-school associates, with an escape route from the prefab. They use the prefab's rest room to build relationships — "one of our professional duties." Runa privately resolves to let any associate leave for a new connection: "if I ended up ruined—as I had in the game—there was no need for them to be at my side during my downfall."
- On a visit to Akiko's four-and-a-half-mat room, one cake goes missing from the Teisei truck Runa brought to bribe the dorm. "Oh, I bet the zashiki warashi ate it. That happens a lot." Everyone looks at Hotaru (holding whipped cream); falsely accused, she sets out to find the culprit.

*Kanna Mizuki's explanation*
- Zashiki warashi are "paradoxical": homes "needed a reason to explain why they prospered, so they came up with zashiki warashi" — in an age when a child's survival was a gamble.
- With a map of the academy (and site reports from real-estate and construction clients of the Kanna house), she finds many small shrines and Jizō statues — and "**more maternity and pediatrics departments here than in other neighborhoods.**"
- **The Kaihouin family** "used to serve as **personal mediums for the Keikain family**." Their gift is not prophecy but "the ability to decide whether a person's fate will consist of good luck or bad luck. That's how they can bend history to their will." (Her example: whether the bullet in Dallas in 1963 struck Kennedy.)
- "**This academy itself is a giant feng-shui magic circle.** The entire thing is intended to be seen as a single 'house' creating a constant supply of zashiki warashi. The source of such spirits is departed children—**mainly the souls of aborted fetuses and children who died before reaching adulthood. They used people like me to provide them.**" Women with no livelihood but their bodies bore the children of prominent families, and the dead children became the nation's luck: "That was how our country managed to make up for our industrial revolution taking place a hundred years late." The sorcerer who built it "had a direct hand in this academy's founding. And that was **Kaihouin-san's grandfather**."
- Runa, nauseated: "had they truly been willing to go that far?" Mizuki: "At the time, they believed this was the only way that Japan could catch up… It's actually pretty efficient." Runa repeats Caesar's line on good intentions: "When your industrial revolution comes a hundred years late, I'm sure there's a hefty bill to pay."
- The fault: **a church** stands where a shrine was, on a key point of the circle — a remnant of the Allies' campaign against State Shinto (in this world there were no direct GHQ orders, but the empire "was still seen as too dangerous to ignore"). A foreign spiritual system inserted into a "perfect feng-shui arrangement" causes "errors." Runa: "So these are like the errors that occur when you connect a PC to a slightly different operating system?" — "And those errors affect the server itself." — "Nooooo! It's Honami all over again." (Honami's real-world-style system failures continue under Teia.) Her lingering question: if the academy produces zashiki warashi, why was Hotaru meant to become one?

*Takahashi Akiko*
- "Takahashi Akiko was a girl with the wrong blank": **the wrong sex** (the most gifted of four children of a prefectural police chief, but a daughter to be married off); **the wrong age** (in any other year she would top her class; the Quartet are in hers); **the wrong friends** (charmed by Runa's kendo — "Ahh—what wasted talent with a sword"; "Let's compete again sometime." — "I don't want to. I'll just lose."); **the wrong location** (she turned down scholarships elsewhere).
- **Tachibana's offer**, with a bow: "I hope you'll remain friends with the young lady… It is incredibly important that she compete against members of the same sex." Her family and dojo were already surrounded. "Ahh… I'm the only one who can choose to move away from Keikain-san, aren't I?" — "As much as you can. If you do that, I'll guarantee you a job within the Keika Group." She accepts, muttering, "I just wanted to cross swords with Keikain-san…"
- A voice: "**Don't you ever wish that you could fix all those things you got wrong?… I can let you redo everything you messed up.**" Mizuki had coached her: say nothing, then "play along after a while. Then get it to tell you what it's really after"; spirits can punish actions, not thoughts; "Words are both their weapon and their weakness… If they want something from us, then it'll be something they can't do themselves."
- Akiko asks; it answers: "**Please convince Kaihouin-san to become a zashiki warashi again, so that she can guide this country to good fortune.**" In the bath she works it out: "That thing wants to possess me so that it can get close to Kaihouin-san." At the dorm's homemade shrine she prays to the real zashiki warashi; her slipper strikes **an old bell** — a ward against evil — which she wears from then on.

*The noisy way*
- Mizuki, seeing the bell, signals Runa by writing in the air; they skip class with **Katsuki Shiori** ("Her story's already finished, so she can see those 'things'"). Through **the Cintamani stone** (which the Kanna master told Runa always to wear) they see a small child behind Mizuki in the prefab rest room — "if I'd gotten pregnant here, that child would've been mine" — and, on the sports field, zashiki warashi **guarding Akiko** and **blocking Hotaru** from approaching her.
- Runa grasps it: if the circle is a glitching system, **Hotaru is the "patch."** "Are you telling me this is what Hotaru-chan was born for?! They'd really go that far to improve this country?!" Her deepest anger: "**we'd never been offered that sort of salvation in my past life.**" Mizuki: what will you do? — "**Destroy it, of course.**… I couldn't just sit back and let my friend be sacrificed for the country's sake." Quiet way or noisy way? "Let's make as much noise as possible."
- **The operation:** a newspaper item reports that a church near the academy, built after the war on land that had been a State Shinto shrine, will move to a donated plot (alumni pay; over ¥1 billion) and the shrine be rebuilt. Asuka's father, **Representative Kasugano Yousuke**, newly a **Ministry of Education** official after the election, handles it with Runa's money; the new shrine will honour a local god to avoid any charge of reviving State Shinto; **Kanna Sera** mobilises the whole Kanna house to repair the circle (Runa pays them over ¥1 billion). "If I didn't use it now, when would I ever use it?" Runa identifies the circle's builder as Hotaru's grandfather or great-grandfather, and realises that Hotaru's old wish "to be sacrificed to the gods" meant being killed.
- At the demolition site, Mizuki: zashiki warashi are almost all seven or younger (hence Shichi-Go-San); Hotaru, a junior-high student, would already have vanished — "I'm really grateful for those mandarins." The zashiki warashi "want Kaihouin-san to be happy" and kept her from joining them, and watched the thing possessing Akiko.
- Runa's choice: **neutralise the circle and hold a memorial service for the zashiki warashi** rather than restore it. "This country has come a long way since the Meiji Restoration… we'll get by just fine without the zashiki warashi. At least, I think we will." Mizuki: "Maybe you're just being sentimental." Runa: "**whenever I'm defeated, or if I have my back against the wall, I won't take any of you down with me.**"
- Mizuki: blood is the strongest impurity — menstruation has made Hotaru fully human. Hotaru, in tears: "**Asuka-chan. Runa-chan… I'm sorry. I can't be anything but human now…**"
- News: the Diet deliberates three Karafuto bills — the **Worker Dispatch amendment**, the **Karafuto Special Zone Act** (soon to a committee vote) and the **Privy Council Reformation Act** (stonewalled by the Privy Council); "twenty-four million citizens of the former Northern Japanese regime"; analysts cite nearly a million excess workers; with the zaibatsu broken up, foreign "activist shareholders" press for restructuring. Runa, at her window: "We have **three and a half million unemployed and twenty-four million** former Northern Japanese citizens… That's a pretty heavy weight." Mizuki had told her the country would likely do better if the circle kept producing zashiki warashi; Runa "I'd chosen to stay with Hotaru-chan rather than receiving magical or supernatural protection," and must bear the consequences.

*Later developments*
- Akiko's bell falls silent. Mizuki: the possessor was "a spirit that failed to become a zashiki warashi… **someone must've taken that spirit's desire to live longer and turned it into something malicious.**" Three wonders down (mirror, letters, zashiki warashi), four to come; "**Something is going this far to reach the people close to Keikain-san.**" Without Hotaru's power, Mizuki must lead.
- She recruits Akiko as a sword — "don't you ever get the urge to cut into an evil spirit with a blade?" — with a blank cheque for "the finest weapon available." And reveals the "quiet way": **a sniper from Runa's private army would have shot Akiko** near-fatally, with medics standing by, to drive the spirit out. Runa recoiled and chose the way where "the only price to pay was in money" — though whether that was "of Runa's own free will," Mizuki will not say. Akiko accepts: "If a supporting actor keeps up the fight, they can upstage a lead actor." To Hotaru: "Thank you. This time, it will be my turn to make it up to you."

*Elder Kaihouin*
- At the central library, **Hotaru's grandfather**, head of the Kaihouin house, rages at **Head Librarian Takamiya Haruka**: "My granddaughter was tricked! Our entire family wanted to bring peace to the country, and now it's all for nothing!" Takamiya: "**Students at this school are allowed to choose their own future!** How dare you insult that girl's choice instead of celebrating it?!"
- Mizuki steps in (reading the change of experts — from State Shinto, Buddhism and feng shui to "Western-style magic" — as "a transfer of power being carried out among Japan's upper classes"). Takamiya catches his raised hand.

The traitor's daughter!**… Don't you have any idea what **the late Duke Keikain** was trying to accomplish, little girl?! Japan's future is ruined! **The dream of hegemony is no more!**" Runa, enjoying it: "Is that some sort of problem?… A traitor and a whore teamed up to harm their country's future… I'll make sure to harm this country's future, just as your curse detailed. No matter what I do, this country will still be…" — Takamiya pulls her into her arms: "**Don't let the past hold you captive, Keikain-san. Don't look at the future in despair. Runa and Asuka offer to destroy him with money and Papa; Takamiya: "Have you forgotten that that man is Kaihouin-san's grandfather?" ## Chapter 7 — Professor Kanbe's Grown-Up Sociology Classes

**Narrative position:** Runa's first person; autumn 2003 into the winter. Because Narita's aftermath makes it hard for her to visit the college, Runa pays for **Kanbe** to lecture at the academy; the lecture hall fills with her friends, associates and the children of the elite. Eight short lectures, each with a provocative title, each followed by a reaction. The chapter is the volume's essay: a course in how power works, written for children who will hold it.

**What happens**

*1. "Buyout theory"*
- Kanbe shares Runa's favourite Machiavelli line — "The best way to get to heaven is to study the roads to hell." A redevelopment contract of **¥10 billion at 30% profit = ¥3 billion**. Paying the rival's project manager (salary ¥10 million) and a Diet member a total of **¥100 million** leaves ¥2.9 billion; without the bribe, the rival bids ¥9.9 billion and to win you must bid ¥9.8 billion, cutting profit to ¥2.8 billion — "doesn't it seem logical to spend a hundred million on a bribe instead?" (Runa starts to object — "It's thirty percent of 9.8 billion yen, so…" — and stays quiet. *An economics note:* Kanbe's figure is right if costs are fixed at ¥7 billion, so every yen cut from the bid comes straight off profit; Runa's objection assumes a constant 30% margin, which would give ¥2.94 billion. The professor's version is the realistic one for a fixed-scope project.) Bribery scandals surface when the payer short-changes or the recipient splurges — "So make sure to be more careful than them, okay?"
- "Most people aren't saints… Capitalism can't deny such desires… without any rules, no one but the rich would ever be able to win… You must all maintain a delicate balance as you interact with those who tread in gray areas."

*2. "Treason theory"*
- "What part of your country would you be willing to sell?" — its will, its domestic policy. A state that sets its own will and makes others follow is a "hegemonic state"; neutrality between overwhelming powers is foolish; samurai houses split their bets (Nanboku-chō).
- Japan "continually sold" itself: to **Britain** (the Sino-Japanese and Russo-Japanese wars, World War I), then to **Germany** ("That's why we suffered so much in the Second World War"), then to **the United States** under the Pax Americana.
- **The chance of 1989**, and the in-world timeline written on the board: 1989 Berlin Wall; 1990 Gulf War; 1991 the Soviet fall; 1992 Maastricht; **1993 an opposition coalition government; 1994 the fall of Northern Japan and unification**; 1995 the Hanshin earthquake and a new-religious-movement terror attack; 1996 a Fellowship prime minister again; 1997 Hong Kong's return, the Asian crisis, bad-debt disposal; 1998 the Russian crisis; 1999 the IT bubble and the euro; 2001 Koizumi and 9/11. At each point Japan did not try to challenge American hegemony — leaders who remembered the war "never wanted… to challenge the United States in a war again."
- "**What's so bad about second place?** You can call it a ceasefire, an armistice, a surrender… our country was defeated by the United States in the Pacific War." To Runa: "Would you gamble the one hundred and forty million people of this country… to challenge a hegemonic nation—all so that you could carry out your own will?" — "**No, I wouldn't. Just like Prime Minister Koizumi, I'm quite pleased with my status and way of life as they are now.**"

*3. "Theory of equality"*
- "People are not all equal… That's why society is obliged to present itself as impartial." Fukuzawa's "Heaven does not create any man above or below another" continues "It is the pursuit of knowledge that separates the wise from the fools" (from the *Jitsugokyō*): *An Encouragement of Learning* means "make sure that you study properly." What lifted a late-industrialising Japan to second place? "**Greed**" — now called **capitalism**. Capitalism beat socialism by changing what equality means: **equality of opportunity** instead of **equality of outcome**; the word that affirms these disparities is **neoliberalism** — "**We'll offer opportunities but never lower the bar.**"
- Runa: "the magic word that had sent the country into turmoil in my past life—**the concept that had cut down everyone who was weak, including myself.**" Caesar again. Nozuki Misaki: isn't that what the hardcore players say in their online game?

*4. "Hometown theory"*
- Four songs, with lyrics: a 1977 letter from an elder brother who stayed home to siblings in the city; a 1983 song of a girl with only a middle-school education leaving a hostile hometown (1985: the Plaza Accord); a 1993 song of an Osaka woman who stays in Tokyo after love ends (the coalition government, the rice shortage); and a recent hit.
- Depopulation followed the shift from primary to secondary industry and the energy revolution — "**charcoal → coal → petroleum**." Television, broadcasting from Tokyo, "seemingly eliminated cultural differences" and made **Tokyo the nation's hometown**; today's TV cries "Eliminate all wasteful public-works projects!" and "Give money to the cities, not rural areas!" (which is why Keika's Tokyo projects are spared criticism). Asuka goes pale.
- Runa: "Tokyo really has become our hometown… the current me, Keikain Runa, was one of those people who'd made Tokyo their hometown." She invites the boys to see the last song's setting, though "the cherry blossoms aren't blooming right now."

*5. "Political peddlers"*
- A "political peddler" is, properly, "**a talented politician who is only interested in self-preservation and personal profit**" — "someone valuable to have in your pocket." The ideal is the "**well-and-fence politician**," who spends his fortune on the nation until only the well and the fence are left. "Isn't that the kind of politician you aspire to be?… **Would your family, your friends, and your loved ones approve of that path?**" (He looks at Runa.) Koizumi, she thinks, is such a man — "incredibly respectable… also incredibly lonely."
- "People motivated by their own convictions, rather than by profit, are a nuisance… Many geniuses throughout history had their talents siphoned away by the people around them." The narration recalls that Kanbe's televised stance is that "**the sacrifice of a million children is beneficial to the country if that sacrifice brings in a single god.**" A mass society should mass-produce competent "peddlers" rather than wait for geniuses; postwar politics "developed somewhere between selfishness and selflessness."
- Asuka asks about TV politicians on late-night shows. Kanbe: they turn talk-show opinion into "problems" for the government — "This cycle of justice is intoxicating" — such as attacks on rural public works; they are "**political actors**."

*6. "Violence theory"*
- With her guards present: the **Detective Industry Act**, the **amended Security Industry Act** and **a bounty system for criminal investigations** have let the elite expand private security and permit "certain varieties of preemptive strikes" — bounty hunters and PMCs as "swords and shields for the well-off," gated communities, as in the US and Europe since 2001.
- **Iraq, autumn 2003 (in-world):** nearly **100,000 PMC personnel**; the Kurdish north fighting Turkey; **Iran "slowly creeping into the southern Shia region"**; a Sunni guerrilla war in the centre against multinational forces including **160,000 Americans**; Washington "**seriously considered the use of nuclear weapons in Iraq**"; a "realistic" plan to **split Iraq in three** — give the north to Turkey, make the stable south independent, and so let **the British Army and the JSDF stop fighting Iran's military** and move to the centre — before the president's re-election.
- "Nations generally don't allow other nations to exist within them. Once that happens, you've regressed back to the Middle Ages… **You don't want to blow up when the time isn't right.**" Runa reads it as **a government warning** to the nobility, now "tremendously despised" over the Karafuto scandal. Justification matters because "there will always be third parties watching you impartially."
- Kitagumo Ryouko: talking "doesn't work against zealots… **If you're going to crush someone, do it without drawing any attention to yourself.**" She has heard that the mafia sent hitmen after Karafuto culprits sheltering with nobles, and that her film has inspired would-be "allies of justice" — copycat "Keikain Runas" — in the scandal.

*7. "Outsider theory"*
- **Shinpan** (the shogun's kin, eligible to succeed but kept out of government), **fudai** (hereditary vassals who ran the shogunate on small fiefs), **tozama** (outsiders with great domains far from power). The shogunate held about a third of the country — enough to win with one ally. The Taira and the Hōjō, who each held half of Japan, were overthrown when the outsiders united. Modern firms are mostly tozama: "how to turn tozama into shinpan or fudai, how to control your tozama, and if you're a tozama, how to prevent yourself from being purged…"
- Runa places herself: she is a shinpan to the main Keikain house (hence Father's and Nakamaro's worry); Mio, Asuka and Hotaru are her shinpan; Yuka a fudai; the associates tozama on the way to fudai; the Keika Group itself "nothing more than a tozama daimyo." The narration: "the GHQ helped us finish things up after the Pacific War, but **they'd never ruled over us in this world**."
- Yuka: the associates could not run the group; tozama should be divided, not driven together. Runa hears a phantom of the game — **Takanashi Mizuho** pleading to the student body about discrimination against scholarship students, the commoner "tozama" uniting under their "emperor" to defeat Runa, keeper of vested interests. "**What chance would I ever have of defeating someone like that…?**"

*8. "Fool theory"*
- Recorded votes punish changes of position — American legislators who voted for the Gulf War and against Iraq; a governor with no war votes leads the Democratic primaries. Kanbe holds up ***Don Quixote***: in Part II the world, having read Part I, comes to honour the unchanged fool. **Koizumi** has called for postal privatisation since **1979**, was called a "crank," became Minister of Posts and Telecommunications in December 1992, and never wavered; people support him "just like the characters who supported Don Quixote." "You can watch the waves of the times form and keep riding them, or you can wait like a fool for a wave to come to you."
- Runa: "As someone who rides the waves, I can't change course now… **if you try to get on the wave and miss, you'll crash and burn.**" Watching Kanna Mizuki leave on a boy's arm, she remembers her question — "what if there was an option C as well?" — and thinks: "Perhaps true happiness meant having enough strength to avoid those modern waves in the first place… if he'd never become a knight in the first place, he might have achieved an ordinary kind of happiness."

**Revelations**
- **In-world chronology:** the opposition coalition in 1993; **Northern Japan's fall and unification in 1994**; Aum-type terrorism in 1995; the Fellowship back in power in 1996. Japan was defeated by the US in the Pacific War but **never occupied**.
- **In-world Iraq, late 2003:** Iran in the south, Turkey in the north, a Sunni insurgency; JSDF and British troops **fighting Iran's forces**; Washington again considering nuclear use; a partition plan.
- Japan's new security and bounty laws have created a private-violence market; the government is warning the nobles through Kanbe.
- Kanbe's public view on "the sacrifice of a million children" for "a single god."
- Runa says she would not risk 140 million people to challenge American hegemony — "just like Prime Minister Koizumi."

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** cartels; the MMO = ***Ragnarok Online***; the songs = **"Kakashi"** (Sada Masashi, 1977), **"Fight!"** (Nakajima Miyuki, 1983), **"Tokyo"** (Yashiki Takajin, 1993) and **"Sakura Zaka"** (Fukuyama Masaharu, 2000); the **Plaza Accord** (1985) and the yen's rise into the bubble; "I'll kill you if you underestimate me" from ***Bandit: Giden Taiheiki*** (Kawabe Masamichi); the Democratic primaries' surging governor of Vermont (**Howard Dean**), beaten in Iowa.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **This is the series' civics curriculum, delivered to the children who will hold power.** Every lecture is a lesson about the world Runa already operates in — bribes, alignment, inequality, hometowns, politicians, violence, factions, persistence — given now to her whole circle. Kanbe's method (teach the road to hell to avoid it) is the series' own.

It restates Vol. 5's verdict (Koizumi over Ishihara) in her own voice — and sets her against the Kaihouins' "dream of hegemony" (Ch. 6).

The series is fair to the doctrine — it is how the West won — and honest about who paid.

Her fear — "What chance would I ever have of defeating someone like that…?" — is the first time she fears the heroine.

## Chapter 8 — School Festival: Junior High Edition

**Narrative position:** Runa's first person; the three-day autumn school festival of 2003. The longest stretch of pure respite in the volume — homeroom votes, inter-class bargaining, a maid café, yakisoba, a charity performance of *Madama Butterfly*, an auction, fireworks — with the volume's darkest private thought placed at its centre.

**What happens**

*Planning*
- Junior-high classes may now run shops. The rules (Mitsuya, events committee): only approved suppliers, no cooking, canned juice served unopened, sweets in sealed bags — "Rules were always made because some idiot had done something stupid in the past." Economics: juice bought at ¥50 and sold at ¥100; sweet bread bought at ¥100 and sold at ¥200; past sales of about 400 cans and 300 breads over three days give **¥50,000 profit, ¥1,500 a student**; the beautification committee will also pay each class ¥10,000 to collect rubbish. In the game, Takanashi Mizuho used the festival to earn money ("A young maiden needed money to be fashionable and go on dates").
- Runa muses on doubling up as café worker and paid cleaner, then checks herself: "The school festival's true value was having everyone chip in, work together, and build shared memories."
- Class 1-A (Runa, Eiichi) votes for a café (27 votes). At the committee meeting three classes want haunted houses; B and C merge and win it; **Asuka's 1-E** is pushed to a café — after Runa's, which filed first. Asuka converts defeat into a deal: 1-E supplies storage, workers and budget for **one joint café and one after-party** — "Let's get the details in writing with the executive committee as witnesses!"
- Runa is to perform at a **charity concert with the Teia International Philharmonic** on the second evening — the festival's main event. Her choice: an aria from ***Madama Butterfly***.
- The night before: her used maid uniforms from **Vesuna**, her Kudanshita maid café, mended by hand (Keiko files them as donations); Hotaru drifting "just like a zashiki warashi"; Shiori acting as class secretary; Mizuki pleased to have the prefab.
- Mizuki: "you really go out of your way to participate in this stuff." Runa: "This is the sort of thing we won't get to experience again after we grow up… Growing up meant forgetting all the fun times like these. As someone who'd experienced that myself, I found these moments even more precious." She recalls a film's demon asking, "**would you make this moment last forever if you could?**" Eiichi, passing: "**No way. Life's only fun because you get to keep changing, right? Whatever happens tomorrow, it'll still be fun if I get to spend it with you, Yuujirou, and Mitsuya.**" — "You idiot!"

*The festival*
- **"Pure," the maid café:** an hour-long queue for Runa in a maid uniform; the line-managers announce her shift times; Asuka has scheduled "the most beautiful girls" on every shift and stocked double the usual supplies. Runa crosses the balcony for supplies and her "world debut as a maid" becomes the café's advertisement. Rudaka Miu: "Being a maid is all about stamina!" When Runa's shift ends, the queue vanishes.
- The kendo club's yakisoba stall: Akiko, wearing her silent bell, winks and sends Yuujirou off on his break. A storeroom feast of festival food: "I'm incredibly grateful for the food in my life." Mitsuya: "you're quite a commoner. I mean that in a good way."
- Collecting rubbish with Mitsuya ("It's important not to forget these sorts of things when you're a leader. It was CEO Ichijou… who taught me that"), a paparazzo's flash; Mitsuya steps in front of her and warns him off in English; **Anisha** apologises; Runa: "This is the risk that arises with tolerance. Please don't be too hard on them."

*"Un bel dì, vedremo"*
- She sings "Un bel dì, vedremo" in a full **oiran** costume (twenty kilos, stripped to four or five): "a blonde-haired oiran." The hair ornaments are lent by **Kanna Sera** and **Saitou Keiko** — "the pair actually knew each other." Both had been "first-rate hostesses." The concert becomes a staged opera with TV cameras (admitted, secretly, so the audience can be legally filmed for security); police arrest ticket scalpers.
- Runa recounts the plot (a US naval officer, a Japanese "wife," a child, an American wife, suicide) and its era — Mori Ōgai's "The Dancing Girl" (1890), the "Madame Butterfly" story (1898), the opera (1904), the Sino-Japanese and Russo-Japanese wars — "the time period when Japan was pushing forward to 'enrich the country.'"
- In her dressing room lies **a real dagger** — Mizuki's, for warding off spirits; it would react if Shizuka were still possessed; it does not.
- Walking to the stage: "Madama Butterfly would end up plunging that knife into her chest at the end of the story… **You were lucky, Madama Butterfly. You got to die of your own free will… I imagined that Keikain Runa never got that opportunity.** I would channel those feelings of pity and nostalgia into my song of mourning—both for Madama Butterfly and for Keikain Runa."

*The auction*
- Day three with **Amane Mio**. The festival flea market and auction sell the goods of families ruined by the bubble (the cursed vanity of Ch. 2 was to have been auctioned here). Mio's father, the Keikains' art dealer, buys an art-deco desk for Mio and suggests that Runa start a collection: "if you have a 'Keika Collection' of your own, your name will live on in art history."
- She falls for **a Tiffany stained-glass lamp**: expected price ¥150,000–300,000. He bids for her and explains the trade's logic — a dealer bids up to the resale price; only those "simply captivated" go beyond it. Sold at **¥300,000**. Holding a ¥100 water balloon, she sees the gap between herself and ordinary people. "I really love art nouveau… let's start with a modest hundred million." Mio: "A hundred million is 'modest' for a junior high schooler…" — the first item of the **Keikain Collection**.

*The bonfire*
- At the joint after-party Runa stands by the wall "savoring the end of the festival": "someone once said that we should calculate our actions numerically. We only have two school festivals left in junior high, or five total."
- Eiichi: "**You should only feel satisfied once your time is up.**" — **Shibusawa Eiichi**, after whom he was named. Fireworks. "**There aren't 'only' five festivals left, Runa. There are five more festivals to look forward to.** And we'll make the next ones better and better. I know the two of us can do that."
- Runa: the boys' teasing makes her happy, "all the more heartbreaking knowing that **I would have to leave them someday**." "This life I'd been born into was just like a festival. It would be a waste not to enjoy it." Eiichi offers his hand — "Be my escort, okay?" — "Of course, my lady." She keeps the group photo "like a treasure."

**Revelations**
- **Runa believes the game's Keikain Runa did not die by her own choice** — her pity for Butterfly ("You got to die of your own free will") is the first explicit statement of how the game's villainess ended.
- Kanna Sera and Saitou Keiko knew each other as top hostesses.
- The **Keikain Collection** begins (art nouveau; ¥100 million budget).
- Runa owns a maid café, Vesuna, in Kudanshita.
- Eiichi was named after Shibusawa Eiichi (Runa remembers the game had a scene revealing it).

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** Puccini's ***Madama Butterfly*** ("famous for the death of its soprano character"); Mori Ōgai's **"The Dancing Girl"** (*Maihime*; an elite Japanese in Germany abandons his lover for his career); the lamp = **Tiffany**; the film = ***Urusei Yatsura 2: Beautiful Dreamer*** (Oshii Mamoru, 1984), whose dream-demon offers an endless school-festival eve.
- **Historically real, used accurately:** "Un bel dì, vedremo" (Act II); John Luther Long's story (1898) and Belasco's play before Puccini's 1904 premiere; *Maihime* (1890); the Sino-Japanese (1894–95) and Russo-Japanese (1904–05) wars; *fukoku kyōhei* ("enrich the country"); oiran costume; art deco and art nouveau; Tiffany Studios lamps; auction dynamics (dealers bid to resale value); Japanese school-festival rules on food safety; **Shibusawa Eiichi** (1840–1931), "father of Japanese capitalism."
- **Unverified:** the exact Shibusawa quotation ("You should only feel satisfied once your time is up") — I have not traced it to a source.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The "Beautiful Dreamer" question is the volume's — and the series' — temptation.** A dream in which the festival eve never ends is exactly what Runa, who knows how the next five years end, might want. Eiichi refuses it for her: life is fun "because you get to keep changing." The romance advances by his rejecting stasis.
- *Strongly supported:* **The Butterfly line is the darkest sentence in the volume.** A heroine who chooses her death is "lucky"; the game's Runa was denied even that. It explains the household's fear (Ch. 6: "I won't take any of you down with me") and her reluctance to promise her friends anything about the future.
- *Strongly supported:* **Respite is the point.** The chapter is built from small economies — ¥1,500 profit, a ¥10,000 cleaning contract, a ¥100 water balloon — set against her ¥300,000 lamp and "modest" ¥100 million; the novel loves the small ones more. "This life I'd been born into was just like a festival."
- *Plausible:* **Butterfly's era is Japan's catch-up era** — the same "enrich the country" whose hidden price Ch. 6 revealed (the zashiki warashi). A Japanese woman abandoned by a Western officer is the period's own allegory of a late-developing nation.
- *Plausible:* **The Keikain Collection** answers Vol. 6's "patronizing culture" (Holland Town, the Choufuu Council foundation) with something personal — art bought because it captivated her, not because it laundered or protected anything.
- *Speculative:* **"I would have to leave them someday"** may be a decision, not a fear: she may intend to separate herself from them before 2008 so as not to drag them down (Ch. 6).

**Connections:** the game (a scene, Runa recalls, "just like that" explained Eiichi's name); Vol. 1 Ch. 8 (the concert; Micaëla's aria sung to Eiichi); Vol. 2 Ch. 4 (*La Traviata*, the Queen of the Night; the student council); Vol. 4 Ch. 4 (the festival committees); Vol. 5 Ch. 8 (Eiichi's proposals); Vol. 6 Ch. 3 ("I couldn't help wanting to become an adult with Eiichi-kun and my friends"), Ch. 4 (Machiyoi Sanae: "a duty to be happy"; the Teia Philharmonic); this volume Ch. 2 (the vanity and Shizuka), Ch. 4 (Kanna Sera, the Kanna house), Ch. 6 (Akiko's bell; "I won't take any of you down with me").

## Chapter 9 — The Police Officers' Banquet

**Narrative position:** Third person; a private back room in a police bar in Tokyo, winter 2003–04 (spring personnel moves are being settled "informally until winter"). Runa does not appear. A short chapter in which the police reveal two things the household has kept from her: a new prosecution of Tachibana over her father's death, and a noble "fixer" in the Metropolitan Police.

**What happens**
- **Superintendent Maefuji Shouichi**, **Inspector Natsume Kentarou** and **Inspector Ono Kenichi** drink in the back room of a bar that was "a great place to talk about anything and everything."
- **Personnel moves:** Ono will be promoted to **superintendent and deputy chief of the Kojimachi police station** — "person in charge of handling the young lady," the man who will take the heat if she causes trouble; he will retire as a station chief. Natsume, after his second letter of apology, goes to the **Kudanshita police box**. Maefuji stays director of the Third Foreign Affairs Section but is seconded to **the joint Japan–US–Russia team on the Karafuto case**, and may become **temporary director of the Karafuto Prefectural Police** for some years; he has used his remaining influence to place his friends.
- Career officers now avoid the young lady's detail (after "Fushimi Inari Shrine" and Narita); Ono will recruit non-career officers. The Americans will send "trainees" who are really **Secret Service**: losing "a poster girl for the war on terror" would affect Iraq, so the president imposes "forced kindness." The CIA, FBI and Secret Service are fighting turf wars in Japan over the Karafuto case.
- With Natsume out of the room (he cannot hold his drink — one reason for choosing this bar), Maefuji comes to the point: **the Tokyo prosecutors' Special Investigations Department** is investigating "**something even further back. It's about her father. He committed suicide over secret collaborations with the Eastern Bloc, and now they're saying that the young lady's butler, Tachibana Ryuuji-shi, was involved in his death.**"
- If noble privileges are abolished, Tachibana could be arrested. "He's the last walking encyclopedia of Showa postwar history." There were whispers that **the prime minister** wanted to bring him down over the **Sakata land dealings**; the prosecutors, "having lost him once before," are eager; and an angle exists to paint "the young lady could just be a puppet controlled by Tachibana-san" — making Tachibana look like the controller of the whole Keikain house. (The prosecutors had expected the Keikains not to shield him in Vol. 4; they did.) Maefuji: new documents on the Eastern-bloc collusion case may surface in the Karafuto investigation — "Knowing him, he's probably kept his hands clean, but please tell the others anyway."
- Ono: her security staff warn that "some idiots might try to attack nobles in order to bring about '**world reformation**'"; the administration is warning people unofficially. Maefuji: "Some of them deserve to get their own world 'reformed'… Oh, excuse me. We're still police officers." Ono: "I don't think that the little miss would try to reform the world, like someone would in a movie."
- **Douanji Harumichi**, a baron and superintendent, the Metropolitan Police's **director of special records** — "the guide through the labyrinth that is Sakurada Gate" — is introduced. His department "set things straight with the general public whenever a noble used their diplomatic immunity to escape a crime"; street police call its staff "snakes." Ono once screamed at them. Douanji: "There's trouble involving the Keikain Dukedom, and I want you to help me deal with it." Ono accepts — "my rule is always to accept the gifts offered to me. And Natsume's too young for this job." Maefuji: "**We don't want Tachibana-san to attract attention.**"
- A few days later, at dawn, Ono on patrol chats with **Tachibana**, sweeping the pavement outside Kudanshita Tower — "no one realized that… Inspector Ono and Tachibana were exchanging information."

**Revelations**
- **Prosecutors are reopening Otsumaro's death and Tachibana's part in it**, with the abolition of noble immunity as the lever; Koizumi is rumoured to be behind it, via the Sakata land affair.
- The Metropolitan Police have a noble-run department that manages the public consequences of noble immunity, and it now needs Ono's help with "trouble involving the Keikain Dukedom."
- The US is sending Secret Service agents disguised as trainees; American agencies are fighting over the Karafuto case.
- Maefuji may run Karafuto's police; Ono becomes the official handler of "the young lady"; Ono and Tachibana have a back channel.

**Historical references**
- **Historically real, used accurately:** the Tokyo District Public Prosecutors Office's **Special Investigation Department** (*Tokusōbu*), which handles political corruption; "Sakuradamon" as metonym for the Metropolitan Police HQ; Japanese police practice of settling spring transfers informally in winter; career (*kyaria*) versus non-career officers.
- **Fictional / in-world:** the special-records department; Karafuto Prefectural Police; the joint investigation.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The Otsumaro question is coming due.** Vol. 2 Ch. 7 said Tachibana "pushed him to suicide"; Vol. 6 Ch. 3 said the ruling party drove him to it to shield Katou; Vol. 7 Ch. 5 showed Ichijou's career rising from the same scandal. Now the state wants the answer, and the abolition of noble immunity would let it take Tachibana. The "trouble involving the Keikain Dukedom" and Maefuji's "We don't want Tachibana-san to attract attention" point the same way.
- *Strongly supported:* **The adults again keep the most important thing from her.** The chapter is told without her; the information passes to Tachibana in a dawn chat on the pavement. Runa chose ignorance about Narita (Vol. 6 Ch. 8); here she is not offered the choice.
- *Plausible:* **"World reformation"** is the movie-driven vigilantism of Ch. 7 aimed at the nobles — the series' fear of a new "restoration" in a popular form.
- *Plausible:* **Koizumi's reforms and the prosecution are the same pressure.** Abolishing noble immunity (Ch. 3) is the "showpiece" bill; the case against Tachibana is what it would make possible.

**Connections:** Vol. 1 Ch. 2 (Otsumaro's scandal and death), Ch. 4 (Katou's complicity); Vol. 2 Ch. 7 (Tachibana "pushed him to suicide"); Vol. 3 (Runa spirited away at Fushimi Inari); Vol. 4 Ch. 9 (the dawn raid; noble immunity; the Sakata land); Vol. 6 Ch. 3 (Nakamaro's second account), Ch. 7 (Natsume's apology; the joint investigation); this volume Ch. 3 (the immunity bill), Ch. 7 (Kanbe's violence lecture; "allies of justice"), Ch. 10 (Natsume and Ono's transfers seen from Runa's side).

## Chapter 10 — Giving Thanks to the Fox God

**Narrative position:** Runa's first person; winter 2003–04. After the church's removal (Ch. 6) the academy needs a god for its rebuilt shrine; Runa, Kanna Mizuki and Hotaru go to Kyoto to thank the fox god who once spirited Runa away at Fushimi Inari (Vol. 3 Ch. 10). Between the theology lessons and the epiphany sits a business-magazine digest that drops the volume's biggest financial revelation in passing.

**What happens**

*Which god?*
- Mizuki wants "a god that has some connection to you." Local gods' names were mostly lost for two reasons: the **Meiji anti-Buddhist movement** (temples, which kept the records and served as schools and local government, were split from shrines, and the records went with them; State Shinto then exalted certain gods and the Allies later abolished it), and — she adds on the train — **bubble-era development**, which removed shrines and renamed places ("The Kannas make good money from respectable land dealings like those"). Developers came to see their bad debts as divine punishment.
- Gods "went on to become **franchises**": villages attached their gods to the lineages of the heavenly (*amatsukami*) and earthly (*kunitsukami*) gods to assert dominance. A quiz: at **Usa Jingū** (Usa Hachimangū), Hachiman is not in the grand central hall — **Hime-ōkami** is; Hachiman is on the left. Runa: honne and tatemae. Mizuki learned it all from Hotaru (Runa does not want to know how).

*The magazine digest*
- **Keika Resource Development** pays **¥140 billion** of debt to acquire **Imperial Metals and Chemicals** (a ferroalloy maker in court reorganisation since 2002; parent **Hachitomi Steel**; main bank Honami), to be supported through the Sakata refinery.
- **Keika Pacific Ferry** (Sapporo; part of the group since Hokkaido Kaitaku Bank) buys **Hokkaido Ferry** for **¥70 billion** — the largest carrier on the Hokkaido–Karafuto route, ruined by bubble-era hotels in Tomakomai, a ski resort in Yonezawa and a new Niigata–Fukuoka route — taking all its debts on condition that every founder resigns. "Hokkaido has truly become Keika's second home."
- **Moonlight Resorts** takes on the **Tomamu** resort's liabilities (given as "twenty-two million" and then ¥22 billion); **Keika Hotels** takes **Suikan Hotel**'s ¥34 billion. Hokkaido is reviving as the stopover between Honshu and Karafuto, with Karafuto migrants swelling Wakkanai and Asahikawa; Moonlight Resorts runs gated communities abroad and **Moonlight Resort Yubari**, where "many members of the military can be spotted." Runa's total: ¥266 billion — "nothing major" beside the ¥6 trillion she made in the summer.
- **The exposé on Keika Resource Development:** few know that Keika Corp's resource division, merged with Gulf Oil Development, was "the core of the Moonlight Fund"; insiders expected the Fund to become the group's holding company, so placing it under Keika Corp suggests **Keika Corp now leads the group**. The trio running the group: **Tachibana** (Keika Railway; stripped of representative rights over suspected tax evasion in the Sakata land deals), **Ichijou** (Keika Holdings; rumoured to be losing power as the Fund moves away; "no solid foundation of support"; hurrying to hand over to **Senior Director Angela Sullivan**), **Toudou** (Keika Corp).
- "**Another mystery is why Keika Resource Development, despite being a subsidiary of Keika Corp, only owns 49.9 percent of its shares, while the remaining 50.1 percent are held by a Swiss private bank.** Even the name of the account that holds them has not been made public. It's speculated that this account must belong to someone within the Keikain family, and **it's hard to ignore the rumblings in Russia that the account actually contains the Romanov family's secret assets…**"
- Runa reads scavenger logic in it — her rescues as waste. "Still…I want to save as many people as I can. Does that make me arrogant?" Mizuki: "Selfishness is like any other accessory for a girl. As long as you find a gentleman you love who accepts that sort of thing, what's the big deal?"
- Divine punishment "comes in the form of natural disasters": a riverside shrine marks flooding; to avoid it, read the land records and local histories.

*To Kyoto*
- Aki stays in Tokyo (she was there when Runa vanished last time). The police reshuffle of Ono and Natsume has convinced the Japanese side "that some god must be trying to lure me in"; a note goes to the Kyoto police: "Whatever happens, we won't blame you." The Kannas helped arrange the reshuffle — a sign of their political reach.
- Mizuki and Hotaru veto enshrining the **Cintamani stone** at the school ("you'll lose something that protects you" while the Seven Wonders escalate); Hotaru proposes thanking the fox god with inari sushi.
- The full guard: Yuka, Nanami, Yuna, Misaki, Irina, Glasya, Yulia; Nakajima, Ryouko and Anisha waiting in Kyoto. **Eva:** "I've even brought in supernatural researchers from my home country, and **satellites** are standing by… We won't lose sight of you." — "Whose satellites?" — "…No comment." America's agencies are at war over Karafuto Bank; the Pentagon and State are furious about Narita; the new **Department of Homeland Security** is horrified that a "spiriting away" could happen to someone so important; fewer than a battalion's worth of Americans are in Kyoto disguised as tourists.

*Dakiniten*
- Near Kyoto, in the empty Shinkansen car, the fox god in a white kimono and fox mask: "**If you will worship me for all of your life, then I shall grant you happiness.**" ("That sounds more like a demon's contract.") Hotaru appears and shakes her head. "Someone who failed to become a god thinks that she can defy me?"
- Mizuki: "I'd say it's pretty childish of you to try to run away with Keikain-san, **Dakiniten**-sama." (Dakiniten, a flesh-eating Buddhist sky-demon who in Japan merged with Inari — "worshipped as the goddess of love and sexuality," in Mizuki's account.) The god, delighted with Mizuki — "You're my favorite type" — offers a choice of **a sword, a bundle of rice, or a sickle** ("Every item before you comes with a fitting advantage. But this is a choice a human must make, not a god").
- The sickle "is for warding off evil. Long ago, they used that tool to stop the dead from coming back to life." Runa chooses it: "I'm already very prosperous… I have all the power I need too… **The whole reason I came here was to save Hotaru-chan and the departed children, so I believe the sickle is the best choice.**"
- Gifts: **Hariti's pomegranate seeds** for Mizuki — "Plant these at your shrine and summon her. She'll be able to guide the departed children to their next destination." For Hotaru: "Your only choice now is to live as a human. However, you haven't lost the protection granted by **the tanuki and Kobo Daishi**" — **a calligraphy brush**, because "if you are to live as a human, then without words, you will be devoid of human relationships… this brush shall allow you to convey reason."
- To Runa: "**You whose fate is an adverse one. Forget not that the choice to obey or to resist your fate lies solely in your hands. All we can do is watch over you as you make your choices…**"
- The train stops; Eva sees the sickle; the carriage is sealed and searched; "What…? Washington is in a rage?!" About a dozen Washington officials write letters of apology; her guards return part of their pay; the Kyoto police wear "we warned you" looks. She prays at Fushimi Inari the next day under heavy guard. An **Inari shrine with the sickle as its *shintai*** is planned for the campus; a white fox is seen eating pomegranates by its boundary.

*The hokora*
- Three days later the shrine is a small **hokora**; the sickle will move to a main hall later, and **Hariti** will then occupy the hokora; more hokora will house the forgotten local gods ("the gods will be happy to have a nice new home… and we'll be happy that they're not cursing us"). Tachibana smiles: such cases were common in the bubble.
- Mizuki, in miko dress, is "being mean to Kaihouin-san's grandfather": the **Kaihouins** have been obstructing the shrine through the municipal government, the Ministry of Education and Fushimi Inari itself — until the sickle arrived. "**Occultists would never try harassing a god.**" Narration: "Thus, **the plan to bring prosperity to this nation using a hundred years of children as a sacrifice was completely halted** by me—someone who thought the strategy was behind the times—and the whim of a certain god."
- The officiating priest is a **Teisei Department Stores employee** (department stores keep rooftop shrines) who refuses to tend the gods daily; he becomes director of her new **religious corporation** (needed to fund the shrine without misusing school money; she will not use it to dodge tax). Mizuki and Hotaru become its shrine maidens; Hotaru strikes a pose.
- Practicalities: a moat, bridge, vermilion torii and a new well; a shrine office staffed by **Kitakaba Security** that doubles as **a panic room**; police patrols; demands that came, Runa guesses, from the police and the US government (the White House was in "a real commotion").
- The sacred sake bottle is found empty with its cork in place; Mizuki's peaches and Hotaru's inari sushi have vanished. "Let's pretend that we never saw any of this." Every day the inari sushi offered at the hokora disappears. "It was a happy ending to the story."

**Revelations**
- **Keika Resource Development is 49.9% Keika Corp and 50.1% a Swiss private-bank account** — Runa's — and Russia rumours that the account holds **the Romanov secret assets**. (This settles Vol. 6 Ch. 9's ambiguous "49.9 percent": Keika Corp holds 49.9%; the Moonlight Fund's Swiss account keeps the majority.)
- The press now treats Keika Corp as the group's real centre and Ichijou as fading; Tachibana lost his representative rights over the Sakata tax affair.
- Keika is buying up Hokkaido's bubble wrecks (ferries, Tomamu, hotels); military personnel are visible at the Yubari gated community.
- Dakiniten names Runa's fate "adverse" and says resisting it is her choice; the gods' gifts (a sickle, Hariti's seeds, Kūkai's brush) seal the end of the zashiki-warashi system and Hotaru's humanity.
- The Kaihouins stop obstructing once a god is involved.

**Historical references**
- **Confirmed by the glossary:** the **Department of Homeland Security** (established 2003, after 9/11); a battalion ≈ 1,000 people; *hokora* / *sotomiya* as "franchise" outposts of a lineage; **department-store priests** (employees who maintain rooftop shrines; the value of shrine networks in rural business).
- **Historically real, used accurately:** the Meiji separation of Shinto and Buddhism (*shinbutsu bunri*, 1868) and the *haibutsu kishaku* destruction of temples; temples as Edo-period registries (*danka seido*); State Shinto and the 1945 Shinto Directive; *amatsukami* and *kunitsukami*; **Usa Jingū**, whose three main halls enshrine Hachiman, Hime-ōkami and Empress Jingū (the novel's placing of **Hime-ōkami in the central hall** matches standard accounts as I know them, but the hall order was not confirmed in this pass); the **Dōkyō incident** (769) and the Usa oracle; **Dakiniten** and her fusion with Inari in esoteric Buddhism (Toyokawa Inari); **Hariti** (Kishimojin), the child-devouring demon turned protector of children, and her pomegranate; Kūkai as master calligrapher; the "fox's wedding" sun shower; Fushimi Inari's inari-sushi offerings; Japanese religious corporations (*shūkyō hōjin*) and their tax status; Tomamu's resort bankruptcy and later rescue (Hoshino Resorts began operating it in 2004 — here Moonlight Resorts).
- **My identifications:** **Hachitomi Steel = Nippon Steel** — *Hachi-* (八, Yawata) + *-tomi* (富, Fuji), the two steelmakers that merged in 1970; **Imperial Metals and Chemicals = Japan Metals & Chemicals** (Nippon Jūkagaku Kōgyō), a ferroalloy maker that went into corporate reorganisation in 2002 — *plausible*, not verified in this pass. Hokkaido Ferry may echo **Higashi Nihon Ferry** (bubble-era hotel and resort overreach; civil rehabilitation in 2003) — *uncertain*. Suikan Hotel is unidentified.
- **Production slip:** Tomamu's liabilities are "twenty-two million" in one sentence and "twenty-two billion" in the next.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **The 50.1% puts Runa back where the plots want her.** The Moonlight Fund's Swiss account was Runa's from Vol. 1, and Vol. 1's glossary noted the Romanov-fortune legends behind Swiss banking. Now the press links her majority stake to "the Romanov family's secret assets" — exactly the fiction the Anastasia schemers needed (Vol. 6 Ch. 6: "what if the Romanov family treasure actually existed?… that treasure's owner would be the young lady"). Her own structure makes her look like the heir.
- *Strongly supported:* **"Choose" is the god's whole message.** "The choice to obey or to resist your fate lies solely in your hands" — the supernatural voice agrees with Kanbe and Takamiya. Her choice of the sickle — not power, not prosperity, but the tool that lets the dead rest — is the volume's ethic in one gesture.
- *Strongly supported:* **The supernatural arc closes with institutions, not miracles.** A religious corporation, a director, a panic room, police patrols, a White House "commotion": the series' habit of turning the uncanny into governance (Vol. 6 Ch. 7's escape routes) applies even to gods.
- *Plausible:* **Hotaru's brush is a gift of speech.** The girl who spoke only in nods and two-word answers receives the means "to convey reason"; her humanity is defined as having words.
- *Plausible:* **The god wants Runa as a worshipper for life** — the fox bride of Vol. 3 was Runa herself as an adult; the offer of "happiness" for lifelong worship is the bride's contract again, refused by choosing the sickle.
- *Speculative:* **Military personnel at Yubari** and the satellites "standing by" suggest the household is preparing for something larger than kidnapping — the "restoration" or the Karafuto state.

**Connections:** Vol. 1 Ch. 4 (the Swiss account; Romanov-fortune legends in the glossary); Vol. 2 (Keika Pacific Ferry's origins with Hokkaido Kaitaku Bank; Yubari); Vol. 3 Ch. 10 (Fushimi Inari; the fox bride who was herself; the Cintamani stone); Vol. 4 Ch. 9 (Tachibana's Sakata tax affair); Vol. 5 Ch. 8 (the Kanna house; Cintamani "will lead you where you need to go"); Vol. 6 Ch. 6 (Anastasia), Ch. 9 (Keika Resource Development; 49.9%); this volume Ch. 3 (scavengers; "dumpster diving"), Ch. 6 (the zashiki warashi; the Kaihouins), Ch. 9 (Ono and Natsume's transfers).

## Side Story — The Failure of the Young Lady's Escort Mission

**Narrative position:** Third person, three offices in Washington, D.C., plus a one-line coda in the Oval Office; the American side of Ch. 10's Kyoto trip. A farce of inter-agency rivalry, written as a sequence of phone calls.

**What happens**
- **Part One (the CIA — "the Company").** Angela is kept out: she is needed on Wall Street to stop other organisations meddling in the laundering case, and "she failed to protect the young lady on the last Kyoto escort mission" — her superiors do not want it to hold back her rise. The CIA has ten people with Runa and twenty agents posing as tourists — until **the Department of Homeland Security** pays to take over the tourist bus the CIA had booked, "waving the Stars and Stripes around."
- **Part Two (the Pentagon).** It has formed a "Funny Platoon" to show it can both kill terrorists and protect the young lady. "Japanese people are too naive when it comes to the realm of magic." Then word arrives that **FBI special agents** have flown in to investigate the supernatural at Fushimi Inari, taking the Kyoto police's general-affairs chief and newspaper reporters with them.
- **Part Three (a coordinating office).** "Why are they doing this in a country we're allied to?! The Japanese government might get pissed and withdraw their troops from Iraq!… more of our men coming back from Iraq in caskets." (An aide: "But the young lady did put herself in all kinds of danger during the Narita Airport incident.") A new threat: **Russian anti-establishment forces** have concluded that "the best way to shake up their country is to target the young lady"; Japan's public-security police stop most at the border, except in Karafuto, which has flights to Vladivostok; the Osaka, Kyoto and Hyogo police are hunting them under cover of gang investigations. And the public-security officers are booked into the same hotels as the American personnel. CIA and Pentagon chiefs wait in the lobby "with very scary looks."
- **The Oval Office, a few days later**, to the assembled agency representatives: "**…It sounds like you guys had a lot of fun on your sightseeing trip to Kyoto.**"

**Revelations**
- Russian anti-government groups now target Runa as a way to destabilise Russia — her Romanov blood as a political weapon from the other side.
- Karafuto's direct flights to Vladivostok are Japan's weak point in border control.
- American agencies treat her safety as tied to Japan's commitment in Iraq.

**Historical references**
- **Historically real, used accurately:** the CIA nicknamed "the Company"; the Department of Homeland Security (2003) and its rivalry with older agencies; the FBI's domestic remit (the joke of its "sightseeing" abroad).
- **Fictional:** the operations; the "Funny Platoon"; Russian anti-establishment cells targeting her.

**Deeper significance**
- *Strongly supported:* **Comedy with a strategic point.** The Americans are absurd, but their reasoning is not: if she is hurt, Japan may withdraw from Iraq. Her safety has become a variable in the war.
- *Plausible:* **The Russian threat is the mirror of Europe's plan** (Vol. 6 Ch. 10): one side would marry her into Russia; the other would use an attack on her to shake the Russian state.

**Connections:** Vol. 3 Ch. 10 (Fushimi Inari; Angela's "I don't believe in the occult"); Vol. 4 (Angela and the CIA's "science division"); Vol. 6 Chs. 6–7 (Narita; the US agency feud); this volume Ch. 9 (Secret Service "trainees"), Ch. 10 (the Kyoto trip; "less than a full battalion").

## A Few Article Excerpts

**Narrative position:** Six unattributed news excerpts, late 2003 to 2004, giving the world picture after the volume's events. As in Vol. 5 Ch. 6, the documents carry much of the alternate history.

**What happens**
1. **The CIA director resigns** "for 'personal reasons'"; the deputy takes over. The CIA was attacked in Congress when **Karafuto Bank's laundered capital was traced to espionage funds meant to overthrow the Eastern Bloc**; White House insiders blame the agency's lack of foresight for Iraq; the **neocons** are said to be using it as a scapegoat — and may be next.
2. **The State Department rises; the Pentagon falls.** Generals in Iraq criticise the war; after **Turkey's invasion of the northern Kurdish region** and **Iran's military occupation of the south** "to protect the Shia," Washington had to negotiate with both to keep US troops in the centre safe. State shares a course with Britain and **Japan**, "who have also dispatched troops," and welcomed the Japanese ruling party's victory, which "has shipped many troops to the Gulf region."
3. **Russia:** some Karafuto Bank funds went to **Chechen guerrillas**; the president's crackdown on the Russian mafia (blamed for Narita) has provoked counter-attacks; fighting spreads in Chechnya and **North Ossetia**.

The reincarnated narrator is now a historian of her own era, and the afterword confirms that the author is writing as one too.

## §3. The zashiki-warashi machine — catch-up growth, luck and who paid for it

*An economics note.* Kanna Mizuki's explanation in Chapter 6 is, in form, an economic model. A late industrialiser needs a surplus to finance its catch-up; the academy's feng-shui circle converts the deaths of unwanted children — "mainly the souls of aborted fetuses and children who died before reaching adulthood… They used people like me to provide them" — into national luck. "That was how our country managed to make up for our industrial revolution taking place a hundred years late." "It's actually pretty efficient."

- **The real hidden subsidies of catch-up.** *Plausible, my reading:* the circle condenses into one image the real costs of Japan's Meiji-to-Shōwa growth that fell on people without a voice — a land tax that financed the state from the countryside, mills run on the labour of young women, rural infanticide (*mabiki*) in hard years, and a sexual economy at home and abroad. The novel's own clues point this way: the Kanna-type women, "those with no means of getting by except selling their bodies," and, two chapters later, *Madama Butterfly* — a Japanese woman abandoned by an American officer — placed in "the time period when Japan was pushing forward to 'enrich the country'" (Ch. 8). (A folkloric theory links zashiki warashi to the spirits of children lost to *mabiki*; the novel adopts it.)
- **Why the machine broke.** A postwar church inserted into "a perfect feng-shui arrangement" causes "errors" — "like the errors that occur when you connect a PC to a slightly different operating system," Runa says: "It's Honami all over again" (Honami being the series' Mizuho, whose real 2002 merger began with a famous systems failure — general knowledge). *Plausible:* the joke is also an allegory. Japan's catch-up machinery — main banks, the convoy system, lifetime employment — glitched when foreign standards were grafted onto it, which is how the 1990s debate over "global standards" described the lost decade.
- **Hotaru as the patch, Koizumi's luck as the subject.** The afterword makes the allegory explicit: Hotaru was invented because "Keikain Runa needed to have amazing luck too," to match "the real-life Prime Minister Koizumi and his incredible luck," and the junior-high years will show "the two girls connected to the supernatural fighting against this 'incredible luck.'" Real-world background: the **Izanami expansion**, February 2002 to February 2008, was the longest of Japan's postwar recoveries, but it was driven largely by exports and a weak yen and was widely described as a recovery that households did not feel. *Strongly supported* (author's statement): the supernatural plot is about the luck of the era. *Plausible* (inference from the afterword's juxtaposition): the luck had a price, and the people who paid it are the dispatch generation the author names in the same paragraph — the people of Runa's past life.
- **Runa's refusal and its cost.** "I decided to choose Hotaru-chan, my friend, over this country's stability." She does not pretend it is free: "three and a half million unemployed and twenty-four million former Northern Japanese citizens… That's a pretty heavy weight." The choice is a refusal of a growth model whose costs fall on children who cannot consent. It also has an ironic edge, which Ch. 3 states: her own fortune is "an extreme form of insider trading" with "past-life memories." She is herself a kind of supernatural luck for the Keika Group.
- **Douanji's question is the premise of the series.** "If that plot allowed one to go back in time and make it so that the bubble never burst, would this country's people be able to resist the temptation?" (Ch. 13). Runa is the one person who has, in effect, gone back in time. The volume's answer is in her conduct: she uses her knowledge, but she refuses to buy outcomes with children.

## §4. Karafuto as an underclass — the three bills and the economics of dispatch work

The volume's politics turn on three bills that Koizumi brings to the autumn Diet. The opposition's platform (Ch. 1), Tachibana's briefing (Ch. 3), the narrator's verdict (Ch. 5) and the news (Ch. 6) follow them.

| Bill | In-world purpose | Real counterpart | Runa's stance | The verdict in the text |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Worker Dispatch amendment | More jobs for the bad-debt era's unemployed and displaced Northerners; ID and social-insurance requirements for temporary workers (the ruling side, Ch. 1) | The real amendment passed in **June 2003** and took effect on **1 March 2004**, extending dispatch work to manufacturing | Resists it this session: "It wasn't like I'd forgotten all the suffering it had caused me so long ago" (Ch. 3) | "Temporary relief for the unemployed would become permanent, causing a job drought that would bring misfortune to an entire generation" (Ch. 5) |
| Karafuto Special Zone Act | Abolish the Reconstruction Agency; a special economic zone and "proving ground" for deregulation (Ch. 3) | Koizumi's **Special Zones for Structural Reform** (enacted December 2002) — regional exceptions to national regulation | Backs it (Ch. 3) | "The issue of immigrants and refugees would worsen tensions in Japan" (Ch. 5) |
| Privy Council Reformation Act | Strip nobles of immunity from arrest (the "showpiece," Ch. 3) | None (Japan's peerage was abolished in 1947) | Believes immunity "shouldn't exist," but is "terrified" to decide without foreknowledge (Ch. 3) | Stonewalled by the Privy Council (Ch. 6); enacted privately when the Kaihouins waive immunity (Ch. 13) |

*An economics note — why liberalising dispatch work helped and hurt.*
- **Reform at the margin.** Japanese case law made it very hard to dismiss regular employees (the doctrine was written into the Labour Standards Act in 2003 — general knowledge). When regular contracts cannot easily be changed, firms adjust through the contracts they *can* change. Liberalising temporary work therefore lowers adjustment costs only for outsiders. The result is a **dual labour market**: insiders keep their protection; outsiders absorb every shock. (Spain's liberalisation of fixed-term contracts in 1984 is the textbook European case — general knowledge.)
- **The real numbers.** Dispatched workers grew from about 240,000 in 1994 to 690,000 in 2002 (in regular-employee equivalents), and short-time workers were 24.1% of employees in 2003, when unemployment averaged 5.3%, or about 3.5 million people (JIL). The novel's "three and a half million unemployed" is exact.
- **The link to 2008.** When the financial crisis reached Japanese manufacturing in late 2008, firms cancelled dispatch contracts first (*haken-giri*). From 31 December 2008 to 5 January 2009 volunteers ran a "New Year's dispatch village" (*Toshikoshi Haken Mura*) in Hibiya Park for more than 500 workers who had lost both jobs and company dormitories. *Plausible, my inference:* the crisis Runa is preparing for will fall first on the workers the 2003 amendment creates; her resistance to the bill is part of her preparation for 2008, whether or not she says so.
- **The in-world twist.** In the novel the outsiders are a region and a people — twenty-odd million Karafuto-born workers whose wages are low because of "factors such as their not requiring guarantees of identity or social security" (Kanbe, Ch. 1). That makes visible, as an ethnic and regional divide, what in real Japan was mainly a generational one (the "employment ice age" cohort). Kanbe's reform — equal ID and insurance requirements — removes the regulatory arbitrage that makes Karafuto labour cheap; the opposition's "identical circumstances" anticipates the real equal-pay rules Japan adopted only in 2020–21 (general knowledge).
- **East Germany.** The opposition's comparison (Ch. 1) is apt in one direction: reunification brought low wages, bankrupt state firms and unemployment to the East, and migration westward. The novel adds what the German case shows too: resentment on both sides — "bullying targeted at anyone from Karafuto" (Ch. 4) and the Kyoto old man's "Anyone from Karafuto is completely disposable!" (Ch. 1).

*Strongly supported:* **the volume's political subject is Karafuto as an underclass, and Runa's first life is her guide to it.** The opposition's "All human beings are of equal value" (Ch. 1), Kanbe's "never lower the bar," the narrator's verdict and the afterword's "a story where no one winds up happy… even though it was reality" form one argument, from four voices.

## §5. The scavengers — shareholder value, free cash flow and the Roppongi preview

- **Who they are.** "Vultures who feast on the bodies of other vultures" (Mark Barnes's phrase, Ch. 3) — funds that move into a market after the first wave of distressed investors. Their creed (top-down standardisation, global sourcing, personnel cuts, returning capital to shareholders) is the glossary's "GE Capital, as well as Japanese McDonald's." Their real counterparts in 2002–06 Japan were the first activist and hostile-bid funds and the new-economy entrepreneurs — Steel Partners, the Murakami Fund, Livedoor — whose slogan was that "the company belongs to its shareholders" (general knowledge).
- **The theory in their advertisement.** "Any shareholder would be angered to see a company make off with tens of billions of dollars, then seemingly squander it on corporate acquisitions and public-works projects… let us do it for you instead." This is Michael Jensen's **free-cash-flow theory** (*American Economic Review*, 1986): the conflict between managers and shareholders is sharpest "over such issues as the optimal size of the firm and the payment of cash to shareholders," and worst when firms have more cash than good projects. Runa's ¥266 billion of Hokkaido purchases in Ch. 10 (ferries, Tomamu, hotels) are, to them, empire-building.
- **Tachibana's two "disadvantages."** (1) She is a minor, so the group depends on "the wisdom… of a child"; (2) there is only one of her. In valuation terms, these are a **key-person risk** and a **controlling-shareholder risk**: her readiness "to intervene in company management" undoes the point of listing. Tachibana agrees with the scavengers — the series' habit of giving its antagonists the era's logic, and much of its truth.
- **"Dumpster diving" as private social policy.** "People asked me to save them, so I did… It's all because no one ever reached out and helped me when I needed it all those years ago" (Ch. 3). Her rescues do privately what Japan's state did through the **Industrial Revitalization Corporation of Japan** (established 16 April 2003, dissolved 15 March 2007, after supporting 41 corporate groups). The critics' name for such rescues is "zombie" support: the influential argument of Caballero, Hoshi and Kashyap (2008) is that keeping insolvent firms alive depressed restructuring and job creation in 1990s Japan (general knowledge). The novel presents both views: to the market her rescues are waste; to her they are the safety net her past life lacked. "I want to save as many people as I can. Does that make me arrogant?" (Ch. 10).
- **Distressed debt as leverage.** The scavengers buy Nichikaba's worthless bonds (Ch. 3) because creditors can obstruct a restructuring — here, the merger of Nichikaba with Gulf Oil Development into Keika Resource Development. Okazaki reads the move as a courtship: they "stopped you from wasting two trillion yen, just so they could earn your favor," and "they're trying to save you with Wall Street logic."
- **Owning without managing.** Chapter 4 turns the scavengers' critique into comedy: she does not know that her photographer is an independent director of Teisei; a dispute between two of her companies is settled for her by their presidents in advance, and the meeting is staged so that both sides have it on record. "I really felt the importance of separating ownership from management" — Berle and Means's problem, learnt in a department-store meeting room.
- **The preview of Volume 8 makes them a class.** "The winners who'd managed to recover before anyone else," on "the top floor of a famous tower in Roppongi," toasting "the era of one winner for every hundred losers" and planning to "use the amended Worker Dispatch Act to speed up layoffs." *My identification:* the tower is **Roppongi Hills Mori Tower** (opened 25 April 2003), home of the period's new-money tenants — Livedoor, raided there in January 2006, among them. The "winners and losers" language is the 2000s Japanese discourse of *kachigumi* and *makegumi* (general knowledge). The preview's last line — that Runa "had been one of those people who were chewed up and spat out in her past life" — sets her against them.

## §6. The 50.1% — a Swiss account, Romanov rumours and control

Chapter 10's magazine settles Volume 6's ambiguous "49.9 percent": **Keika Corp owns 49.9% of Keika Resource Development; a Swiss private bank holds 50.1%**, under an undisclosed account name, "speculated" to belong to "someone within the Keikain family," with "rumblings in Russia that the account actually contains the Romanov family's secret assets."

- **How a 49.9% holding can be a "subsidiary."** Japanese practice judged control, not just majority ownership: "a company that holds 40% to 50% of a subsidiary's voting rights is interpreted as a parent" if it has close ties to it or appoints a majority of its board (Cabinet Office, *Annual Report on the Japanese Economy*, 2003). So Keika Corp can consolidate and run KRD — satisfying the consolidated-accounting logic of Vol. 6 — while Runa's account keeps the majority of votes in reserve. It is a two-key structure: the listed parent manages; the private owner can always decide.
- **Why the structure matters.** It keeps her crown jewel (resources, the old core of the Moonlight Fund) out of the scavengers' reach; it explains why the press now sees Keika Corp as the group's centre and Ichijou as fading; and the **group chart** shows the Moonlight Fund still above the holding companies as "Financial Management." *Strongly supported:* her power has been formalised (Vol. 6) without being given up.
- **The myth it feeds.** In Vol. 6 Ch. 6 the Anastasia account — a Karafuto Bank laundering vehicle labelled "the Romanov family treasure" — needed a legitimate heir to become real. Now her own account, honestly hers, is rumoured to hold Romanov assets. Background: the Anastasia legend's most famous claimant, Anna Anderson, was shown by DNA tests in 1994 not to be a Romanov (Penn State); legends of tsarist money in foreign banks recur (Vol. 1's glossary). *Strongly supported:* **her secrecy writes her myth.** The structure that protects her makes her look like the heir the plotters needed.
- **The pressure to come** (*speculative*). The immunity bill and the prosecutors' interest in Tachibana's Sakata tax affair (Chs. 9–10) both point at the household's finances; an anonymous foreign majority stake is exactly what an investigation, or a Russian claimant, would ask about.

## §7. Kanbe's curriculum — eight lectures as a course in power

Kanbe's lectures (Ch. 7) follow the order of a political-economy course: incentives, the international order, distribution, geography, the political class, force, coalitions and time.

| Lecture | Thesis | The concept underneath (my gloss) | What it tells Runa |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Buyout theory | A ¥100 million bribe is cheaper than cutting a ¥10 billion bid | Rent-seeking and bid-rigging (*dangō*): a bribe is worth up to the rent it protects | "Make sure to be more careful than them"; she lives in "gray areas" |
| 2. Treason theory | Sell your will to the hegemon; "What's so bad about second place?" | Hegemonic stability; alliance as purchased protection | Her answer: "No, I wouldn't" gamble 140 million people — against the old man's "true independence" |
| 3. Theory of equality | Capitalism won by redefining equality as opportunity, not outcome | Meritocracy; Fukuzawa read whole | "The concept that had cut down everyone who was weak, including myself" |
| 4. Hometown theory | Industrial and energy change emptied the regions; TV made Tokyo everyone's hometown | Structural change (primary → secondary → tertiary); the energy revolution | Runa admits she is a Tokyo person — after Sakata (Ch. 5) |
| 5. Political peddlers | Mass-produce competent self-interested politicians; the "well-and-fence" ideal is lonely | Public choice; selection of political talent | "Would your family, your friends, and your loved ones approve of that path?" — addressed to her |
| 6. Violence theory | Private security and bounties; "Nations generally don't allow other nations to exist within them" | Weber's state monopoly of legitimate violence | A warning to the nobles; Ryouko: "do it without drawing any attention to yourself" |
| 7. Outsider theory | Shinpan, fudai, tozama; a third of the country plus one ally wins | The minimum winning coalition (Riker, 1962) | The game's ending as a tozama coalition under Mizuho; Keika is "nothing more than a tozama daimyo" |
| 8. Fool theory | *Don Quixote*; Koizumi's unchanged cause since 1979 | Reputation and credible commitment | "If you try to get on the wave and miss, you'll crash and burn"; "option C" |

- *Strongly supported:* **the course turns the series' themes into a syllabus for the next elite** — her friends, associates and "the other elite little eggs who will shoulder this country's future someday" fill the hall. Its method is the Machiavelli line Kanbe borrows from Runa: study the roads to hell to avoid them.
- *Strongly supported:* **Kanbe has two voices.** On television he is the plain-spoken neoliberal ("offer opportunities but never lower the bar," Ch. 1) and the theorist of genius ("the sacrifice of a million children… a single god"). In the lecture hall he is the realist who asks her whether her loved ones would approve. The volume lets both stand.
- *Plausible:* **"A million children… a single god"** and the zashiki-warashi machine of Ch. 6 are deliberately paired; Douanji, Kanbe's friend, recalls the line while arresting the man who built the machine's last version (Ch. 13).

## §8. Marriage as the new field of play

| Party | Move | Ch. |
|---|---|---|
| Eiichi | Has told his father and grandfather that he proposed — "a marriage between the Teia Group and the Keika Group"; refuses a Futaki marriage meeting | 4 |
| Futaki–Yodoyabashi | Merchant houses that "developed their businesses through their female descendants"; choosing candidates for her; eyeing Honami | 4 |
| Iwazaki | Erika's suitors at the imoni party; Aki's marriage to a Keika-Iwazaki Chemical executive; Sakurako — "The Iwazaki zaibatsu was trying to surround me" | 5 |
| Europe | The Romanov-consort plan (Vol. 6 Ch. 10); Western blue bloods' "Duchess of Aquitaine" | 4 |
| Television | "You'll either go bankrupt and disappear in a pathetic fashion, or you'll get married and disappear that way" | 4 |
| Kanna Mizuki | "Your family name will change once you're married… That will mean starting a new family" | 4 |
| Runa | "Becoming an adult meant choosing another person"; "I would have to leave them someday" | 4, 8 |

*An economics note.* In a family-business system, marriage is the merger market and adoption is the succession technology. The glossary confirms the Kansai practice of passing a business through a daughter to an adopted son-in-law (*muko yōshi*). Research on Japanese family firms finds that firms run by such non-blood heirs perform best: "We find non-blood heirs' firms to be star performers, consistent with their being selected for talent" (Mehrotra, Morck, Shim and Wiwattanakantang, *Journal of Financial Economics*, 2013). The Futaki–Yodoyabashi method is that market at work, and Aki's marriage uses it for protection: the husband will be adopted into the Tokitou name, founding "the highest-ranking clan dedicated to my protection" (Ch. 5).

- *Strongly supported:* **Runa is the reverse case.** Yuujirou's "first-generation founder" (Ch. 4) means her group has no lineage behind it: whoever she marries, and on what terms, decides its fate. That is why every zaibatsu now has a marriage policy towards her.
- *Plausible:* **The "Duchess of Aquitaine" is the right comparison.** Eleanor's second marriage carried Aquitaine to the future Henry II and redrew the map of medieval Europe; Mitsuya's joke ("what if she marries each of us and divorces us one by one?") is Eleanor's career told as a threat.
- *Plausible:* **Michimaro's birth makes her a better match.** No longer the Keikain heir, she can marry out without taking the dukedom with her — which frees her, and also makes her available to everyone else's plans.

## §9. The state closes in — noble immunity and the case against Tachibana

Immunity appears in the volume in six forms:
- **Shelter for the guilty** — Karafuto officials who fled (Chs. 1, 3); the Shisuka marquessate: "Thank goodness we have diplomatic immunity as nobles" (Ch. 3).
- **A queue at the main residence** — people caught up in the bribery scandal who "want to walk free with the same diplomatic immunity we're granted as nobles" (Ch. 3).
- **Private punishment of its misuse** — Shiori's tutor and his girlfriend "forced to atone with their lives" (Ch. 2).
- **Public relations** — the police's special-records "snakes," who "set things straight with the general public" after a noble escapes a crime (Ch. 9).
- **Okazaki's verdict on *Wall Street*** — "because there's such thing as diplomatic immunity" (Ch. 3).
- **A private abolition** — the Kaihouin house waives immunity for its own elder by treaty with the Keikains (Ch. 13).

*Strongly supported:* **the institution is eroding from within before the Diet acts.** Houses waive it, punish its abuse and lend it out; the Privy Council blocks its legal reform (Ch. 6). Ono's line to the old man — "times have changed, and this is a new era" — describes a class abandoning a privilege it can no longer defend.

**Tachibana.** The accounts of Otsumaro's death now number four: Tachibana "pushed him to suicide" (Vol. 2 Ch. 7); the ruling party drove him to it to shield Katou (Nakamaro, Vol. 6 Ch. 3); the same scandal made Ichijou's career (his "rabbit," Ch. 5); and the Tokyo prosecutors' Special Investigations Department now suspects Tachibana's involvement (Ch. 9). The lever is abolition; the motive, rumour says, is the prime minister's grudge over the Sakata land; the angle is that "the young lady could just be a puppet controlled by Tachibana-san." Real background: the Special Investigations Department's high-profile cases of the period fell on figures who had challenged the establishment (the Livedoor raid of January 2006 is the best known), and critics called them "national-policy investigations" (*kokusaku sōsa*) — general knowledge. *Strongly supported:* **the volume prepares the case against Tachibana as the next threat to her household** — and, as in Vol. 6, the adults keep it from her.

## §10. What is Runa, in this volume?

Volume 7 gives the series' question a vocabulary from the occult.

1. **A possessing spirit.** "I was essentially someone who'd 'possessed' Keikain Runa as well" (Ch. 2) — said while Mizuki explains that "Being possessed isn't always bad… she really did change for the better."
2. **The mourner of her original.** "You were lucky, Madama Butterfly. You got to die of your own free will… I imagined that Keikain Runa never got that opportunity" (Ch. 8). The song is "for Madama Butterfly and for Keikain Runa" — the game's girl, as someone else.
3. **A scavenger** (Ch. 3) and **a first-generation founder** (Ch. 4).
4. **"The Duchess of Aquitaine"** (Ch. 4) and **a would-be sovereign** of a Northern Karafuto haven (Ch. 3).
5. **"The traitor's daughter"** (Elder Kaihouin, Ch. 6) — the heir of Hikomaro's "dream of hegemony" who dismantles it.
6. **An idol and an entertainer** — the model Shizuka's spirit copies (Ch. 2); "Keikain Runa, the entertainer" (Ch. 4).
7. **A shinpan of the main house** whose group is "nothing more than a tozama daimyo" (Ch. 7).
8. **"A poster girl for the war on terror"** (Ch. 9) and a variable in Japan's Iraq commitment (Ch. 11).
9. **A Romanov heir, by rumour** (Ch. 10).
10. **"You whose fate is an adverse one"** (Dakiniten, Ch. 10).
11. **One of those "chewed up and spat out in her past life"** (the Vol. 8 preview).
12. **A human being** — Takamiya: "None of you are gods, traitors, or whores. You're human beings."

The volume's distinctive answer is the first two together. The supernatural frame lets the series say plainly what it has implied for six volumes: she is a soul living in a borrowed life, and she grieves for the girl whose life it was. The volume judges possession by its effects, as Mizuki does — and Takamiya's verdict closes the list.

## §11. The era — nostalgia and trauma in autumn 2003

- **Trauma, named in the author's own voice.** The afterword: "Japan has been enduring that bitter pain for twenty years now"; the dispatch amendment as "an attempt to stop the bleeding"; the 1997 failures of Sanyo Securities, Hokkaidō Takushoku and Yamaichi already forgotten — "the line between 'our past' and 'history' must exist somewhere around that point in time." In the text: the narrator's "job drought"; Koizumi's cuts to local budgets (Ch. 5); depopulation (Ch. 7); bubble wrecks — the Kurimori resorts, Far Eastern Developments, Hokkaido Ferry, Tomamu, Suikan Hotel; bullying of Karafuto-born students.
- **Nostalgia, catalogued.** *Armored Core 3*; *New Rally-X*, *Mr. Do!* and *Pengo* in roadside restaurants; *Super Puyo Puyo*; the Tokyo Game Show; *Ragnarok Online*; Suzuka on 12 October 2003, when Barrichello won and Schumacher took his sixth title; late-night variety TV and *Suiyō Dō Deshō*; "Kakashi," "Fight!," "Tokyo," "Sakura Zaka"; *Beautiful Dreamer*; consommé chips at midnight; a Kudanshita maid café; imoni on the Mogami.
- **Memory as the volume's motif.** At Jūroku Rakan Iwa: "it's wrong to go to extremes just to preserve it. That's why we should commit the scene before us to memory" — and the photograph becomes "like a treasure to all four of us" (Ch. 5). At the festival, the group photo again "like a treasure" (Ch. 8). *Strongly supported:* the volume prefers remembering to preserving at any cost — the opposite of the monks who died for the carvings and of the nationalists who sacrificed children for prosperity. *Plausible:* the series itself is that act of memory — 2003 written down before it turns into "history."
- **Respite as a value.** Eiichi's "Life's only fun because you get to keep changing" (Ch. 8) answers the *Beautiful Dreamer* temptation of an endless festival, and Runa's "This life I'd been born into was just like a festival" makes her second life a gift rather than a mission.
- **The next era.** The Vol. 8 preview moves the story from the losers of the 1990s to the winners of the 2000s — "the era of one winner for every hundred losers" — and from bad debts to "credit default swaps." The series is walking towards 2008 through its causes.

## §12. The missing demonstration, in Volume 7

The series' world has never seen a city destroyed by a nuclear weapon: what it lacks is not nuclear use but a real demonstration (Vol. 5 Part II §3a). Volume 7 keeps the theme in four places.

- **Nuclear deliberation as routine.** Kanbe reports in passing that Washington "seriously considered the use of nuclear weapons in Iraq" (Ch. 7). Without a shared image of what such use means, the option is discussed like any other.
- **Partition three years early.** The in-world plan to split Iraq in three (Chs. 7, 12) anticipates a real proposal — Senator Biden's of May 2006 for "Shi'ite, Sunni and Kurdish autonomous regions, with a central government in Baghdad" — and adds Turkish and Iranian occupations and JSDF combat with Iran.
- **The nationalists' substitute weapon.** "If she became a god, this country would be able to defeat the United States for real this time! Our true independence hung in the balance!" (Ch. 13). *Plausible:* in a world without a demonstrated bomb, the old man's guardian god is a magical deterrent — a weapon bought with children — and Kanbe's "What's so bad about second place?" is the realist's reply.
- **The vocabulary of deterrence in private life.** Runa calls her relationship with Koizumi "something closer to mutually assured destruction" (Ch. 3), and Kanbe warns the nobles, "You don't want to blow up when the time isn't right" (Ch. 7). The language of nuclear strategy runs through the volume's domestic politics while the real thing stays undemonstrated.

Three deserve emphasis.
- **Hariti.** A demon who devoured children, turned by the Buddha into their protector, receives the task of guiding "the departed children to their next destination" (Ch. 10). *Strongly supported:* she is the volume's arc in one figure — a system that consumed children turned into a memorial for them.
- ***Madama Butterfly* and *Beautiful Dreamer*.** One offers a death chosen freely; the other a festival that never ends. Runa envies the first and is tempted by the second; Eiichi refuses both for her ("keep changing").
- **Shibusawa Eiichi.** *Plausible, my reading:* the father of Japanese capitalism, remembered for joining ethics to profit (*The Analects and the Abacus*), is the volume's quiet counter to the scavengers — and the boy named after him is the one who will "make the next ones better and better."

## §14. Where the volume strains

- **The supernatural turn.** The author admits some readers were "perplexed." The allegory is heavy and its rules (curses as "paradoxical," blood as impurity, a church as an "error") are asserted rather than tested; "someone" who weaponised the spirits is left unexplained, with four wonders to come.
- **Sexual content around minors.** Kanna Mizuki's partners and pills, her advice to Runa, the boys' talk and a photographer's request sit uneasily in a volume whose moral centre is the protection of children.
- **A quick, private resolution.** The Kaihouin elder is ended by a family treaty, house arrest and silence (Ch. 13). That fits the series' realism about nobles, but the volume's most monstrous project closes off-stage.
- **The family tree and the text disagree.** The front-matter tree shows no legitimate daughter of Hikomaro, while Ch. 2 says Shiori's mother "was the legitimate daughter of the duke and his wife." Either the tree is simplified ("Other Relatives") or one of them is a slip.
- **Chronology.** The excerpts (Ch. 12) reach into mid- and late 2004 (Tenet's resignation; UNSCR 1559) inside an "autumn and winter 2003" volume; the Iraq partition debate is moved forward from 2006; Hoshino Resorts' real takeover of Tomamu (2004) becomes Moonlight Resorts'.
- **Production slips.** Tomamu's "twenty-two million"/"billion"; "Tachibana Akiko"; out-of-date cast-list titles (Okazaki).
- **Numbers that look inconsistent but are not.** "Twenty million residents of Karafuto descent in Karafuto, and a few million more" (Ch. 1) and "twenty-four million citizens of the former Northern Japanese regime" (Ch. 6) count different groups.
- **Voices missing.** Karafuto workers appear as statistics, guards and maids (Kitakaba), and a bullied marquess's daughter; Lydia is the exception. Iraqis are absent again.

# Part III — Synthesis

## Alternate-history ledger

**Preserved:** the **Worker Dispatch amendment** (passed June 2003; manufacturing dispatch from 1 March 2004); Koizumi's **Special Zones for Structural Reform**; postal privatisation as the ruling party's internal fight, and Koizumi's long campaign for it (parliamentary vice-minister of finance in 1979; Minister of Posts and Telecommunications in 1992); the post-clean-up credit crunch; Koizumi's cuts to local budgets and the Great Heisei municipal mergers; unemployment of about 3.5 million (5.3%) in 2003; the shareholder-value creed and the first activist funds; the **2003 Japanese Grand Prix** (12 October; Barrichello wins; Schumacher's sixth title); the Tokyo Game Show and the period's games; the new **Department of Homeland Security**; the CIA director's resignation "for personal reasons" (Tenet, June 2004); the **2003 European heat wave**; the **November 2003 Istanbul bombings**; violence spreading from Chechnya to North Ossetia; **UNSCR 1559** (2 September 2004); Sharon's January 2003 victory; Howard Dean's surge and defeat; Sakata's Sankyo warehouses, the 1976 fire and its lion heads, the Honma family, Jūroku Rakan Iwa; Tomamu's collapse; Ishiwara Kanji's grave below Mount Chōkai.

**Altered:**
- **Japan sent troops to the Vietnam War** (Ch. 1).
- **The JSDF is already in southern Iraq in 2003, fighting Iran's forces** (Chs. 1, 7, 12); really the SDF went to Samawah in 2004 on a non-combat mission.
- **Turkey occupies the Kurdish north and Iran the Shia south** (Chs. 7, 12).
- **Saddam Hussein is still at large** when the excerpts are written (Ch. 12); really he was captured on 13 December 2003.
- **Washington considers nuclear use in Iraq, and a three-way partition is "realistic" in 2003–04** (Chs. 7, 12); the real partition debate began with Biden's plan of May 2006.
- **UNSCR 1559** is aimed at keeping Syrian volunteers out of Iraq (Ch. 12); really it called for foreign (Syrian) forces to leave Lebanon and for militias to disarm. (The novel's four sponsors are correct: the draft was submitted by France, Germany, the United Kingdom and the United States.)
- **The heat wave's toll** is "over ten thousand" deaths in France and a harvest at "50%" (Ch. 12); really about 14,800 French deaths and a French wheat crop down about 20% (EU output down about 10%).
- **The CIA director falls over Karafuto Bank's laundered capital** (Ch. 12).
- **In-world chronology:** an opposition coalition in 1993 and **Northern Japan's fall and unification in 1994** (Ch. 7); Japan defeated in the Pacific War but **never occupied** (Chs. 6–7); the war's end brought by a change of government, with the Guards loyal (Ch. 2).
- **Tomamu** is rescued by Moonlight Resorts (Ch. 10); really Hoshino Resorts began operating it in 2004.
- **Private-security, detective and bounty laws** create a market in private violence (Ch. 7).

**Wholly fictional:** the academy's feng-shui circle and the Kaihouin ritual; the Seven Wonders; the Kanna house; Northern Karafuto as a no-man's-land and the plan for a new state; Nichikaba Oil Development; Keika Resource Development's Swiss 50.1%; Tír na nÓg; the Keikain Collection; the Metropolitan Police's special-records department; the Privy Council Reformation Act; *The Witches of Tokyo*; the game.

## Open questions

1. **Who is "someone"** who turned a dead child's "desire to live longer" into malice (Ch. 6) — and what are the **four remaining Seven Wonders**?
2. **Shiori's betrayal**, "still further ahead": what form can it take now that she is Runa's friend?
3. **Runa's luck.** With Hotaru human, does the "incredible luck" the afterword describes pass to Koizumi alone? Vol. 5's Wheel of Fortune question returns.

## Interpretive possibilities

**Strongly supported**
- The supernatural arc allegorises the luck of the era (the author's statement) and the hidden costs of catch-up growth (the text's own model) (Part II §3).
- Runa chooses a friend over the nation's luck — Kanbe's "choose the people at your side" turned into an act, with its costs acknowledged.
- The game's roles are being refilled by the same people in changed relations; the subprime whisper shows the script being reproduced by other hands (§2).
- Karafuto is the series' picture of the precarious generation, and the dispatch amendment is Runa's past-life wound (§4).
- The 50.1% keeps control private while feeding the Romanov myth that the plotters need (§6).
- Marriage has replaced business as the field on which others try to control her (§8).
- Noble immunity is being abolished from within before the law acts (§9).
- The author has promised a happy ending: the question is now *how* Runa escapes, not *whether*.

**Plausible**
- Resisting the dispatch amendment is part of her preparation for 2008, when dispatch workers were the first to be cut.
- The old man's guardian god is a nationalist substitute for the deterrent this world has never seen demonstrated.
- The two legal conditions of the game's ending — her heirship and her immunity — are both eroding.
- Kanbe's televised "a million children… a single god" is deliberately paired with the zashiki-warashi machine.
- Eiichi's namesake (Shibusawa) sets him up as the ethical capitalist against the scavengers.
- Takamiya is a hidden figure of the literary-political establishment, not just a librarian.

**Speculative**
- The "someone" behind the possessions is connected to the Northern Karafuto plotters or to Europe.
- Hotaru's lost power will show as Runa's luck running out at the 2005 postal election.
- "Teikyou Airlines" is JAL, and its hidden debts are the next Keika rescue.
- Yubari's soldiers and the satellites are preparation for a Karafuto crisis.
- Shiori's betrayal will be tied to the family's "pesky flies" idea and to Kanna Mizuki.

## On the "What is Runa?" question

Volume 7 answers with the vocabulary of possession.

- **Names she is given:** "Lady Runa" (Mizuki, Ch. 2); "You're my idol, Keikain-san" (Shizuka, Ch. 2); "a 'first-generation founder'" and "the 'Duchess of Aquitaine'" (Ch. 4); "Keikain Runa, the entertainer" (Ch. 4); "The traitor's daughter!" (Ch. 6); "a poster girl for the war on terror" (Ch. 9); "the young lady" whom Ono must handle (Ch. 9); "You whose fate is an adverse one" (Ch. 10); the "heroine" of the winners' script (Vol. 8 preview).
- **Names she gives herself:** "someone who'd 'possessed' Keikain Runa" (Ch. 2); Carlotta (Ch. 2); a scavenger (Ch. 3); a villainess with "my duty to fulfill the villainous role I'd been granted" (Ch. 5); "a traitor's daughter, after all" (Ch. 6); "one of those people who'd made Tokyo their hometown" (Ch. 7); "someone who rides the waves" (Ch. 7).
- **What the plots say:** to the Northern Karafuto fugitives she is a sovereign who would shelter them; to the scavengers, a governance risk; to Europe, a consort; to Russian rumour, a Romanov heir; to the old man, the obstacle to a god; to television, a show whose last episode is "your downfall."
- **What the household says:** Okazaki: "Your goal is to change the world itself"; Tachibana: "I, Tachibana, shall serve you for as long as I am alive"; Erika: "my hope is that Sakata will become your hometown"; Takamiya: "Your future belongs to you and you alone."
- **What she says to herself:** "whenever I'm defeated, or if I have my back against the wall, I won't take any of you down with me" (Ch. 6); "I would have to leave them someday" (Ch. 8); "This life I'd been born into was just like a festival" (Ch. 8).

The volume's own answer is in two scenes placed side by side. In Ch. 2 she recognises herself in a possessed girl who "really did change for the better"; in Ch. 8 she sings a lament for "Keikain Runa," the girl who "never got that opportunity" to choose her death. She is the soul in a borrowed life, mourning its first owner. Against every name in the list, Takamiya's verdict stands: "None of you are gods, traitors, or whores. You're human beings."
